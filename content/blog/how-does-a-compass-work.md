A compass is one of the oldest tools in the world. Sailors used them a thousand years before anyone had maps of the open ocean. But how does a little floating needle manage to always point north?

The secret is that Earth itself is a giant magnet — and the compass needle is a tiny one that cannot resist.

## Earth is a giant magnet

Deep inside our planet, there is a churning core of hot, liquid metal. That moving metal acts like a huge magnet, wrapping the whole Earth in an invisible magnetic field. You cannot see it, but it is everywhere around you.

## The needle is a tiny magnet too

Inside a compass, the needle is a small bar magnet, balanced so it can spin freely. And here is the rule: magnets that are free to turn will line themselves up with other magnets. So the needle swings until it lines up with Earth's giant magnetic field — and one end of that line points north.

The end painted red points to the north magnetic pole, and the other end points south.

## North on the compass, north on the map

There is a tiny cheat coders and sailors have to know: the compass points to the **magnetic** north pole, but maps are drawn with **true** north. The two are not exactly the same place, and the difference is different for every city. Map-makers mark this gap, called **declination**, so explorers can correct.

The magnetic pole even moves a little every year. So a compass tells you roughly where north is — and over a thousand years of exploration, "roughly" was plenty.

## Why magnets get in line

Try it yourself. Rub a sewing needle on a fridge magnet a few dozen times — one direction only — and the needle becomes magnetic. Float it on a cork in a bowl of water and it will slowly turn to point north-south. Magnets always want to line up with other magnets, and Earth is the biggest magnet around.

So the compass is not reading a map. It is simply the oldest magnet trick in the book: put a tiny magnet in a world that already has one, and let them agree.

> Try this at home: make a needle a magnet by rubbing it on a fridge magnet, float it on a cork or leaf in still water, and watch it find north by itself.
