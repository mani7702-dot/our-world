## A dot that moved at the speed of a car

Most nights, when Charles Kowal lifted his eye to the 1.2-meter telescope at Palomar Observatory on October 18, 1977, he was hunting something specific: slow-moving space rocks that crawl between the orbits of Saturn and Uranus.

On photographic plates taken a few hours apart, he used a **blink comparator** — a device that flips back and forth between two images — looking for any dot that shifted position. And there it was: an 18th-magnitude speck that drifted only **3 arcminutes per day**. That is so slow that if you held a penny at arm's length, it would take the object three days just to move behind the penny's width.

At first, he called it an asteroid and gave it the temporary label **1977 UB**. Nobody suspected they had just found the first member of an entirely new family.

## 10 things that make Chiron the galaxy's strangest visitor

1. **Wrong neighborhood.** Chiron orbits the Sun between Saturn and Uranus — a cosmic no-man's land where few objects were expected to live stably. Its orbit is stretched like a rugby ball, swinging from inside Saturn's path to just beyond Uranus.

2. **Half-rock, half-comet.** After its discovery, Chiron behaved like a comet. In 1989, telescopes spotted a faint dusty coma and tail around it. It is now officially both a **minor planet and a comet** (designation 95P/Chiron).

3. **It has rings.** In the 2010s, astronomers confirmed Chiron is one of only four minor planets known to host a **ring system** — a narrow band of dark material orbiting just above its equator.

4. **Born in the Kuiper Belt.** Like most Centaurs, Chiron likely began life in the frozen outer reaches beyond Neptune, then was gravitationically shoved inward by the giant planets into its current unstable orbit.

5. **A time traveler.** Precursor images from **1895** show Chiron was already on its 1977 path — meaning it had been invisible to astronomers for decades, hiding in the dark far from the Sun's glare.

6. **Too big to miss.** With a diameter of roughly 250 kilometers (155 miles), Chiron is the largest known Centaur — a true heavyweight of the in-between zone.

7. **Worth ten planets.** When the press found out about the discovery, some outlets suggested Chiron could be the **tenth planet** — before Pluto's 1930 discovery settled that debate.

8. **A cosmic bridge.** Centaurs are the missing evolutionary link between small planetesimals and planets. By studying them, scientists learn how the outer Solar System assembled itself.

9. **Dancing with Saturn.** Roughly every 760 years, Saturn's gravity gives Chiron a gravitational tug that reshapes its orbit — a slow celestial waltz that may eventually eject it from the Solar System entirely.

10. **Named for a wise teacher.** Kowal named his discovery after the mythological centaur Chiron — son of Saturn, grandfather of the planets, and the wise tutor of heroes like Achilles and Jason.

## Try this at home

On a clear autumn night, find **Jupiter** (bright and steady) and **Saturn** (golden, a bit fainter) in the southern sky. That band of space between them — roughly where Centaurs like Chiron wander — is the same region where Kowal found his surprise visitor. With a small telescope, scan slowly back and forth; you are hunting for the same kind of slow, lonely drift that led to a whole new class of Solar System citizen.

**Fun fact:** Chiron is too faint for backyard binoculars, but if you could see it, it would look like a steady, starlike point of light that creeps less than a finger-width across the sky each hour.
