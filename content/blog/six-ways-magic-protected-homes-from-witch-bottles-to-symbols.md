You have probably walked past a house with a small glass owl in the window, or seen someone hang a sprig of rosemary by their door. Humans have always wanted to keep their homes safe — and for thousands of years, that meant tucking away secret magical protections.

But these were not silly superstitions. In the past, they were serious business. When a house burned down, when a child caught a fever, or when a storm knocked down a wall, families looked for ways to push the bad luck away. They carved symbols into door frames, buried mysterious bottles under floors, and hid objects inside walls — hoping to keep harm far from the people they loved.

Here are six ways people of the past used magic to protect their homes:

## 1. Witch bottles — the original burglar alarm

In old England and colonial America, a witch bottle was a clay jar filled with sharp objects — pins, needles, nails — and sometimes the occupant's own urine or hair. The bottle was buried under the house or by the front door.

The idea was simple: if someone tried to place a curse on the house, the spell would get "stuck" in the bottle instead. The sharp objects would pinch the evil spirit, and the buried bottle would trap it far from the family. Some were even thrown into fires on purpose — the pop and hiss was thought to break the spell.

## 2. Hexafoils and daisy wheels — the mysterious six-petal mark

Look closely at old doors, window frames, or floorboards in medieval European homes, and you might spot a strange pattern: six overlapping circles forming a flower shape, often with a hexagon in the center.

Called hexafoils or daisy wheels by researchers, these marks were carved by handymen and builders right into the wood and plaster. Some think they were just decorative — but they show up again and again at places that were considered dangerous: above fireplaces, around chimneys, and near front doors. Many historians believe they were meant to confuse or trap evil spirits, making them dizzy in the swirling pattern instead of letting them hurt the family.

Some of the earliest documented examples come from 17th-century England, where builders carved them into the beams of new houses.

## 3. Rowan trees and rosemary bushes — the living guard

Across Britain and northern Europe, people planted specific trees and herbs near their homes on purpose. A rowan tree by the front door was thought to ward off the evil eye — the belief that someone's bad gaze could bring bad luck.

Rosemary was another favorite. According to old herb guides, a rosemary bush by the door would keep witches away — especially the kind that were thought to ride on rooftops at night. The plant's strong smell was believed to be powerful enough to drive them off.

## 4. Concealed shoes — the shoe that never came off

Inside old English and European homes, builders sometimes found shoes hidden in walls, under floorboards, or tucked into chimney spaces. These were not lost footwear — they were carefully placed there on purpose.

Dozens of concealed shoes have been found in historic buildings across Europe, from grand houses to humble cottages. Often a single shoe, sometimes a child's, was tucked away during construction. Historians believe the shoe was meant to trap evil spirits — the idea being that a spirit would get stuck in the shoe's shape and be unable to move through the house.

The practice was so common that museums now collect these finds, and researchers have catalogued hundreds of locations where concealed shoes were discovered.

## 5. Demon tiles — the face that scared away spirits

In Japan, China, and parts of East Asia, people placed small terracotta or ceramic figures — often grotesque demon faces — near doorways and along garden walls.

These "demon tiles" or guardian figures were meant to scare away not just burglars, but evil spirits. The theory was that a spirit trying to enter the home would be frightened away by the fierce, snarling face staring back at it. Some were placed on rooftops to watch over the whole property.

## 6. Star symbols and mason marks — the numbers that kept bad things out

Long before house numbers, builders carved mysterious symbols into door frames, window sills, and stair rails. Five-pointed stars, six-pointed stars, interlocking V-shapes, and geometric patterns were common.

Sometimes these were mason's marks — signatures of the craftsmen who built the house. But their placement was too consistent to be random: stars appeared above doorways, hexag- eals near hearths, and protective patterns on anything that could let in wind or cold. Many historians now believe these marks served a double purpose — proving who built the home, and keeping harm away from it.

## What these protections tell us

What ties all six methods together is something beautiful: the desire to make a place not just shelter, but sanctuary. Whether carved in wood, planted in soil, or buried beneath stone, these protections all said the same thing: this home matters, and we will guard it.

Today, when we hang a wind chime by the door or place a welcome mat at the threshold, we are carrying on a tradition that spans thousands of years — the small, quiet magic of making a house feel safe.

## Try this at home

Ask an adult to help you look at the door frames and windowsills in your house. Do you see any marks carved into the wood? Some older homes have small star symbols or geometric patterns that builders left behind. Try drawing your own protective symbol on a piece of paper — what would your magical safeguard look like?

## Fun Fact

The word "apotropaic" comes from Ancient Greek words meaning "to turn away." People have been turning away bad luck with symbols, plants, and hidden objects for at least 3,000 years — and some of the same ideas are still alive today in good-luck charms, door decorations, and even the tradition of not walking under ladders.
