You are reading this, and there is a decent chance that within the next minute or two you will yawn. Maybe you already have. Sorry about that.

Yawning is one of the most universal things humans do. It happens when we are tired, when we are bored, when we are wide awake, and — the strangest part — when we simply see or hear someone else do it. Here is what is going on.

## Why do we yawn at all?

Honest answer: scientists are still not completely sure, and there is no single confirmed reason. What is clear is that a yawn is a big, deep breath in, a wide stretch of the jaw, and a long breath out. Researchers have tested several ideas over the years.

The oldest idea was that yawning pulls in extra oxygen when you are tired or low on air. That theory has largely not held up — experiments found that breathing more oxygen, or more carbon dioxide, did not really change how much people yawn.

A leading idea today is that yawning helps cool the brain. A deep breath and a stretched jaw may boost blood flow and let heat escape, waking your mind up a little. Other researchers think yawning is mostly about gently stretching and waking up the lungs and the body, especially when you are shifting between sleepy and alert.

The bottom line: a yawn is probably a small reset button for your body. But nobody has proven exactly which button it presses.

## The contagious part is the weird part

Here is where it gets fascinating. There are two kinds of yawning. A **spontaneous yawn** is one you just do on your own. A **contagious yawn** is one you do in response to seeing, hearing, or even reading about someone else's yawn. Just picturing a big yawn is often enough to trigger your own.

Contagious yawning is common in people and in some of our close primate relatives, and scientists think it may be tied to something called **emotional contagion** — a basic building block of empathy, the ability to pick up and share someone else's feelings.

One idea is that our brains contain **mirror neurons**, cells that fire both when we do something and when we watch someone else do it. Watching a yawn may prime our own yawn the way watching someone wince can make you wince too. Your brain quietly rehearses what it sees.

## Who you yawn with matters

The research gets even more interesting. Studies have found that contagious yawning is not random — it depends on the relationship.

In a study published in the journal *Frontiers in Psychology*, researchers found that people yawned most in response to the yawns of those closest to them. Contagious yawning was highest between close friends and family members, and lower between people who were less bonded. The team concluded that social closeness is one of the main drivers of whether a yawn spreads.

Another body of research found that pregnant women — people who are, in a sense, especially tuned to other people's emotions — were more likely to yawn contagiously than others. The researchers took that as support for the idea that contagious yawning is linked to emotional closeness.

## The honest caveat

There is one more twist worth knowing: scientists have not fully settled the connection between contagious yawning and empathy. Some studies find the link, and others search for it and come up short. So while contagious yawning is absolutely real, exactly *why* it happens is still an open question.

What is not in doubt is the effect itself. One person yawns, and the yawn can ripple across a classroom, a bus, or a group of friends. Your brain is wired, at least a little, to copy the people around you.

So if you yawned while reading this, that is not a bug. That is your brain quietly connecting with a stranger — in this case, whoever wrote this sentence.
