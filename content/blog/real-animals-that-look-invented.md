Some animals look like a child's drawing of an animal, made by someone who had never seen one. They are all real. And the best part is that every weird part of them is doing a job.

Here are six of the strangest, plus the trick for telling a real oddball from a photo someone invented.

## 1. The aye-aye taps trees like a woodpecker

The aye-aye lives in the forests of Madagascar and looks like a mix of a squirrel, a bat and a very old man. It has huge ears, ragged teeth and one extremely long, thin middle finger.

It uses that finger as a tool. The aye-aye taps it on a branch, listens to the sound, and works out where a grub is hiding inside the wood. Then it chews a small hole and hooks the grub out with the same finger.

That tapping is a way of seeing with sound, a bit like the echo tricks bats use. It is one of the few animals known to find food this way.

## 2. The saiga antelope has a nose like a bendy hose

The saiga lives on the grasslands of Central Asia. Its nose is long, soft and hangs down over its mouth, and it can wiggle. It looks like a cartoon character that got its face squashed.

Scientists think the nose helps in two ways: it warms up freezing air before it reaches the lungs in winter, and it filters out dust in summer when the herd is on the move and the ground is dry.

Saigas are in real trouble. Herds have crashed in the last few decades, partly from disease and hunting, and the species now depends on a small number of remaining groups.

## 3. The thorny devil drinks through its feet

The thorny devil is a small lizard from the Australian desert, about 20 centimetres long, covered from head to tail in hard spikes. Even its eyelids have a spike.

Rain almost never falls where it lives. So it uses its skin. Dew and damp sand collect in tiny channels between its scales, and the water is carried along the body to the corners of the mouth. The lizard is basically wearing its own plumbing.

It also has a strange lump on the back of its neck that looks like a second head. When a predator comes near, the lizard dips its real head and the attacker bites a mouthful of spikes instead.

## 4. The glass frog has a see-through belly

Glass frogs live in the rainforests of Central and South America. From above they are a nice leafy green, like any other tree frog. Turn one over and you can see its heart beating through the skin.

The trick gets stranger. When a glass frog is resting on a leaf, most of its red blood cells move into its liver, so the body looks even more transparent. The frog becomes a green blur on a green leaf and nearly vanishes. When it wakes up and moves, the blood flows back out and it becomes visible again.

## 5. The red-lipped batfish walks instead of swimming

This fish lives around the Galapagos Islands, in the Pacific Ocean off Ecuador. It is brown and bumpy on top, and it has bright red, shiny lips that look painted on.

It is a terrible swimmer. Instead of gliding through the water, it uses its fins like legs and walks across the sea floor. On its head there is a single spine that works as a lure to bring small fish and crabs close enough to eat.

Nobody is completely sure why the lips are red. One idea is that the colour helps the fish recognise its own species when it is time to lay eggs.

## 6. The blobfish is not really a blob

You have probably seen the blobfish: a sad pink jelly with a droopy nose, famous as the world's ugliest animal. That photo is real, but it is not what the fish looks like.

The famous specimen, nicknamed Mr Blobby, was pulled up from more than a kilometre deep in 2003. Down there, the water pressure is about a hundred times stronger than at the surface, and it is that pressure that holds the blobfish's soft, jelly-like body in shape. Hauled up fast, the fish collapses. What the camera caught was a body squashed by the change in pressure, not a face.

Underwater cameras have filmed living blobfish. They look like ordinary fish: pinkish, tadpole-shaped, with a normal head.

## How to check a weird animal before you share it

Not every impossible-looking animal is real. Some are toys, some are sculptures, and some are photos that have been edited.

Three quick checks:

1. **Is it in a museum, a zoo or a wildlife list?** Real animals end up in official lists of species, with a scientific name.
2. **Where is the photo from?** A picture credited to a museum or a research team is a good sign. A picture with no credit, reposted a thousand times, is worth doubting.
3. **Does the strange part have a job?** Nature is full of odd shapes, but they are usually doing something: keeping the animal cool, hiding it, or helping it eat.

## Try this at home

Pick the animal from this list you find weirdest, and invent a cousin. Give it one impossible feature, then write one sentence explaining what the feature is for.

A nose that sprays water to scare off hawks? A tail that works as a fan? The rule is the same one nature follows: every strange part needs a reason.

**For grown-ups:** Each species here is documented, but children's retellings often smooth off the rough edges. The aye-aye's percussive foraging is well established (tap, listen, gnaw, hook), though how much it relies on acoustic cues versus other senses is still studied. Saiga nose function is described in the literature as hypotheses rather than settled fact, with thermoregulation and dust filtration the two leading explanations. Thorny devil capillary water transport is experimentally documented, and the false head is a genuine anti-predator structure. Glass frog transparency involves blood storage in the liver, described in a 2022 Science paper; the family is also sensitive to habitat fragmentation. The red-lipped batfish's lip colour is genuinely unexplained, with species recognition the leading hypothesis. Blobfish taxonomy is the trap: the famous 2003 NORFANZ specimen (Australian Museum, AMS I.42771-001) is Psychrolutes microporos, while popular articles usually name P. marcidus, and the viral photo shows barotrauma from rapid decompression. Useful classroom case for how one photograph can define a species' reputation for decades.
