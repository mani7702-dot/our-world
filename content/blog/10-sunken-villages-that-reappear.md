You have probably heard the saying "the lake swallowed the town." It sounds like a fairy tale, but it is real science — and it is reversible.

When engineers build a dam across a river valley, they flood a valley to make a reservoir. The water fills in behind the dam, creeping up the slopes, over streets and houses, until the whole place is underwater. To the people who lived there, their world is gone — roofs and all.

But nature does not keep secrets forever. During dry seasons and long droughts, reservoir levels drop. The water pulls back like a curtain, and suddenly the stones come back into view.

## 1. Vilarinho da Furna, Portugal — a village that refused to be forgotten

High in the mountains of northern Portugal, the village of Vilarinho da Furna sat right on the banks of the Homem River. Nearly 300 people called it home, sharing fields and stone houses through a special way of life that had lasted for generations.

When the electricity company bought the land to build a dam, families left in 1971. They took what they could carry — even the roof tiles. The village was flooded in 1972. But during droughts, the stone walls of the old houses rise again from the water like a memory made of rock.

## 2. St. Thomas, Nevada — the last resident paddled away

Out in the American desert, a small town in Nevada grew up beside the Muddy River in the 1800s. The town of St. Thomas had stores, a school, a church, and even its own soda fountain.

When the Hoover Dam was built in the 1930s, Lake Mead began filling behind it. Water crept into the streets. One by one, families left. The last resident, Hugh Lord, reportedly paddled away in 1938 as the water reached his doorstep. For decades the ruins sat 60 feet underwater. By the 2000s, a long drought had pulled the water back, and St. Thomas stood dry again — with its old street grid still visible on the desert floor. The National Park Service now protects the ruins.

## 3. Mediano, Spain — the bell tower that became a buoy

The Church of the Assumption in Mediano, Spain, had a tall bell tower that used to look over the village. When the dam was built, the tower ended up standing alone in the reservoir — a stone lighthouse that floated on the water.

At the bottom of the tower sits the rest of the village. During a bad drought, the water drops low enough for visitors to walk across the cracked lakebed and visit the old church and cemetery. When rains return, the tower is once again an island in the water.

## 4. Sant Romà de Sau, Spain — the church that rose and fell with the drought

In Catalonia, the 11th-century church of Sant Romà de Sau became a symbol of Spain's drought crisis. When the Sau Reservoir filled in the 1960s, the whole village disappeared underwater. Only the church tower peeked above the surface — a familiar landmark that rose and fell with the seasons.

In early 2024, the reservoir fell to nearly 1% of capacity. The entire church and village ruins sat on dry, cracked earth — visitors could walk right up to the old stone walls. Then came heavy rains in March 2025. Within weeks, the water covered everything again. The bell tower rose, sank, rose — a living clock telling the story of too little water and then too much.

## 5. Curon Venosta, Italy — the tower that fooled everyone

An idyllic village in Italy's Alps sat beside a clear mountain lake. When the dam went up in 1950, 900 people had to leave. The village church was destroyed, but its 14th-century bell tower was saved — and left standing in the middle of the new reservoir.

For 70 years the tower sat in the water like a postcard. It suggested that an intact village lay below. But in 2021, the reservoir was deliberately drained for repairs. Stone steps, cellar walls, and street pieces emerged for the first time in decades — showing how much of the village had been hidden under the lake all along.

## 6. Adaminby, Australia — 101 buildings moved to save a town

Before it became a ski resort, Adaminby was a farming village in the Snowy Mountains. When the massive Snowy Mountains Hydroelectric Scheme began, Lake Eucumbene began filling behind the Chevril Dam.

The fight was fierce — residents protested and sued. In the end, workers moved 101 buildings, including two churches. Some houses were carried intact on flatbed trucks. Others were dismantled piece by piece. By 1957, the old village was underwater. During a dry period in 2024, bulldozers, car frames, and old foundations resurfaced on the lakebed — ghostly reminders of the town that used to be.

## 7. Tignes, France — the stones that come back on purpose

Tignes in the French Alps was a farming village tucked in a valley by the Isère River. When France needed power after World War II, the giant Chevril Dam was built. The village fought it, but the dam won. By 1952, the valley was flooded.

Unlike the ghost towns that return by accident, Tignes reappears by design. The dam operators drain the reservoir every few years to inspect the dam's walls. When the water goes, the foundations and old bridge of the original village re-emerge. The modern town sits safely up on the hillside, watching its past return every few years for a brief visit.

## What these towns have in common

Every sunken village has a story, and they all share a few surprising features:

- **It is always about water.** Dams create reservoirs, but droughts, maintenance, and repairs let the water level change. It is the same process everywhere — a slow, predictable cycle that can bring a town back to life, at least for a while.
- **People still care.** When ruins reappear, visitors walk where houses once stood. Local groups and museums have saved tools, clothes, and stories — so even when the stones are underwater, the memories stay safe on dry land.
- **The water always returns.** Whether it is a drought or a repair, sunken towns know the rhythm: disappear for a time, reappear for a season, then go back under. The bell tower at Mediano, the church steeple at Sant Romà — they are the only parts that remember being both islands and mainland.

## Why does the water go down?

Most reservoirs are built to store water for farms and cities. When there is plenty of rain, the water stays high. But when there is a drought, managers release or hold back water for other needs — and suddenly, history is on display.

The same thing happens when engineers need to fix the dam itself. The Chevril Dam in France releases water on purpose so workers can check for cracks. The St. Thomas ruins in Nevada have been visible on and off since the 1930s.

These sunken towns are not cursed places or ghost stories. They are a living reminder that nature and human work are always in conversation — a reservoir that rises and falls, a stone wall that sleeps underwater and wakes up in the sun. The next time you draw a line on paper and watch water creep up it, remember: somewhere, a bell tower is going back to sleep, waiting for the next time the water comes down so the town can visit the land again.
