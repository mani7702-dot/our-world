# How does your body know it is daytime?

## The clock inside your head

You have probably never thought about it, but every morning when you wake up feeling hungry or ready for the day, there is a tiny clock inside your brain that helped make that happen. It is so small you could fit it on the head of a pin, but it has been keeping time for you since before you were born.

This clock does not need batteries or winding. It was built over millions of years of evolution so that your body knows when the Sun is up and when it is down. Scientists call this your **circadian rhythm** — a word that comes from Latin words meaning “around the day.” It is a cycle that repeats every 24 hours.

## Light is the time-giver

The most important job of your body clock is knowing what counts as “daytime” and what counts as “nighttime.” How does it do that? Through light.

When light hits your eye, even if you are not looking at the Sun, special cells in your retina send a message straight to your clock. It is like a secret messenger that runs between your eye and your brain telling it, “It is daytime now!” That message resets your clock each morning, keeping it perfectly in sync with the rising and setting of the Sun.

When the Sun goes down and the room grows dark, your brain does the opposite. It starts to release a hormone called **melatonin** — that is the one that makes you feel sleepy. As melatonin builds up in your blood, your body temperature drops, your heart beats slower, and your whole system prepares for rest. It is why you feel tired at bedtime but alert in the morning.

## What happens when day and night mix up

Imagine trying to sleep while someone keeps turning on the lights. That is what happens to people who work at night — nurses, factory workers, and truck drivers who keep the world running while everyone else sleeps. Their body clock still thinks it is morning at 3 a.m., so they struggle to fall asleep when they get home.

> Try this at home: Close your curtains completely in the evening and turn off all the lights. You will feel sleepier faster than when you watch TV or look at your phone in the dark.

**Fun Fact:** Some animals — like certain fish and frogs — have their own tiny clocks even in their tails and fins. That way, each part of their body knows the time without needing the brain to tell it!
