Some fish are born with their fins permanently set to "spicy." Take the mangrove rivulus: a squat, eel-shaped fish that spends part of its life out of water and, when it meets another of its kind, treats the encounter like a sparring match. It darts, flares, and delivers bursts of aggressive swims that would make a soccer mascot jealous.

Now scientists have found something almost too neat: a spoonful of magic mushroom molecule makes these feisty fighters noticeably less punchy.

## The mushroom molecule that talks to brains

The ingredient in question is **psilocybin** — the same compound that gives magic mushrooms their reputation. In humans and other mammals, psilocybin latches onto **serotonin receptors**, the brain's signal hubs for mood, appetite, and yes, aggression. That is why researchers have studied it for mood and anxiety disorders.

But here is the puzzle the team wanted to test: does a mild dose of psilocybin change how social animals act toward each other? To find out, they borrowed a clever animal — the mangrove rivulus (*Kryptolebias marmoratus*), a fish that is naturally, reliably aggressive when another rivulus shows up.

## How the experiment ran

The researchers used three genetically identical families of lab-bred rivulus. They paired each fish with a stranger from a different family, showed them each other through a mesh screen so everyone could see and smell the rival, then timed how many aggressive "swim bursts" each fish launched.

Then they gave half the fish a tiny, waterborne dose of psilocybin — the equivalent of a low, gentle exposure — and ran the same social test the next day. The numbers dropped. Not all of the fish's behavior changed, just the aggressive ones: the calm-down swam in, the spiky posture folded out, and the rivulus still explored and moved normally.

It is the difference between someone getting sleepy medicine that knocks you out and a calm focus that eases the fight without flattening the fun.

## Why a fish that can breed itself matters

The mangrove rivulus is unusual in the animal kingdom for two big reasons. First, it is amphibious: it can gulp air and survive out of water for days, making it a kind of living experiment in how vertebrates adapted to land. Second, some populations are **self-fertilizing hermaphrodites**, so scientists can raise batches of genetically near-identical fish. That genetic uniformity means a behavior change is more likely caused by the mushroom molecule, not by random inherited differences between subjects.

That makes the rivulus a tidy model for asking a big question — whether mood-affecting chemicals act the same across very different vertebrate brains, or whether they only make sense in mammals with a primate-style cortex. The answer, so far, is: the serotonin path is old enough that even a tiny fish still listens to it.

## What "less aggressive" really means

The published paper notes the effect was specific: activity calmed and aggressive swim-bursts against rivals dropped, but the fish did not become listless or stop interacting. That selectivity is what excites researchers — a treatment that eases reactive aggression might, in principle, avoid the "zombie" side effects that blunt the whole personality.

Of course, a fish is not a person, and a single low dose in one lab test is a long, long way from any treatment. This was a test tube of curiosity: a way to learn how ancient brain circuits turn a calm fish into an angry one, and how an old mushroom molecule can nudge the dial back down.

Fun fact: the mangrove rivulus is also one of the few vertebrates that can heal its heart and spine almost completely as an adult — the same genes that let it bounce back from physical injury may also let it bounce back from chemical nudges to mood.
