# When did Mount Vesuvius really erupt — and how do pomegranates help us find out?

## 1. The date everyone memorized

Close your eyes and picture Pompeii. White plaster buildings, red-tiled roofs, bustling market stalls. Then, in an instant, the sky darkens and the mountain beside them roars to life. Mount Vesuvius erupts, blanketing the Roman city under meters of ash and pumice.

For nearly 2,000 years, historians have agreed on one date: **August 24, AD 79**. That date comes from a stone inscription discovered near the volcano, carved by survivors who wanted to mark the day the world changed.

But what if that inscription is wrong? What if the real eruption happened months later?

## 2. The clues in the kitchen

Dr. Jess Venner, an archaeologist studying the volcanic ash, noticed something strange. In the ruins of a luxury villa at Civita Giuliana (just north of Pompeii), her team found something precious: **pomegranate seeds**.

Now, pomegranates don't ripen in August. In the Italian climate, they reach their prime in **late September or October**. If the villa's kitchen was holding fresh pomegranates when Vesuvius erupted, that means the disaster struck later in the year than August 24.

It was not just the fruit. The team also found **grape seeds** and **bean pods** trapped in the ash — all pointing to a harvest season that had already begun, not one that was still weeks away.

## 3. Coins that tell another story

Coins are like time capsules. At nearby Herculaneum, archaeologists digging through the ash layers found silver coins of Emperor Titus. These coins were minted in September, and they only entered circulation after the emperor arrived in Rome in mid-September.

If those coins were already in people's pockets when the eruption hit, Vesuvius could not have erupted on August 24 — the coins did not exist yet.

## 4. Why the date was wrong

The old date came from a stone column found near the volcano. But that column might have been carved by someone who guessed wrong. The volcanic ash tells a different, more precise story.

Scientists now think Vesuvius erupted in **October or November** of AD 79 — months later than the traditional date. The evidence from plants, coins, and careful stratigraphy all point to a **late autumn disaster**, not a late summer one.

It is a reminder that even the dates we think we know best can shift — one pomegranate seed at a time.

## Try this at home

**Make your own "volcano detective kit"!** Fill a clear jar with sand. Add a few dried beans, a small toy coin, and a few drops of red food coloring. Now pour in water slowly — watch how the "ash" settles in layers around your buried objects. Ask: what season would you guess this "eruption" happened? If the beans look plump and ready to harvest, it's autumn. This shows how archaeologists use plant remains and coins to date ancient disasters.

## Fun Fact

Pompeii was not the only city buried by Vesuvius in AD 79. The Roman towns of **Herculaneum** (buried under hotter, faster pyroclastic flows), **Stabiae**, and **Puteoli** were also destroyed. Herculaneum's victims were preserved in a way that is even more remarkable than Pompeii's — their wooden furniture, food, and even scrolls of ancient literature were carbonized but left in perfect detail by the super-heated volcanic material.
