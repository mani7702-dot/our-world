# How did one ship bring home 6,251 heroes after World War II?

## 1. A harbor honors its fallen

Picture this: it's a warm Sunday morning in October 1947. The S.S. Joseph V. Connolly
steams quietly into New York Harbor, flying its American flag at half-mast. Garlands of
laurel leaves drape across the bridge. Navy destroyers swing alongside, their crews
throwing flowers into the water. Fighter planes roar overhead in a silent salute.
Artillery booms 21-gun salutes from three forts along the shore.

The Connolly wasn't carrying cargo or passengers. It carried something far more
solemn—**6,251 caskets** of American service members who had given their lives in the
European theater. Each coffin held a son, a brother, a father—someone's hero. The
ship's arrival was the climax of one of history's greatest acts of remembrance:
bringing an entire war's fallen home.

## 2. From war machine to mercy ship

Before the war, the Joseph V. Connolly was just one of 2,742 Liberty ships—mass-produced
cargo vessels built quickly to move supplies across the oceans. These ships were
441 feet long and could haul 10,800 tons of whatever the Army needed: tanks, jeeps,
ammunition, even locomotives. President Franklin D. Roosevelt once called them "a real
ugly duckling"—built for function, not beauty.

But after World War II ended in 1945, the Connolly took on a new, sacred mission. The
Army's Graves Registration Service converted its cargo holds into something far more
delicate. The ship became part of a fleet dedicated to one task: returning the remains
of the fallen to their families across America. On that October morning in 1947, the
Connolly carried the coffins of privates, sergeants, and even three-star generals—28
days and 6,251 stories of courage, all bound for home.

## 3. Congress said "bring them home"

The effort began not on a battlefield, but in Washington, D.C. On May 16, 1946, Congress
passed a law setting aside **$200 million**—a staggering sum at the time—for the
"evacuation and return of the remains" of Americans who had died in the war. Families
received a pamphlet titled "Tell Me About My Boy" asking one question: "Do you want
your boy brought home?" More than **60% of families** said yes.

The job fell to the Graves Registration Service. After the war, 13,000 workers fanned
out across Europe and North Africa, searching through **454 temporary military
cemeteries** in **86 countries**. They located the remains of 280,000 service members
whose families wanted them home. The Connolly's caskets came from Belgium, France,
and Germany—from fields and towns where young Americans had fought and fallen. Some
rested in the Henri-Chapelle American Cemetery in Belgium; others had been moved
through Newfoundland's Naval Station Argentia. One casket held four young men who
died together in a plane crash in Newfoundland.

## 4. One hero, honored by all

As the Connolly moved through the harbor, the city paused. Officials planned a special
procession up Fifth Avenue for a single, unidentified casket—one American service
member chosen to represent all 6,251 aboard. This honored tradition stretched back to
1921, when the tomb of the Unknown Soldier opened at Arlington.

The chosen hero was a **Medal of Honor** winner—one of America's highest military
honors. But the Army never revealed his name. The press was told only that he was
a Congial Medal of Honor man, and that was enough. His coffin, led by Navy cadets
and a military band, rode through the streets as New Yorkers lined the sidewalks to
pay their respects. In a moment, the whole city saluted the price of freedom.

The Connolly was not alone in this solemn mission. Just 16 days earlier, on October 10,
the ship Honda Knot had docked in San Francisco with 3,027 Pacific war dead. But
the Connolly's 6,251 was the largest single homecoming of the entire repatriation
program—a final roll call of heroes, welcomed home at last.

## Try this at home

**Build a ship of honor.** Gather cardboard boxes, toilet paper rolls, and construction
paper. Make a simple boat shape from a large box, then decorate the sides with stars
and stripes. Cut small cardboard coffins (to represent respect, not sadness) and place
them inside. Add tiny paper flags at half-mast. As you build, talk about how the real
Connolly was decorated with flowers and wreaths to honor those aboard. Display your
model with a note: "In memory of all who served."

## Fun Fact

The American Battle Monuments Commission—created after World War I—still maintains
**15 American military cemeteries overseas** today. These peaceful burial grounds in
Europe and North Africa hold the graves of more than 125,000 American service
members. Each headstone is carefully tended by local "adopters" who care for the
memorials, keeping the memory of the fallen alive for future generations. The tradition
of honoring the dead began with ships like the Connolly—and continues to this day.
