# What Is the 'Energy Ladder' Physicists Waited 40 Years to See?

Imagine you could make a magnet lose its magnetism and a pot of water boil — and discover that, deep down, they follow the *same* mathematical recipe. That strange idea is called **universality**, and it sits at the heart of a discovery physicists have been chasing for four decades.

## The rungs of a hidden ladder

When a material changes from one state to another — ice to water, magnet to non-magnet — the messy, tiny details often stop mattering. Only a few essential features survive, and very different systems can start behaving identically. Physicists describe this shared pattern with a set of equations called **conformal field theory**.

Those equations made a bold prediction: at certain "quantum tipping points," the allowed energy states of a system should line up like the **rungs of a ladder**, with precise spacing ratios. For about 40 years, that ladder existed only on paper. Nobody could actually *measure* it — until now.

## Trapping atoms in a line

To test the prediction, a team at **Caltech**, working with theorists in France and Germany, built a special kind of quantum simulator. Instead of a general-purpose quantum computer, they used *optical tweezers* — tightly focused beams of laser light — to hold a row of **strontium atoms** perfectly in place.

Other lasers pushed the atoms into high-energy states, making each atom strongly affect its neighbours. Suddenly the whole chain stopped acting like a crowd of separate particles and started behaving as **one single quantum object**. By carefully tuning the lasers, the scientists nudged the chain right to the tipping point.

## Seeing the ladder at last

The team then measured the energy levels of the chain and compared them with the 40-year-old predictions. The match was striking: the spacings followed the exact ratios expected from two different conformal field theories — the **Ising** and **tricritical Ising** models. Two historically separate ideas, both confirmed in a single experiment.

"We are at the point where we can use them to do fundamental physics research," said Xiangkai Sun, a co-lead author of the study, describing how a decade of learning to control these systems has finally paid off.

## Why it matters

Testing theory like this builds confidence in the physics that underlies future quantum technologies — sensors, simulations, and maybe computers. It also shows that humble **laser-trapped atoms** can serve as a window onto some of the deepest rules of the universe.

## Try this at home

Grown-up helper needed! Set two glasses of water side by side — one warm, one cold — and add a drop of food colouring to each. Watch how much faster it spreads through the warm water. Temperature changes how energy moves around, just like the lasers change the energy "rungs" in the atom ladder. Ask yourself: which glass is at a "tipping point" for spreading?

**Fun fact:** The atoms in this experiment are chilled to temperatures colder than deep space — closer to **absolute zero** (−273.15 °C) than anywhere in nature — so that their quantum behaviour can shine through.
