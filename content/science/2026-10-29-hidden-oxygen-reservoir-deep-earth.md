# Where does Earth's oxygen come from? Scientists find a hidden reservoir deep underground

When you breathe out on a cold morning and see your breath, you are sharing something
ancient: the oxygen in the air around you did not always exist in our skies. For billions of
years Earth's atmosphere had almost no free oxygen at all. Then, slowly, something began
filling the world with the very air we need today.

Scientists used to think that oxygen came almost entirely from tiny sea creatures called
**cyanobacteria** — microscopic plants that bubble out oxygen as they photosynthesize. And
those do play the biggest role. But now a new discovery shows that **some of our planet's
oxygen may have been made — and stored — far below our feet**, inside rivers of molten rock.

## 1. The surprising secret inside molten rock

Imagine a pot of thick, bubbling soup. Now imagine that soup could quietly soak up
**extra** of one of its ingredients — more salt, more sugar — even though every recipe said
it was already full.

That is what magma (molten rock) appears to do with **oxygen**.

For a long time, scientists believed that the oxygen locked inside magma was fixed: it could
only be as much as the other ingredients (silicon, magnesium, iron) allowed. Oxygen was
treated like a side dish, not something magma could carry on its own.

But a team of researchers testing magma under crushing pressure found something odd. They
heated a fake chunk of **mid-ocean-ridge basalt** — the kind of rock born where tectonic
plates pull apart beneath the oceans — and squeezed it to **7 gigapascals** of pressure.
That is about **69,000 times** the air pressure you feel right now at sea level.

At that depth, they discovered, the molten rock soaked up far more oxygen than it had any
right to — **more than 5 percent of the sample's weight** was excess oxygen that no
textbook predicted.

## 2. A deep-Earth oxygen vault

The big question: was the extra oxygen really *inside* the rock, or just tiny bubbles
trapped in it?

To find out, the scientists used a tool called **Raman spectroscopy** — a way of identifying
substances by watching how they dance with light. The results were clear: there were no
oxygen bubbles. The extra oxygen had genuinely **dissolved into the molten rock itself**.

They also ran **computer simulations** — giant number-crunching models that act like virtual
atoms — and sure enough, the more pressure they added, the more oxygen the magma could
hold. It behaves a lot like water or carbon dioxide: harder to hold at the surface, easier
to swallow deep underground.

So here is the picture: miles down beneath volcanoes and mid-ocean ridges, magma quietly
**bottles up extra oxygen** the way a deep-sea sponge soaks up water.

## 3. Could volcanoes have breathed life into Earth's atmosphere?

That raises a thrilling possibility. As oxygen-heavy magma rises toward the surface, the
pressure drops — and just like a shaken soda losing its fizz, the extra oxygen could
**escape**.

Where would it go? Some might rise into the crust, some into the air. The scientists
noticed something remarkable: between about **2.5 billion and 2.0 billion years ago**, Earth's
oxygen levels surged in what scientists call the **Great Oxidation Event**. That same window
saw the birth of some of Earth's **largest volcanic outpourings** — vast regions of lava the
size of continents, called large igneous provinces.

Coincidence? Maybe not. The team calculated that if ancient magma erupted and released
just **0.27 percent** extra oxygen by weight, it could in theory match **all the oxygen
currently floating in our atmosphere**. That is a bold "could" — not proof that volcanoes
did it, but proof that even a small deep-Earth contribution might have had enormous
consequences.

For the first time, oxygen joins the short list of invisible passengers that magma can carry
between Earth's hidden interior and the world above.

## Try this at home

Grab a clear soda or sparkling water and a still one. Open them both at the same time and
listen. The bubbly one hisses — that gas was *forced* into the liquid under pressure and
pops out the moment the pressure drops. That is a little like what may happen when
oxygen-rich magma rises and the great weight of rock above lightens up. Can you think of
other things that stay hidden under pressure but burst out when the squeeze is released?

## Fun Fact

Earth is the only planet in our solar system with an atmosphere that is **21 percent
oxygen**. Mars and Venus have almost none. If magma really did help ferry oxygen from our
deep interior to the skies, it may be one of the secrets that made our world uniquely
alive.
