# What Is the ‘String of Pearls’ Hiding a Zombie Star in Our Sky?

Have you ever heard of a star that came back from the dead? Not a ghost story — a real, actual star that exploded and then kept shining. Deep in the constellation **Cassiopeia** (the queen on her throne) lives a cosmic treasure that looks like a dandelion made of stardust, and at its heart beats something that shouldn’t exist: a **zombie star**.

## A supernova that history recorded

About **850 years ago**, in the year **1181**, people around the world looked up and saw something strange. Chinese and Japanese astronomers watched a new “guest star” appear in the sky. It burned brightly for months — so bright it was visible during the daytime — then slowly faded away.

That guest star was a **supernova**: a star that exploded at the end of its life. We now know it was the kind called **Type Iax** — a “failed” explosion. In a typical thermonuclear supernova, a white dwarf (the dense core left behind by a dying star) gobbles material from a companion star until it hits a critical mass and detonates, destroying itself completely. But in a Type Iax, the blast only blows off part of the star. The rest survives as a shattered, gravitationally bound remnant.

We call that survivor a **zombie star** — a star that exploded but came back from the dead.

## The cosmic necklace

The wreckage from that 1181 explosion is called **Pa 30**, and it looks like something from another universe. The Gemini North telescope captured the most detailed image yet: a dazzling, lace-like structure of gas that stretches more than a **half-light-year** across. Astronomers nicknamed it the **“string of pearls”** because it looks like a dandelion clock made of glowing bubbles and filaments.

At the very center, hidden among the debris, is the zombie star itself — a faint, hot ember that should have been obliterated by the blast, but somehow remains. This makes Pa 30 the only supernova remnant of its type seen in our entire galaxy, and an invaluable natural laboratory for studying these mysterious failed explosions.

## Why a zombie star matters

Zombie stars are rare — astronomers estimate they happen only **5 to 30 percent** as often as the more common supernovae that fully destroy their star. But they teach us a lot about how stars live, explode, and die. By studying Pa 30, scientists are learning that Type Iax events may be more common than they thought, and that many white dwarfs that seemed to survive an explosion might actually be zombie stars hiding in plain sight.

## Try this at home

On a clear, dark night, look toward the “W” or “M” shape in the northern sky — that’s Cassiopeia. Point a pair of binoculars at the middle of that “W” and you’ll sweep through a patch of sky holding an 850-year-old mystery. With luck (and excellent equipment), you might catch a glimpse of the faint smudge that marks where a star exploded and rose again.

## Fun Fact

The “guest star” of 1181 is one of only **five supernovae** humans have recorded before telescopes existed. The others were in 1006, 1054 (which made the Crab Nebula), 1572, and 1604. And unlike most historical supernovae, the “string of pearls” can still be studied today because Pa 30 is relatively close — just **7,500 light-years** away.
