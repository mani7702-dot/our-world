# Why does Madagascar have a giant patch of Moon rock on Earth?

An astronaut aboard the International Space Station spotted something that looks almost
too perfect: a bright, silvery-white patch in southern Madagascar that stands out against
the rust-colored landscape below — a piece of Earth that looks like it was lifted right
from the Moon's surface.

The secret? The rock is **anorthosite** — the exact same type of stone that makes up the
Moon's bright, reflective highlands. Those pale "seas" you can see through a backyard
telescope when you gaze at the Moon on a clear night? That is anorthosite, too.

## 1. A rock from space, hiding in plain sight

Anorthosite is an **igneous rock** — meaning it forms when molten material deep underground
 slowly cools and hardens. Because it cools so far below the surface, the minerals inside
have time to grow into large, glittering crystals. The result is a rock that is mostly
plagioclase feldspar, a mineral that is unusually light in color and reflects sunlight
beautifully.

On the Moon, anorthosite covers the highlands and gives them that brilliant white shine.
Here on Earth, large deposits exist in only a few places — eastern Canada, Scandinavia,
southern India, and Madagascar. The Saririaky massif in southern Madagascar is one of
 the most striking: about 100 square kilometers of pale rock that is easily visible from
 space.

## 2. 600 million years of hidden history

The Saririaky formation began its journey more than **600 million years ago** during the
late Precambrian — long before dinosaurs, ferns, or even the first shelled creatures
crawled into the oceans. Back then, molten anorthosite intruded into Earth's crust and
slowly cooled beneath the surface. Over millions of years, erosion wore away the layers
above it, revealing the bright rock we see today.

But the landscape around it tells an even older story. The neighboring rocks preserve
scars of enormous forces — ancient continental collisions that squeezed, folded, and
twisted the crust. Those deformations created the rusty, reddish-brown terrain that
makes the pale anorthosite patch pop so vividly from orbit.

## 3. A cosmic connection

Scientists think the Moon's anorthosite highlands formed in a similar way — but in a
more dramatic fashion. When the early solar system was still a messy place of colliding
planetary bodies, the Moon is thought to have had a global "magma ocean." As that ocean
of molten rock slowly cooled, the light plagioclase crystals floated to the top,
forming a crust of anorthosite that still blankets much of the Moon today.

So while Madagascar's patch of Moon-rock is a quiet geological relic, it is connected
to one of the Solar System's oldest chapters: the same rock type that coats our closest
neighbor.

## Try this at home

Build a "rock layer cake" with modeling clay or play-doh. Stack layers of different
colors, then press down slowly. The top layer represents the light-colored minerals
(feldspar) that would float to the surface just like in anorthosite formation. You can
also download a free satellite-imagery app like SkyView to spot the Moon's bright
highlands on a clear night — those pale patches are the same type of rock staring back
at you from a very different world.

**Fun fact:** The word "anorthosite" comes from the Greek words for "without" and
"star" — an early geologist thought the rock looked nothing like the stars it came from,
but modern astronauts know the feeling: they look down at Earth and see a patch that
looks just like home... the Moon.

**for-grownups:** ScienceDaily reports (Oct 11, 2026) that a NASA ISS astronaut photograph
captured the Saririaky anorthosite massif in southern Madagascar — a roughly 100 km²
exposure of anorthosite that is at least 600 million years old and shares its composition
with the Moon's bright highlands. Wikipedia's "Anorthosite" article confirms that
anorthosite is a plagioclase-rich intrusive igneous rock whose lunar counterpart
constitutes the Moon's light-colored highlands; the same rock also occurs in large
terrestrial deposits in Canada, Scandinavia, India, and Madagascar. NASA Earth Observatory
explains that anorthosite forms when molten material cools slowly underground, allowing
large plagioclase crystals to develop, and that the surrounding Madagascar landscape
preserves evidence of ancient continental collisions.
