# Do Spiders Dream? Scientists Find Surprising Clues in a Tiny Hunter's Sleep

## What happens when a coin-sized spider goes to sleep?

Spiders may not curl up and snore like we do, but they do something that looks an awful lot like sleep. And now, scientists have found something incredible: the **tiny jumping spider Portia fimbriata** goes through two different sleep stages — one of which looks very similar to the REM sleep that humans experience when we dream.

REM stands for "rapid eye movement." When people are in REM sleep, our eyes dart back and forth, our breathing gets faster and irregular, and our brains light up with activity. That is the phase when most dreaming happens. Scientists at the **University of Bonn** watched hundreds of Portia spiders over many days and nights, and they noticed something remarkable during the spiders' quiet rest time: the little arachnids' **eyes twitched and their leg muscles twitched too** — just like REM sleep in humans and other animals with backbones.

## The clever spider that inspired the discovery

The star of the story is **Portia fimbriata**, a jumping spider about the size of a one-euro coin. But don't let its tiny size fool you — it is one of the smartest spiders on the planet. While most spiders sit in webs and wait for dinner to fly in, Portia actively **hunts other spiders**. It sneaks up on its prey, sometimes even **imitating the vibrations of a struggling insect** to lure another spider into thinking dinner has arrived. When the victim comes closer, Portia pounces! These clever tricks suggest the spider is capable of learning and problem-solving — traits we usually associate with much larger, brainier animals.

## What do spiders do during their REM-like sleep?

To study the spiders' sleep, the Bonn team placed each Portia in a tiny plastic dish — about 12 centimeters wide — and watched with **infrared cameras** so they would not disturb the nocturnal hunters. They monitored the spiders under different lighting to track their day-night cycles.

The researchers found that Portia spiders go through **two clearly distinguishable sleep stages**:

1. **A quiet rest stage** — body motionless, antennae still, simply resting.
2. **A REM-like stage** — eyes dart rapidly, body muscles twitch, legs sometimes jerk.

Just like in human sleep studies, the scientists tested whether the spiders were *truly* asleep or just sitting still. They played **gentle sound tones** and measured how fast the spiders woke up. During deep rest, the spiders were slow to respond. Then they tried **sleep deprivation** — keeping the spiders awake during their usual rest time — and watched what happened afterward. Like tired humans, the spiders showed **sleep rebound**: they slept extra hard the next time they settled down. These tests confirmed that the spiders were experiencing genuine, regulated sleep — not just inactivity.

## Does this mean spiders dream?

Not quite — at least, we cannot say for sure. When humans are in REM sleep, our brains are very active, and that is when most of our vivid dreams happen. The researchers **could not measure the spiders' brain activity** directly (a spider's nervous system is tiny and very different from ours). But the fact that the spiders show eye movements and muscle twitches during their REM-like stage suggests that **similar features of sleep evolved independently** — showing up in both vertebrates (animals with backbones, like us) and arachnids (spiders and their relatives).

This discovery raises a wonderful question: if sleep shows up in such very different creatures, did it evolve once in a common ancestor, or many times over? Scientists think studying sleep in animals with simpler nervous systems might help us understand **why sleep exists at all** and what it does for the brain.

## Try this at home

Watch your houseplants or a spider's web in the early morning. Look closely — do you see any tiny webs glistening with dew? Spiders rebuild their webs each morning, which takes energy and time. Scientists think sleep helps brains (and spider-nerve systems) **repair, sort memories, and recharge** — whether you have a brain the size of a human's or smaller than a grain of rice. Try gently tapping a pencil a few times, then staying perfectly still and quiet for one minute — notice how you become more aware of small sounds? Spiders may experience a version of that heightened awareness after a good night's rest.

## Fun Fact

Jumping spiders are some of the best visually gifted invertebrates on Earth. While most insects and spiders have relatively poor vision, **jumping spiders have four pairs of eyes**, with the front pair acting almost like tiny binoculars. They can see colors, judge distances, and even recognize human faces. Some species can jump **50 times their own body length** — that is like a person leaping over a football field in a single bound!
