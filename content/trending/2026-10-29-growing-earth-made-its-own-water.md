# How Did Earth Make Its Own Water? The Growing Planet's Rain of Fire

## The big question

Close your eyes and picture Earth's very first sunrise. There was no blue sky, no fluffy clouds, and certainly no ocean sparkling in the morning light. Instead, our young planet was a glowing ball of molten rock, surrounded by an atmosphere thick with hydrogen and helium — gases that today we would never breathe.

Now here is the puzzle that has baffled scientists for decades: if Earth started out bone-dry, where did all our oceans come from? For years the answer seemed simple — mighty comets and asteroids slammed into Earth, each one a dirty snowball carrying water to the newly formed surface. But new research says that story might be only half right.

## Pebble rain in a fiery sky

The new idea comes from experiments led by Susmita Garai and Professor Zachary Sharp at the University of New Mexico, published this month in *Nature Geoscience*.

Here is how it works. When Earth was just a tiny seed of rock, it grew bigger by grabbing millions of small, pebble-sized pieces of rock drifting through space. As the planet's gravity grew stronger, these pebbles began to fall — not gently, but at hurricane speeds — through an atmosphere hotter than an oven, where temperatures soared past 1,600 degrees Celsius.

But here is the clever part. That super-hot air was full of hydrogen, the lightest element in the universe. When each pebble tumbled through the hydrogen, the hydrogen grabbed oxygen atoms right out of the pebble's minerals, freeing that oxygen to float up into the sky. Up there, it bumped into hydrogen atoms and — voila — turned into water vapor. Do that for millions of years, with pebbles falling like cosmic rain, and you end up with an ocean's worth of steam.

"It is hard to imagine that the water we splash in today may have been born 4½ billion years ago in a hell-like atmosphere," says Sharp. "But it very well may have."

## A test in the laboratory

To see if this could really happen, the team heated tiny pieces of ordinary rock to the same impossible temperatures found in Earth's early sky — between 1,200 and 1,400 degrees — then blasted them with pure hydrogen gas. In less than an hour, nearly 80 percent of the iron in the rock had given up its oxygen, and clouds of water vapor formed.

The numbers are staggering. The researchers calculated that pebble rain like this could have produced **hundreds of oceans** worth of water — far more than Earth actually holds today. That extra gas probably escaped into space or soaked into the molten surface, but the point is clear: Earth was never as dry as we thought.

## Will other worlds be wet too?

If water comes this easily, then every rocky world that grows big enough might end up with oceans. That means exoplanets circling distant stars — planets just like Earth — could be wetter than we ever guessed, and maybe, just maybe, alive with the kind of droplets that spark rainbows.

The pebble-rain idea does not rule out that *some* water arrived on icy comets. But it turns out Earth may have been born with a much thirstier recipe than anyone imagined.

## Try this at home

Fill a clear glass halfway with warm water. Drop in one tiny splash of milk so the water turns slightly cloudy — that is your "hot hydrogen air." Now add a single drop of blue food coloring near the bottom. Watch how the coloring swirls upward, mixes, and spreads through the milk, as if it is making new "water" appear in the cloudiness. That slow, spreading swirl shows how tiny ingredients can create something much bigger when they meet the right conditions. (Do not drink it — it is just for watching!)

## Fun Fact

A single cup of Earth's oceans contains roughly the same amount of water as a bathtub — but you would need over **1,500** of those bathtubs to hold all the water Earth currently has. And according to the new pebble-rain experiments, a few hundred more bathtubs may have been brewed up from scratch in Earth's own fiery birth cloud, billions of years before any ocean ever existed.

## for-grownups:

The study ("Dry pebble rain could have delivered water to Earth through hydrogen reduction of iron oxide," *Nature Geoscience*, 12 Oct 2026, Garai et al.) uses high-temperature experiments in H2 and He gas streams to show that FeO in mafic/ultramafic silicate pebbles is reduced to metallic iron at 1,200–1,400 °C, releasing oxygen that combines with atmospheric H2 to form H2O in minutes. Extrapolated peak efficiency yields "hundreds of ocean masses" of water from pebble accretion during the Hadean (4.567–4.031 Ga). This mechanism sidesteps the isotopic mismatch problem: lunar and cometary water have incompatible D/H ratios, whereas Earth's oceans are consistent with in-situ production. Wikipedia's Hadean article confirms Earth possessed a primordial H2-rich "solar nebula"-like atmosphere and that cometary delivery faces isotopic-fraction incompatibility — corroborating an endogenous source. The pebble-accretion paradigm (Ormel 2017; Johansen 2021) provides the astrophysical framework. Whether pebble rain dominates Earth's water budget versus mantle outgassing remains an open question; the paper notes net water gain depends on atmospheric escape versus ingassing into the magma ocean.
