# 1. Did a 170-Year-Old Star Mystery Just Reveal Hidden Planets Near Earth?

Imagine a puzzle so old that astronomers started working on it **before the invention of the light bulb**. That's the story of a nearby star system called **70 Ophiuchi** — and this week, scientists just solved its biggest riddle.

## 2. A Star System That Puzzled Astronomers Since 1855

Back in 1855, a British astronomer named William Jacob studied the orbit of 70 Ophiuchi — two stars locked in a slow, 88-year dance around each other about 16 light-years from Earth. Something didn't add up. The orbit seemed to wobble, as if a hidden planet were tugging on the stars.

That made 70 Ophiuchi the **first star system in history** ever proposed to host a planet beyond our Sun. His idea was later dismissed, but the wobble kept showing up in telescope data for the next 171 years. Astronomers kept asking: is there a giant planet hiding there, or not?

## 3. What Scientists Just Ruled Out

A team led by the University of Michigan finally cracked it. Using incredibly precise measurements — and combining nearly a century of old observations with fresh ones from giant telescopes in Chile and Texas — they searched for planets as big as Jupiter or larger.

Here's the surprising answer: **there is no Jupiter-sized planet there.** Around the bigger star, any planet that massive would have to sit within 5 times the Earth–Sun distance. Around its smaller companion, they ruled out giant planets even closer in.

## 4. Why "Nothing Found" Is Actually Good News

You might think finding nothing is a disappointment — but this is where it gets exciting. When scientists rule out enormous planets, they narrow the search for **smaller** ones. Planets the size of Saturn — or rocky worlds like Earth — could still be hiding in the system's habitable zone, the "just right" region where liquid water could pool on a planet's surface.

"It's a mix of disappointment and excitement," said researcher Yiting Li. The team proved the mysterious wobble comes from changes in the stars themselves — bubbling hotspots on their surfaces that mimic a planet's pull — not from a giant world. Knowing what *isn't* there helps future telescopes know exactly where to look.

## 5. How They Solved a 170-Year-Old Wobble

To crack the mystery, astronomers had to separate a real planet's gravity signal from the natural "noise" of a restless star. They compared the stars' motion toward and away from Earth (called radial velocity) using the 6.5-meter Magellan Clay Telescope in Chile and the 2.7-meter Harlan J. Smith Telescope in Texas, alongside a hundred years of archived data. The pattern pointed to stellar activity — a star that flickers and churns — rather than a giant planet.

## Try this at home

Step outside on a clear night and find a star that looks steady. Watch it for a minute or two. Most stars only *look* still — many are quietly pulsing, flickering, or wobbling. You're seeing the same kind of tiny brightness changes astronomers must filter out to spot a planet.

## Fun Fact

70 Ophiuchi is so close that its light — travelling at 300,000 kilometres per second — still takes **16 years** to reach your eye. The light you see tonight left the star when today's toddlers weren't even born!
