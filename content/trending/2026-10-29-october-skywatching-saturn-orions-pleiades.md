# October's Night Sky: How to Catch Saturn, Halley's Comet Meteors, and the Seven Sisters

## Saturn rises as the evening star

Right now, if you step outside after sunset and look toward the southern sky, you will see
the brightest object hanging low on the horizon — that is **Saturn**, the ringed planet,
and it is putting on one of its best shows of the decade.

On October 4th, Saturn reached a cosmic milestone called **opposition**. From our view on
Earth, Saturn appeared on the exact opposite side of the sky from the Sun. This means the
planet rose the moment the Sun set, climbed high through the night, and set just as
daylight returned. Because Saturn was also closer to Earth than it has been in years, the
planet glowed like a steady, golden star — easy to spot even from city streets.

Saturn is famous for its spectacular rings, but right now amateur astronomers are also
buzzing about something surprising happening in the planet's atmosphere: a ghostly **ten-sided
ring** that surrounds Saturn's south pole. The feature is growing stronger, giving scientists
a rare chance to watch a massive atmospheric structure develop in real time.

## Shooting stars from Halley's Comet

A few weeks after Saturn's opposition, on the night of **October 21st into the 22nd**, sky
watchers around the world can see the **Orionid meteor shower** — and every single streak of
light you see comes from debris left behind by one of the most famous objects in our solar
system: **Halley's Comet**.

Wait — did someone say *Halley's Comet*? You might think you need to wait decades to see it.
Actually, Halley's Comet orbits the Sun every **74.7 years** (its last visit was in 1986),
but as it travels, it leaves behind a long trail of tiny dust particles and rocky bits. Each
year when Earth passes through this trail, those particles slam into our atmosphere at
tremendous speed, burning up as bright streaks of light we call meteors.

This year, a bright **waxing gibbous Moon** will be in the sky for part of the night, which
can wash out some of the fainter meteors. Your best bet is to head out **just before dawn**,
after the Moon has set, find a dark spot far from city lights, and simply look up. With
patience, you can see several meteors per hour streaking across the sky — silent fireworks
from a comet that last visited when your grandparents were young.

## The Moon and the Seven Sisters

On the night of **October 27th into the 28th**, something magical happens for anyone who
looks up: the Moon will pass very close to a famous group of stars called the **Pleiades**
(or the **Seven Sisters**).

To the unaided eye, the Pleiades look like a tiny, hazy cluster of six or seven bright
stars — so easy to spot that many cultures around the world have their own stories about
them. Ancient Japanese astronomers called the cluster **Subaru**. Native American traditions
often saw the Pleiades as seven sisters dancing across the sky.

The cool thing is that the Pleiades star cluster is actually made up of **hundreds** of
stars, but most of us can only see the brightest few without a telescope. If you have
binoculars or a small telescope, you will see dozens of twinkling stars emerging from the
fog — like spotting hidden gems in a cosmic treasure chest.

## Try This at Home

You do not need a fancy telescope to enjoy October's sky show — just your eyes and a
little patience:

1. **Find Saturn:** Look south after sunset. The brightest "star" that does not twinkle
   (it shines steadily like a lamp) is Saturn. Watch over several nights — you will notice
   it seems to drift compared to background stars.
2. **Catch meteors:** Lie on a blanket outside after midnight on October 21st or 22nd. Let
   your eyes adjust to the dark for 15 minutes, then scan the whole sky. You might see
   several shooting stars per hour.
3. **Find the Seven Sisters:** On the evening of October 27th, step outside around 9 p.m.
   Look northeast — the Moon and a small cluster of tiny stars will be side by side. Count
   how many stars you can see without any equipment.

## Fun Fact

Halley's Comet is named after the English astronomer **Edmond Halley**, who noticed that
comets recorded in historical records from 1531, 1607, and 1682 all had very similar orbits.
He predicted the comet would return in 1758 — and it did, even though he did not live to see
it (he died in 1742). The comet has been seen at every apparition since, including in 1986,
when your parents (or maybe even you!) may have have glimpsed it as a fuzzy greenish comet
in the night sky.

## for-grownups:

Saturn (opposition) is the sixth planet from the Sun at a distance of approximately 1.2 billion
km from Earth at opposition. Its rings span up to 2.3 million km in diameter but are
extremely thin (about 10 meters on average). The hexagonal jet stream at Saturn's north pole
was first discovered by the Voyager probes and studied extensively by Cassini. The south-pole
atmospheric wave mentioned in recent ScienceDaily reporting is distinct from the polar hexagon
and is being tracked by the Hubble Space Telescope. The Orionid meteor shower is produced by
3200 Thule (designation for Halley's Comet's parent body 1P/Halley), with Earth encountering
the debris trail at a relative speed of approximately 68 km/s. Peak Zenithal Hourly Rate (ZHR)
is typically 25-50 meteors per hour, reduced significantly by lunar interference this year.
The Pleiades (M45) is an open star cluster at approximately 444 light-years distance,
containing over 1,000 stars with ages of about 115 million years. The six brightest stars
are hot blue S-type stars with strong lithium absorption lines, visible to the naked eye.
