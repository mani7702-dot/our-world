# What did Parker Solar Probe discover about the Sun's twisted magnetic braids?

## Why does the Sun's corona burn millions of degrees hotter than its surface?

Imagine the Sun as a giant ball of hot, glowing gas. Its **surface** (called the photosphere) sits at about 5,500 degrees Celsius (10,000°F) — hot enough to melt lead. But high above that surface, in the thin outer atmosphere called the **corona**, temperatures soar to **over a million degrees Celsius** — hundreds of times hotter than the surface below.

This is a cosmic puzzle: how can the atmosphere above be so much hotter than the surface beneath? Scientists think the answer lies in the Sun's magnetic field, and a new discovery by NASA's **Parker Solar Probe** is helping solve the mystery.

## What are these magnetic braids?

The solar wind is a stream of invisible, electrically charged particles — mostly electrons and protons — that flows outward from the Sun's corona. These particles are guided by invisible lines of magnetic force, like beads sliding along stretched rubber bands.

But the Sun's magnetic field is chaotic. It twists, tangles, and folds over itself. Some twists go one way (right-handed, like a standard screw), and others go the opposite way (left-handed, like a reverse-threaded bolt). Scientists call this property **magnetic helicity** — it measures how much the field lines are knotted, linked, and twisted.

## A surprise at the boundary of two worlds

For decades, solar physicists had to use two different sets of math to understand the solar wind:

- **Big picture** — treating the wind like a flowing fluid (magnetohydrodynamics)
- **Tiny detail** — tracking individual particles spinning around magnetic field lines (kinetic physics)

The problem was that these two pictures didn't connect. It was like trying to understand traffic jams by studying both an aerial map of the whole highway *and* the exact position of every single car — without knowing how the two views fit together.

Parker Solar Probe's instruments found something remarkable at the boundary between these two scales: the **magnetic twists grew larger and larger** as they moved away from the Sun. Instead of the tight, tiny braids seen near the Sun, the field lines stretched into vast, ocean-like waves with much longer wavelengths. This "nonlocal evolution" means the twists don't just interact with their immediate neighborhood — they couple with structures spanning multiple scales, from tiny particles up to massive plasma flows.

## What does this mean for us?

This discovery helps explain how energy moves from the Sun's surface up into its super-hot corona and beyond. The magnetic braids act like invisible highways that carry heat and energy across the solar system. When these braids unravel near the edge of the Sun's influence, they can trigger geomagnetic storms that paint the sky with auroras and sometimes disrupt satellites on Earth.

## Try this at home

Blow a stream of air across the surface of some water (a hair dryer on low, held a foot above the sink works great). Sprinkle a few drops of food coloring in the stream — you will see the dye stretching and folding into long, twisting streaks, just like the magnetic field lines in the solar wind.

## Fun Fact

Parker Solar Probe is the **fastest human-made object ever built**. On its closest approach in 2024, it zipped around the Sun at 690,000 km/h (430,000 mph) — so fast that if you could ride along, you could travel from New York to Tokyo in just **30 seconds**! It was also the first NASA spacecraft named after a living person, honoring physicist Eugene Parker, who first predicted the existence of the solar wind in 1957.

## For grown-ups

The study by Masatomi Iizawa et al. (Technical University of Braunschweig) was published in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*. Using high-resolution magnetic field measurements from Parker Solar Probe's FIELDS instrument suite, the team analyzed **normalized magnetic helicity density spectra** and found a distinct pair of counter-rotating helical peaks emerging at the magnetohydrodynamic–kinetic interface. Critically, the observed power spectrum was biased toward **lower frequencies** compared to local plasma conditions, indicating **nonlocal evolution** — the helical structures couple across scales from ion-kinetic ripples to large-scale magnetohydrodynamic motions. This implies a "helicity barrier" where energy flow through the plasma cascade is partially blocked at the ion boundary, forcing magnetic twists to accumulate and form the "switchbacks" observed in the solar wind. Wikipedia confirms Parker Solar Probe is a NASA mission (launched August 12, 2018) using Venus gravity assists to reach a record-close 6.16 million km from the solar surface, studying the Sun's corona and solar wind; and that the solar wind is a stream of charged particles (electrons, protons, alpha particles) carrying the interplanetary magnetic field, with the Alfvén surface marking the boundary between the corona and the solar wind.
