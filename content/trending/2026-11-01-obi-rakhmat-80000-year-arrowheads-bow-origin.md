# Were These Tiny Stones the First Arrowheads Ever Shot?

Imagine finding a stone so small you almost sweep it away with the dust — then learning it may have changed how our ancestors hunted forever. Deep inside a rocky shelter in Uzbekistan, scientists have picked out tiny, sharp stone points that are about 80,000 years old. They think these little tools are the earliest arrowheads ever found in this part of the world.

## A shelter with a very long story

The site is called Obi-Rakhmat, a rock shelter tucked into the mountains of northeastern Uzbekistan at about 1,250 metres above sea level. People used it on and off for tens of thousands of years, leaving behind a thick layer of tools and debris. From these layers, researchers carefully sorted out more than a hundred miniature points, many no wider than your fingernail.

The team, led by Hugues Plisson of the University of Bordeaux, published their findings in the journal *PLOS One*. Instead of guessing from shape alone, they studied the **impact marks** — tiny scratches and fractures on the edges of each point.

## How do you prove a stone was an arrowhead?

Here is the trick. When a point is hurled as part of a spear, the force is huge, so the stone must be thick and sturdy. But when a point is shot from a bow, it flies fast and light, so it only needs to be **sharp**. Its small cutting edge has to match the slim shaft it once sat on.

The Obi-Rakhmat points are just 1.5 to 2.4 centimetres wide and weigh only a gram or two. That is far too dainty to survive being thrown by hand — but perfect for a light shaft. The team built modern copies and fired them at targets, then matched the breakage patterns to the ancient stones. It was a match.

## Why this matters

> If these really are arrowheads, then bows may have existed in Central Asia **25,000 years before** the famous French site where similar tiny points were found.

That flips an old idea on its head. For a long time, scientists thought modern humans marched straight from Africa into Europe around 45,000 years ago. The new picture suggests some groups settled in Central Asia much earlier, then spread outwards — carrying clever hunting tools with them.

The same kind of tiny points turn up at a French shelter called Mandrin, 6,000 kilometres away and 25,000 years later. The stones are so alike that, as the researchers put it, you could swap one for the other without anyone noticing — except for the stone itself.

## Fun Fact

Stone Age toolmakers did not just chip anything handy. At Obi-Rakhmat there were plenty of big, strong blades available, yet the hunters deliberately chose to make **tiny** points for their light weapons. Choosing the right size for the job is a kind of engineering — 80,000 years before the first engineer.

## Try this at home

Make your own "impact test." Take a soft pencil and press it straight down onto paper, then throw it (gently!) point-first at a cardboard box. Look at how the tip crushes differently depending on how much force it takes. Archaeologists read those same clues on ancient stones to know whether a weapon was throwable or shootable.
