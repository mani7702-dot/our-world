# How does Shanghai's brand-new Grand Opera House lift a staircase right off the riverbank?

## What is this impossible staircase?

Picture a giant spiral staircase — so grand and curvy it looks like it should float in mid-air. Now imagine that staircase is **part of a real building**, wrapping around the outside of a magnificent new opera house on the banks of Shanghai's Huangpu River. That is exactly what visitors find at the **Shanghai Grand Opera House**, which opened its doors last weekend.

Built by the Norwegian architecture firm **Snøhetta**, the 1.6-million-square-foot (147,000-square-meter) building doesn't just look beautiful — it invites everyone to climb aboard its sweeping exterior staircase and take a walk, even if they don't have a ticket to the show.

## Why is architecture like a song?

Opera houses have been some of the most iconic buildings in cities for centuries. But many of them have something called "thresholds" — fancy doors, security checks, and ticket booths that keep visitors at arm's length. The architects at Snøhetta wanted to tear those barriers down. As they put it, symphonies are divided into movements, and **movement** should act as both an architectural and social connector.

Their solution? A **gravity-defying spiral staircase** inspired by the Chinese fan, rising from the riverbank up to an observation platform. Visitors can climb all the way up and enjoy a free view of the glittering central business district across the water — no ticket required.

## Three halls, one stunning design

Inside, the opera house has **three separate auditoriums** with room for 4,200 people in total:

- **The Harmony Hall** — the largest, with 2,000 crimson-red seats spread across five levels, designed like a finely tuned instrument with perfect acoustics.
- **The Soar Theater** — 1,200 seats for intimate performances, especially traditional Chinese *Xiqu* opera and baroque works.
- **The Open Stage** — a flexible 1,000-seat space with floor-to-ceiling windows that bathe the stage in natural light, perfect for experimental productions.

The building opened with Giacomo Puccini's *Tosca* — a fitting choice since it is one of the most famous operas ever written, even though it is not set in China!

## Fun Fact

The name "opera" comes from the Italian word for "work" — and the first surviving opera, *Dafne*, was written way back in 1598 in Florence, Italy by Jacopo Peri. That makes the art form **over 400 years old** — but the buildings where opera is performed keep getting more and more imaginative!

## For Grown-ups

The Shanghai Grand Opera House was designed by **Snøhetta**, the Norwegian architecture firm known for projects like the Oslo Opera House (whose sloped roof you can actually walk on). The building sits along the **Huangpu River**, which Wikipedia confirms is a 113 km-long river flowing north through Shanghai into the Yangtze estuary, dividing the city into Puxi (west) and Pudong (east). The Huangpu is the biggest river in central Shanghai, with the Bund and Lujiazuidi financial district on its banks. Wikipedia's "Opera" article confirms that opera originated in late 16th-century Italy and incorporates music, singing, acting, scenery, and costume. The building's design philosophy of "removing thresholds" aligns with Snøhetta's stated goal of creating architecture that invites civic interaction rather than imposing barriers. The opening weekend featured Puccini's *Tosca* and Verdi's *Aida*, with the American Ballet Company's *Swan Lake* scheduled three weeks later.
