## The Sun is covered in fountains of fire

Look at the Sun's edge through a special telescope and you will see something astonishing: millions of tiny jets of glowing gas leaping upward, again and again. Scientists call them **spicules**. Each one races up at tens of kilometres per second, reaching thousands of kilometres high before tumbling back down — all in just a few minutes.

For more than a century, scientists pictured each spicule as its own little twisting tornado. But a new computer study says we've been fooled — and the truth is even stranger.

## An optical illusion in space

A team from the University of Sheffield, the Indian Institute for Astrophysics and the University of Newcastle built a **computer simulation** of the Sun using a program called Pencil Code. Instead of pointing a telescope at the Sun, they re-created the churning, boiling gas of its surface on a computer and watched what happened.

Their model showed that the Sun's surface "drums" on the atmosphere above it, launching waves that travel along invisible magnetic field lines. As those waves rise, they grow taller and taller until they **break**, blasting huge **curtains** of plasma upward — sheets of glowing gas folded like an accordion.

Here's the trick: we usually see the Sun from the side, which flattens everything into two dimensions. When all those folds line up, they *look* like separate spinning jets. Move your viewpoint, and the truth appears — it was one rippling curtain all along.

- **Model:** Pencil Code magnetohydrodynamic simulation
- **Real data checked:** NASA's IRIS and Japan's Hinode satellites
- **Verdict:** the "spinning jets" are the papery folds of plasma curtains

## Fun Fact

The Sun's surface is about **6,000°C**, but its outer atmosphere, the corona, is over **1,000,000°C** — hundreds of times hotter. Nobody is quite sure why, and scientists have been arguing about it for about 80 years. This "curtain" research is one clue in that great detective story!

## Try this at home

Grab a sheet of kitchen paper and hold it by one edge. Now gently blow underneath it and let it ripple. Look at it from the *side* (edge-on) — the folds seem like separate waves. But look at it from *above* and you can see it is really one single sheet moving. That's exactly how astronomers think the Sun's "spinning jets" fool our eyes!

## The real whirlpools

The simulation also uncovered something brand new. Hidden in the model were giant spinning columns of plasma — **hundreds of kilometres across**, twirling at up to 20 kilometres per second. The team named them **Coronal Swirling Conduits**, or CoSCos.

They may be the missing piece of the puzzle. As the plasma curtains lose energy and sink, these whirlpools grab them and pull them back up into the corona — carrying heat and helping power the **solar wind** that streams out through the whole solar system. When that wind reaches Earth, it can paint the sky with glowing auroras.

There is still plenty left to discover, and a fleet of brand-new Sun-watching spacecraft is on the job. Not bad for a star that keeps disguising its own fireworks as something smaller.
