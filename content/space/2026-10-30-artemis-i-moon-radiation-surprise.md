# 1. How Do Astronauts Know Where the Invisible Rays Are Hitting?

## 2. Salt crystals as tiny radiation detectives

When NASA launched **Artemis I** around the Moon in November 2022, there were no astronauts aboard — just in case anything went wrong. But two **human-shaped mannequins** named **Zohar** and **Helga** were strapped into the Orion capsule. Their mission? To feel the invisible rays of space radiation and report back what they found.

Deep space is filled with a cosmic storm: atomic nuclei from exploded stars, the Sun's own solar wind, and high-energy particles from distant galaxies. In low Earth orbit — where the International Space Station lives at about **400 kilometers up** — our planet's magnetic field and the **Van Allen radiation belts** act like a shield, trapping most of the dangerous particles in invisible doughnuts of swirling electrons and protons.

But once you sail beyond the Moon, that protection disappears. It is like stepping out from behind a protective wall into a hailstorm.

## 3. Uniform hits vs. uneven hits — the Moon's surprising pattern

The detectors on Zohar and Helga revealed something unexpected. Near the Moon, radiation doesn't just hit one spot — it **hits the whole body almost evenly**. Imagine someone throwing tiny pebbles at you from every direction at once. Every part of you gets the same amount of "pebble" hits.

This happens because the dominant radiation near the Moon — called **galactic cosmic rays** — are ultra-high-energy atomic nuclei from outside our solar system. They are so energetic that they punch straight through a person's body, depositing a little bit of energy along their entire path. There is no "skin deep" dose and "deep interior" dose — the particles simply pass through everything.

Back in low Earth orbit, the story is different. The trapped protons in the Van Allen belts collide with the astronaut's body at shallower angles and lose energy quickly. That means the **skin and bone marrow** get bombarded the most, while organs deeper inside are relatively shielded. It is like hail hitting one side of a house — the impact is concentrated where the hail hits first.

## 4. Why the difference is smaller than expected

Here is the surprise: even though astronauts near the Moon are exposed to **more total radiation** than those in low Earth orbit, the difference is **not as dramatic as scientists thought it would be**.

Think of it this way: in Earth orbit, you are standing in a light rain sheltered under a roof. Water drips off the edge in a few spots — concentrated, predictable. Near the Moon, you are out in a light, misty rain that covers your whole body more evenly but with less intensity overall.

Low-Earth-orbit astronauts still get a significant dose from the **Van Allen belt protons** — but those are energetic particles trapped in Earth's magnetic field. Near the Moon, the galactic cosmic rays are individually more energetic (capable of traveling completely through the body), but they are **less numerous**. The sparse but penetrating cosmic rays end up giving a total dose that is only modestly higher than the dense but localized trapped-proton flux of Earth orbit.

## Try this at home

You can model radiation shielding with a flashlight and a colander!

1. Turn on a flashlight and shine it straight through a colander in a dark room.
2. First, hold the colander **close** to a wall — the light that gets through makes a bright, concentrated pattern. This is like Van Allen belt protons hitting just the skin side.
3. Now hold the colander **far** from the wall — the light spreads out and covers the wall more evenly. This is like galactic cosmic rays spreading through the entire body.

The colander's holes are the radiation particles, and the spreading pattern shows how the same number of particles can distribute differently depending on the geometry. Real astronauts don't have colanders for skin, of course — but space agencies use this kind of modeling to design better shielding for future Moon missions.

## Fun Fact

The **Van Allen belts** were discovered in 1958 by the launch of **Explorer 1**, America's first satellite. Physicist James Van Allen realized the satellite's radiation detector was showing a huge spike in radiation at certain altitudes — and realized Earth is wrapped in invisible rings of trapped particles. Today, NASA plans the trajectories of every Apollo, Artemis, and ISS mission to pass quickly through the belts, minimizing crew exposure. The "Safe Zone" between the inner and outer belts — roughly at **2 to 4 Earth radii** — was used as a transit corridor by Apollo missions and will be used again by Artemis astronauts.
