Far away from Earth, past Jupiter and Saturn, there is a planet that rolls around the Sun on its side like a giant bowling ball. That planet is **Uranus**, and it has a small, pale moon named **Ariel**. Now some scientists think Ariel may be hiding a surprise — or at least, it once did.

## A moon covered in cracks

Ariel is not big — only about **720 miles (1,159 kilometres)** across. But its surface is strange. Ancient impact craters sit right next to smooth, young-looking patches, and the whole moon is laced with **fractures**: long cracks, ridges and sunken strips of crust called grabens.

Some of those features are bigger than almost anything of their kind anywhere else in the Solar System. Something powerful must have shaped them. The question is: what?

## The squeezing-and-stretching trick

A team of scientists, led by Caleb Strom with Alex Patthoff and Tom Nordheim, had an idea. They mapped the big structures on Ariel's surface, then used computer models to test a possibility.

The idea is called **tidal stress**. If a moon's orbit is a slightly stretched oval instead of a perfect circle, then as it travels around its planet it gets pulled and squeezed over and over — changing shape from round like a football, back to round like a soccer ball, and back again. Do that for long enough and the crust can crack.

The models suggest Ariel's orbit was once about **40 times more stretched** than it is today — and about four times more stretched than Jupiter's moon Europa, which is famously covered in cracks for exactly this reason.

## You need an ocean to do it

Here is the surprising part. When the team compared their models with the real cracks on Ariel, they found the maths only works if there was **an ocean** under the ice. A thin ice shell floating on a huge ocean could do it — or a slightly smaller ocean squeezed harder by a stretched orbit.

Either way, they estimate the hidden ocean may have been **more than 100 miles (170 kilometres) deep**. To picture that: the Pacific Ocean is only about 2.5 miles (4 km) deep on average. Ariel's possible ocean would have been dozens of times deeper.

"It's a really exciting result," the researchers explain, because it means tiny, icy moons can hide enormous secrets beneath a quiet crust.

## Try this at home

Take a bar of chocolate or a thin biscuit. Gently bend it back and forth — not hard enough to snap, just enough to feel it flex. Do it thirty times in a row. That repeated flexing is tidal stress: the same push-and-pull that may have cracked the crust of a small moon billions of kilometres away.

## Fun fact

Ariel is not the only suspect. An earlier study by the same team found hints of a hidden ocean on another Uranian moon, **Miranda**. That has some scientists wondering whether Uranus might have **twin ocean worlds** circling it — two quiet moons that each once held a sea.
