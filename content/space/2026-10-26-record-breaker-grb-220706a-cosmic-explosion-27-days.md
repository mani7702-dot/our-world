# What made a gamma-ray burst flare longer than ever — for 27 days?

## A flash that wouldn't quit

Some of the most dramatic light shows in the universe are called **gamma-ray bursts** (GRBs) — brief but titanic flashes of gamma rays that, for a few seconds, can outshine the entire rest of the sky. Most of the action fades fast, within minutes or hours.

But a newly studied burst broke that rule. Designated **GRB 220706A**, it was first caught on **July 6, 2022**, and then something surprising happened: its central engine kept lighting up X-rays on and off for about **27 days** — roughly 20 days longer than the previous record holder.

Astronomer Benjamin Gompertz of the University of Birmingham summed it up: "The fact that we see flaring at around 27 days in the GRB's rest frame tells us there's an ongoing active process, and that's unprecedented in the known population."

## What powers the engine?

Gamma-ray bursts come in two main flavors. **Long** bursts (those lasting more than two seconds) are tied to the collapse of a massive star — a star much heavier than our Sun that runs out of fuel and implodes, forming a **black hole** and launching twin jets of energy. Wikipedia's "Gamma-ray burst" article confirms this collapsar model: the crushing collapse creates a rapidly feeding black hole whose jets punch through the dying star.

**Short** bursts, by contrast, come from the **merging of two neutron stars** — city-sized stellar remnants crashing together in a violent dance.

The puzzle with GRB 220706A is that its engine kept churning long after the initial blast. X-ray flaring right after a GRB is common, but it almost always fades within the first few days. For it to continue for 27 days means a sustained process was still at work — and scientists aren't yet sure whether the engine was a newborn black hole, a highly magnetized neutron star (a *magnetar*) that kept spinning down, or something else entirely.

## How do we watch such a fast, far-away flash?

The burst was snapped up by NASA's **Swift** satellite, which constantly scans the sky for these sudden explosions and immediately pivots its instruments to study the fading afterglow at X-ray, ultraviolet, and optical wavelengths. After the initial gamma-ray detection, Swift kept watching night after night, recording the repeated X-ray flares as the engine flickered on and off.

It's a bit like hearing a firework go off in the distance, then waiting to see if a second bang follows — except in this case, the "fireworks" happened **billions of light-years away** and the delay stretched across nearly a month of Earth time.

## Try this at home

Fill a shallow pan with water and add one drop of milk so the water turns cloudy. Drip food coloring in one spot — that single drop spreads, swirls, and eventually fades, like a quick burst of energy. Now add a second drop every few seconds to keep the swirls going. That's a simple model of how a single central engine can keep stirring up a blast long after the first flash.

## Fun Fact

The most energetic gamma-ray burst ever recorded, **GRB 221006A** (don't confuse the names!), packed enough punch that if it had happened in our own Milky Way — and pointed our way — it would have rivaled the brightness of the full Moon and stripped Earth's ozone layer. Luckily, the universe is vast and these beams are narrow: the odds of one hitting us are tiny, and GRB 220706A was safely billions of light-years away.

## For grown-ups

GRB 220706A's 27-day X-ray flaring activity challenges the standard fireball model, which predicts that energy injection from the central engine should cease within days. The sustained flaring suggests ongoing accretion or magnetic energy release from a long-lived compact object. The collapsar model (Woosley 1993) remains the standard origin for long GRBs: a rapidly rotating, massive Wolf-Rayet star collapses to form a black hole, with accretion-disk-fed jets producing the gamma-ray emission. Whether GRB 220706A's central engine was a supramassive magnetar (which can spin down over weeks) or prolonged fallback accretion onto a black hole is an active area of research. The Swift Gamma-Ray Burst Mission (launched 2004) is NASA's dedicated GRB observatory, localizing bursts and monitoring their afterglows across the electromagnetic spectrum. Wikipedia confirms that long GRBs (duration >2 s) are associated with star-forming regions and the deaths of massive stars, while short GRBs (<2 s) originate from compact object mergers (neutron star–neutron star or neutron star–black hole). Wikipedia also confirms the "collapsar" mechanism for long GRBs and that Swift's Burst Alert Telescope (BAT) triggers autonomous slewing of the spacecraft within seconds of a detection.
