# 1. How Did a Camera in Space Take This Picture?

Imagine trying to photograph a flying airplane while you are standing on a mountaintop far below — and the airplane is flying faster than a bullet. Now imagine doing that with a spacecraft orbiting 250 miles overhead. Sounds impossible, right?

That is exactly what happened here. A **commercial satellite named WorldView 3**, owned by the company Vantor, snapped this photo from only **56 miles (90 kilometers)** away — that is practically next door in space terms! The camera is so sharp it can see objects as small as a smartphone from that distance. To put that in perspective: if you were standing on a football field, you could read the title of a book held by someone at the other goalpost, all from space.

# 2. What Is Shenlong, and Why Is It So Secret?

**Shenlong** (pronounced "shen-long," meaning "divine dragon" in Chinese) is China's secretive **reusable robotic space plane**. It looks like a miniature space shuttle with swept-back wings, but unlike the old Space Shuttle, it launches folded up inside a rocket's nose cone — like a present tucked inside a box.

Once in orbit, Shenlong opens its cargo bay doors and can do all sorts of hidden jobs: it might carry secret sensors, test new space technologies, or deploy small satellites into orbit. China has only said that the space plane helps test technologies for "more convenient and affordable round-trip methods for the peaceful use of space." But here is the catch: Shenlong has also been spotted **releasing mystery objects** into orbit on past missions, which has space watchers guessing what it is really up to.

# 3. A Photo Worth a Thousand Questions

This new 2024 photo — finally released by the U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) — shows Shenlong **docked to another satellite** with its big solar-panel arrays unfurled like metallic wings. The most revealing detail? Its **payload bay doors are wide open**, hinting at whatever cargo it is carrying on its current, fourth mission (which launched in February 2026).

It is not yet known which satellite Shenlong is visiting, or what experiments are inside that open cargo bay. But the photo is a rare, crystal-clear glimpse of a machine that usually stays hidden in the shadows of space.

## Try this at home

You can play space photographer too! On a clear night, step outside and look up at the brightest point of light moving steadily across the sky — that is probably the International Space Station, zooming 250 miles overhead at 17,500 mph (that's 5 times faster than a bullet). Use a smartphone app like SkySafari or Star Walk to find it. Time yourself: the ISS crosses from horizon to horizon in about 90 seconds! Real space photographers use special tracking mounts that spin in the opposite direction of Earth's rotation to keep these fast-moving targets in frame for longer exposures. The WorldView satellites that photographed Shenlong are like robot versions of that — they track, zoom, and snap.

## Fun Fact

The United States operates its own secretive space plane called the **X-37B**, built by Boeing. It is nearly a twin to Shenlong — both are robotic, both land like airplane on a runway, and neither one has passengers. The X-37B has been in orbit for more than 2,000 days on its current mission, carrying out science experiments and mysterious military tasks. When China launched Shenlong in 2021, some space watchers joked that both countries were running a parallel "space plane space race" — invisible to the public but tracked closely by astronomers and satellite hunters. The difference? Shenlong now has its first high-resolution photo from space, while the X-37B has released only artist's drawings and one official painting.
