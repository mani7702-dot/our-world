Some of the most exciting science happens when something looks ordinary at first — and then turns out to be anything but.

## A flash that seemed too weak

Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are the biggest explosions in the universe, second only to the Big Bang. They blaze for a few seconds when a giant star runs out of fuel and its core collapses, firing out twin jets — narrow beams of glowing plasma racing outward at nearly the speed of light.

On 10 March 2026, NASA's Fermi telescope caught one named **GRB 260310A**. Its first flash of gamma rays was oddly weak. If it were just another burst, astronomers might have shrugged and moved on.

## A flashlight held at an angle

They didn't — because this burst had a secret. Most GRBs are spotted when one of their jets is aimed straight at us, like a flashlight shining in your eyes. This one was pointed *to the side*. Astronomers call it an **off-axis** jet.

"We see hundreds of GRBs every year, but the oddballs are the most informative ones," said co-author Roberto Ricci. Lead author Yu-Han Yang put it simply: the jet was like a flashlight seen from the side — dimmer, but still there.

## Clues in many colours of light

Because the burst was nearby (in space terms!), teams could study it with many telescopes at once: NASA's Chandra, ESA's XMM-Newton, the Einstein Probe, the Large Binocular Telescope and the Very Large Array radio dishes. Together they watched the afterglow change across X-rays, visible light and radio waves — revealing two shock waves travelling through the jet.

## The surprise three weeks later

Then something unexpected happened: about three weeks after the blast, the X-rays brightened *again*. That pointed to a two-part jet — a bright inner core wrapped in a wider, dimmer outer layer — rather than a simple cone.

So the burst wasn't weak at all. It was one of the brightest afterglows ever recorded. It just happened to be facing the wrong way.

## Try this at home

Shine a torch straight at a wall, then turn it sideways. The beam is just as bright — your view changed, not the light. That is exactly what happened with GRB 260310A.

## Fun fact

Gamma-ray bursts were discovered by accident in the 1960s. Satellites built to watch for secret nuclear bomb tests kept picking up flashes coming from deep space instead!
