Imagine a place so cold that air itself would freeze solid and fall to the ground. That place is **Pluto**, the dwarf planet at the edge of our Solar System. Its surface temperature is around **−229 °C** — cold enough that almost nothing moves.

So scientists were puzzled when they spotted something odd in pictures of Pluto's most famous feature: a huge, bright, **heart-shaped glacier** called **Sputnik Planitia**.

## A heart with dark, shiny lines

Sputnik Planitia is a vast plain of frozen **nitrogen** ice, bigger than Texas and Oklahoma put together. Up close, it is not flat and smooth. It is filled with bumpy **polygons** — sort of like tiles in a giant bathroom floor — separated by thin, dark lines.

Some of those dark lines look *wet*. Not wet with water, though. Scientists think they might be wet with **liquid nitrogen**.

## Why that is strange

Liquid nitrogen cannot rain down from Pluto's sky. The air is far too thin and far too cold, so nitrogen there is either gas high up or solid ice on the ground. There is no in-between.

So where could a liquid come from? The team's answer: **from below**.

## The trick: squeezing ice until it melts

A team led by Dr. Alan Stern compared the dark lines on Pluto with satellite pictures of **glaciers on Earth** — in Greenland and Antarctica. On Earth, when meltwater flows on top of ice, it leaves dark, narrow streaks that look almost exactly the same.

The team thinks the same thing is happening on Pluto, but with nitrogen instead of water. Here's the clever part: the nitrogen ice at the bottom of the basin is **several kilometres deep**. All that weight creates enormous pressure — and squeezing ice under pressure can make it melt, even when it is very cold.

That melted nitrogen can then squeeze upward through narrow cracks, bubbling to the surface like lava or a geyser, spreading across the glacier, and leaving dark marks behind before freezing again. It is a kind of **cryovolcano** — a volcano that erupts ice instead of hot rock.

"This surface is quite young, probably less than one million years," says Dr. Kelsi Singer, one of the study's authors. That means the marks we see must have formed recently. And since more than **half of Pluto has never been photographed in detail**, similar leaks could be hiding elsewhere.

## Try this at home

Fill a small plastic bag with ice cubes and press down on it hard with a heavy book for a few minutes. The pressure squeezes the ice at the bottom, and a little of it melts into water — even though the room is nowhere near warm enough to melt it on its own. That is pressure-melting, the same trick that may be making liquid nitrogen on Pluto.

## Fun fact

Pluto's weird leaking glacier is not unique in the Solar System. Neptune's moon **Triton** has nitrogen geysers that Voyager 2 spotted back in 1989 — and scientists think the very same pressure-melting process could be the cause. Two icy worlds, one surprising recipe.
