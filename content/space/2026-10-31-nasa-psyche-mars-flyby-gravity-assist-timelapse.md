Imagine packing a car for a very long road trip — then pulling into a random town along the way, not to visit, but simply to check that your engine, brakes and headlights still work.

That is roughly what NASA's **Psyche** spacecraft just did at **Mars**.

## A spacecraft chasing a metal world

**Psyche** launched in **October 2023** and is headed for a one-of-a-kind target: an asteroid also called **Psyche** (full name **16 Psyche**), which orbits in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. Unlike most asteroids, which are made of rock and ice, 16 Psyche appears to be largely **metal**.

That makes it fascinating. Scientists wonder whether it might be the exposed **core of a small planet** — a leftover building block that never finished forming. Studying it up close could teach us about the hidden metal hearts of planets like Earth, which we can never see directly. The spacecraft is due to arrive in **2029**.

## The trick: borrow a planet's speed

Reaching the asteroid belt takes a lot of fuel, so engineers used a shortcut called a **gravity assist** — a "gravitational slingshot."

Picture a ball rolling past the bottom of a moving swing. The swing nudges the ball faster without you pushing it. In the same way, a spacecraft can skim past a **planet**, and the planet's gravity **speeds it up and bends its path** — all for free.

On **15 May 2026**, Psyche sailed past Mars at an altitude of about **2,864 miles (4,609 kilometres)**. It borrowed a little of Mars's motion and tilted onto exactly the course it needs. "It was years in the making, and the navigation team nailed it," said **Bob Mase**, Psyche's project manager at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

## A free test run on a familiar world

The flyby came with a bonus: the team got to **test every instrument on a world they already know well**.

- The **spectrometer**, which will identify what the asteroid is made of, detected a burst of **neutrons** escaping Mars — close to what scientists predicted.
- The **magnetometer** recorded its first magnetic "signature" from a planet, catching the zone where the solar wind slams into Mars's magnetic field.
- The twin **cameras** photographed windblown craters, Mars's bright south polar ice cap and the double-ringed **Huygens crater** — and even picked out Mars's tiny moons, **Phobos** and **Deimos**, from far away. That was practice for searching for small moons around asteroid Psyche.

Those month-long pictures were stitched into a **time-lapse video**, showing Mars growing from a thin crescent into a detailed world — then shrinking away as the spacecraft sped on.

## Try this at home

Feel a slingshot for yourself. Roll a light ball slowly toward a friend's hand and have them gently flick it as it passes. The ball leaves **faster** than it arrived — the extra push came from your friend, just as a planet nudges a spacecraft. (A planet, of course, is far too big to feel the nudge back.)

## Fun fact

Psyche's cameras caught the moons **Phobos** and **Deimos**, but those worlds are tiny — Deimos is only about **12 kilometres wide**, roughly the length of a city. Spotting them from millions of kilometres away is like reading a friend's name on a sign across a whole city.
