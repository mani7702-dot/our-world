# 1. What Do Divers and Astronauts Have in Common?

Both need to survive in a place where humans do not belong. Astronauts float inside a metal tube that orbits 250 miles above Earth. Aquanauts live in a tiny room at the bottom of the ocean. In both cases, a single mistake with the life-support system can be fatal — and every movement must be planned because you cannot simply "step outside."

## 2. Living Underwater for Five Days

This past summer, Dr. Michaela Musilova led a team called SEA STAR on a mission at the Jules' Undersea Laboratory, a research station 22 feet underwater near Key Largo, Florida. To become an aquanaut, you must stay submerged for at least 24 hours without surfacing — that is when your body enters "saturation," a state where your tissues absorb the surrounding gas. From that point on, a single, slow decompression at the end of the mission replaces daily dives, just like a real spaceship that cannot make quick return trips.

## 3. NASA Has Been Doing This Since 2001

NASA's NEEMO program (NASA Extreme Environment Mission Operations) uses the Aquarius underwater laboratory — the world's only undersea research station — for 7 to 14 day missions. The crew practices spacewalks in weighted boots to simulate microgravity, tests communication delays, grows microgreens, and solves problems that arise in an isolated, alien environment. The water outside is hostile and unforgiving, much like space.

## 4. Why Not Just Use a Giant Pool?

Pools are great for short rehearsals, but they cannot replicate the real psychological and engineering challenges of living in an isolated bubble for weeks. Underwater habitats force aquanauts to manage life-support systems, communicate through radio with delays, and handle unexpected issues — exactly the skills astronauts need on the International Space Station, the Moon, and eventually Mars.

<div class="fun-fact">
<h4>Fun Fact:</h4>
<p>The word "aquanaut" comes from the Latin "aqua" (water) and "nautes" (sailor). After 24 hours underwater, aquanauts and astronauts both experience the same thing — they cannot simply surface or return home on a whim. Every breath depends on systems working perfectly.</p>
