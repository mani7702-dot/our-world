# 1. From Orbit to the Lab: What Happens After a Moon Mission?

When you spend 165 days floating above Earth — and then fly around the far side of the Moon — what do you do when it's time to come home for good? For Reid Wiseman, a retired U.S. Navy captain and former NASA astronaut, the next chapter is not retirement. Instead of hanging up his spacesuit for good, he's putting on a new hat: helping lead one of America's most important space-research centers.

NASA announced in October 2026 that Wiseman, who commanded the historic **Artemis II** mission in April, will step down from active astronaut duty and become the **deputy director of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL)**. He'll start this new role on November 30, 2026, working alongside JPL Director Dave Gallagher to guide the lab that builds some of the most advanced robotic missions in the solar system — missions like the Mars rovers Curiosity and Perseverance, the Europa Clipper, and the Europa Lander.

Artemis II was humanity's first crewed flight beyond the Moon since Apollo 17 in 1972. Wiseman and his three crewmates flew the **Orion** capsule on a 10-day journey around the Moon and back, testing the systems that will carry astronauts to the lunar surface for Artemis III — NASA's planned return to the Moon.

# 2. Why JPL Needs an Astronaut's Perspective

JPL, run by the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) on NASA's behalf, doesn't just build robots — it solves problems that require creative thinking, bold ideas, and the ability to work as a team under pressure. Those are the same skills an astronaut uses every day in space.

"Reid has spent his career at the edge of what is possible," said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman. "He has the technical judgment, operational experience, and leadership to help push that edge farther."

Wiseman's journey to this point has been anything but ordinary. He joined NASA in 2009, first flying to the International Space Station in 2014 for a 165-day mission. He later became NASA's chief astronaut, overseeing crew assignments and flight safety. Then came Artemis II — the test flight that proved Orion could carry humans safely around the Moon and back.

# 3. What's Next for Artemis and JPL

Artemis II commander Reid Wiseman (third from left) with the JPL team. With Wiseman's appointment, JPL gains a leader who has literally seen the Moon up close and understands what it takes to get humans to another world and back.

JPL is currently preparing for the launch of ESA's **Hera** mission to study a binary asteroid system, the final mission of the **Cassini** spacecraft's legacy team, and is in the early design phases of a potential Venus rover. Meanwhile, NASA is targeting mid-2027 for **Artemis III**, which will test the combined Orion capsule and lunar landers (SpaceX's Starship and Blue Origin's Blue Moon) that could land the first astronauts on the Moon since 1972.

Wiseman's experience commanding a lunar flyby and his deep understanding of human spaceflight make him uniquely suited to bridge the gap between NASA's human spaceflight and science divisions. Whether it's planning the next mission to explore Jupiter's moon Europa or helping design the systems that will take humans to the lunar surface, his insights from flying beyond low-Earth orbit will help chart the course for the next great era of space exploration.

Now imagine you had to guide a robot (blindly) through that room using only your map and a few simple instructions. That's what it's like to command a spacecraft millions of miles away. NASA's JPL scientists do this with rovers on Mars, guiding them around rocks and craters using only photos sent back from space!

**Fun Fact:** Reid Wiseman flew to the Moon farther from Earth than anyone since the Apollo missions — Orion's crew reached a distance of about 254,000 miles (408,000 km) from our planet, surpassing the 240,000-mile average distance to the Moon itself because Orion's trajectory looped around the far side before coming home.
