# How did a golden eagle chick hatch on a Scottish grouse moor?

You've probably seen pictures of golden eagles soaring high above mountains. They are among nature's most impressive predators — strong enough to hunt deer and foxes! But here's something surprising: these powerful birds have nearly disappeared from parts of Scotland where people raise birds for hunting.

Recently, something wonderful happened in a secret spot in the Moffat Hills of southern Scotland. A baby golden eagle — given the special name "Beaky Junior" — hatched and learned to fly from a nest sitting right on a **managed grouse moor**. This is the very first time a golden eagle chick has successfully fledged (that means "learned to fly") from one of these moors!

## Why is this a big deal?

Golden eagles used to live all over Scotland, but their numbers dropped to just a handful of breeding pairs in the south. In many places, they faced dangers because of land managed for hunting birds. In fact, a report from the Royal Society for the Protection of Birds found that nearly two-thirds of all bird-of-prey persecution incidents happened on land managed for gamebird shooting.

The Moffat Hills are a beautiful area, but they're also used for raising grouse for hunting. When a golden eagle decides to make a nest there, it can be risky for the baby eagle.

## How did people help?

A group called **Restoring Upland Nature (RUN)** started a project in the summer of 2018. They carefully moved golden eagles from the Scottish Highlands to a safe, secret location in the Moffat Hills. The goal was simple but bold: help the eagles come back to southern Scotland while working alongside the people who live and work on the land.

Dr. Cat Barlow, who leads the project, says it only worked because landowners and gamekeepers gave "incredible support." The gamekeepers helped keep the baby eagles safe, and even helped name them. When Beaky Junior's siblings were born, the gamekeepers named this little one too — showing that people and eagles can be friends.

Because of this teamwork, the golden eagle population in southern Scotland is now at its **highest level in history** — more than 50 birds, including five chicks that fledged this year alone.

## What does this teach us?

The story of Beaky Junior shows that when we bring together two groups who might seem very different — conservation scientists and gamekeepers — amazing things can happen. Golden eagles need wide open spaces and protection from harm. Gamekeepers know the land well and care about nature too.

When they work as a team, even the most majestic birds of prey can return to places they haven't nested in for decades. As Dr. Barlow puts it, this little chick gives "genuine hope" that future generations will get to see golden eagles soaring across the Scottish hills.

## Try this at home

Next time you're outside, look up and try to spot a large bird flying high with slowly beating wings and a steady glide. Golden eagles can be identified by their size — they're as big as a child! You can also visit a local nature center to learn about birds of prey in your area.

## Fun Fact

Golden eagles don't get their name because they're golden-colored all over — it's their neck and head feathers that have a beautiful golden-brown glow! Baby golden eagles are born with white spots on their tails and wings (called "bastard plumage"), which fade as they grow older. The scientific name for the golden eagle is *Aquila chrysaetos*, which comes from ancient Greek words for "gold" and "eagle."
