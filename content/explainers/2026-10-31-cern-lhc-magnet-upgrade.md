# Why is CERN taking apart the world's biggest machine?

Somewhere deep under the border between France and Switzerland sits a machine so big that you could walk around it for hours and still not reach the end. It is called the **Large Hadron Collider (LHC)**, and it is the largest science experiment ever built — a ring **27 kilometres** long.

Right now, teams at CERN are taking parts of it *apart*. But don't worry — nothing is broken. They are doing it on purpose, to make the machine even more powerful.

## What does the LHC actually do?

The LHC takes tiny particles and speeds them up until they are racing around the ring at almost the speed of light. Then it makes them **crash into each other**. When particles smash together at enormous energy, they break into even smaller pieces — and those pieces can tell scientists what the universe is made of.

Thousands of magnets line the ring, and each one has a special job. Imagine them like the rails and brakes of a rollercoaster, only far more precise.

## The secret is in the magnets

The most important magnets for collisions are the **inner triplets** — groups of three magnet units sitting on each side of the four big detectors, like the ATLAS experiment.

Their job is to squeeze the two beams as tightly as possible *just before* they meet. The tighter the squeeze, the higher the chance that particles will collide. That squeeze rate is called **luminosity** — basically, how many crashes happen per second.

The LHC switched off on **29 June 2026** for a long shutdown. Since then, engineers have begun cutting out the old magnets and preparing to slot in a brand-new generation. In September, crews cut the very first magnet connection — the official start of the swap.

## Stronger magnets, bigger discoveries

The new magnets are made with a different recipe. Instead of **niobium-titanium**, they use **niobium-tin** coils, which can create magnetic fields of **11.3 tesla** — about **40% stronger** than before.

Stronger magnets mean tighter beams, which means *more* collisions for scientists to study. It is a bit like upgrading a camera so it can take many more photos per second.

"The replacement of these magnets with the new HiLumi LHC inner triplets is crucial for the coming high-luminosity years," explains **Jean-Philippe Tock**, who helps lead the shutdown work. The first new magnet should arrive in the tunnel around the **start of 2029** — a big job involving **16 cryostats and 28 cryo-assemblies**.

## Try this at home

You can explore magnetism yourself!

1. **Test magnet strength.** Hold a paperclip near a fridge magnet, then near a stronger magnet. Can you feel the difference in pull?
2. **Squeeze a beam of light.** Shine a torch at a wall. Move it closer. Notice how the bright spot gets smaller and brighter — that is "focusing", just like the beams!
3. **Race two marbles.** Roll them toward each other on a table. The harder they hit, the more they bounce apart — that is what collisions do.

## Fun Fact

The LHC's ring is cooled to around **-271 °C** — colder than outer space — so that its magnets can carry electricity with zero resistance. That is what lets them create such powerful magnetic fields!
