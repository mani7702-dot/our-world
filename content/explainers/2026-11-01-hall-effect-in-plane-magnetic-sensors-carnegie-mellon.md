Have you ever wondered how your bike computer knows how fast you're going, or how a car's wheel-speed sensor works without touching anything? Many of those gadgets rely on a 147-year-old physics trick called the **Hall effect**. Now a team at Carnegie Mellon University has discovered the trick works in a completely new way — and no one had managed to prove it before.

## The rule everyone learned in 1879

Back in 1879, an American physicist named **Edwin Hall** found something odd. If you send an electric current through a thin piece of material and hold a **magnet flat against its top or bottom** (that is, the field pointing straight through the film), the moving charges get pushed to one side. That sideways shove creates a tiny voltage you can measure.

For more than a century, scientists assumed the magnet *had* to point straight through the material — "perpendicular," in science-speak — for the effect to appear. Because the voltage tells you how many charge carriers are moving and how easily they travel, the Hall effect became a workhorse tool for testing materials and building sensors.

## The twist: the magnet works sideways too

The Carnegie Mellon team, working in their lab called LIQUID, tried something different. They built a device only a few atoms thick from a crystal called **tantalum iridium telluride**, then stacked a thin magnetic layer on top of it.

When they pointed the magnetic field **flat along the film instead of through it**, they still measured a Hall voltage. That was supposed to be impossible — but there it was. As researcher Simranjeet Singh put it, "For a long time, people thought the Hall effect only worked when the magnetic field was applied perpendicular to the plane of the film. We've shown that that's not true."

The trick was finding a material with just the right shape of internal symmetry, then making it magnetic. Nobody had matched those conditions in a real experiment until now.

## One sensor, many directions

Why does this matter? Today, measuring a magnetic field in two different directions needs **two separate sensors**. Because this new device responds to fields along more than one axis at once, a single ultrathin sensor could do the whole job.

That could lead to smaller, simpler magnetic sensors for electronics, transport and even medical imaging. The team is now testing other material pairs and checking whether the device still works at room temperature — an important step before any real-world use.

## Fun Fact

The Hall effect is so reliable that it helped confirm that electric current in metals is carried by *negative* charges — electrons — before anyone could see an electron directly.

## Try this at home

Ask a grown-up for a fridge magnet and a small torch. Switch the torch on and hold the magnet near the metal body of the torch. Some torches flicker or dim slightly — you are nudging the moving electric charges inside the metal, the same family of effects Edwin Hall studied with a magnet and a wire.
