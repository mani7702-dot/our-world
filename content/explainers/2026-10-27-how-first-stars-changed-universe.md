# How did the first stars change the universe — and where did all the elements come from?

## A universe that started almost empty

Right after the Big Bang, the entire universe was almost nothing but two elements: **hydrogen** (about 75%) and **helium** (about 25%), with just a tiny sprinkle of lithium and beryllium. There were no stars yet, no planets, no oxygen to breathe, no carbon in your body. Everything heavier than helium — every atom of carbon in your cells, every bit of iron in your blood, every grain of sand on Earth — had to be made later, inside stars.

But how? And when did it all begin?

## Meet the first stars

The very first stars — called **Population III** stars — were born from the primordial soup of hydrogen and helium left over from the Big Bang. Because they had no heavy elements to help gas clouds cool and fragment, these stars grew to be **hundreds of times more massive** than our Sun. They burned incredibly hot and bright, but they also died young — in just a few hundred thousand years instead of billions of years like our Sun will live.

When a massive star runs out of fuel, it explodes as a **supernova**. The first stars exploded as spectacular **pair-instability supernovae**, blowing apart completely and scattering their insides across space — the very first delivery service of heavy elements.

## The MEGATRON project: a time machine in code

A team of scientists from the University of Bath (UK), the University of Chicago (US), and the Institut d'Astrophysique de Paris (France) built the **MEGATRON project** — a set of extraordinarily detailed supercomputer simulations. Running on 40 million processor hours of UK national supercomputers (that's like five million laptops working together for a whole year!), MEGATRON tracks how gas moves, how starlight travels, and how elements spread through the early universe over billions of years.

The simulations start with pristine gas containing zero heavy elements — just like the universe right after the Big Bang — and follow the first stars as they form, shine, and explode, seeding the cosmos with carbon, oxygen, iron, and all the other elements.

## Connecting two cosmic time machines

MEGATRON bridges two incredible ways astronomers study the early universe:

1. **JWST's time machine** — The James Webb Space Telescope peers back to see young galaxies as they were billions of years ago.
2. **Stellar archaeology** — Ancient stars in our own Milky Way act like fossils, preserving the chemical fingerprints of the first generations of stars that came before them.

By comparing supercomputer simulations with both JWST observations and the chemical makeup of the oldest stars, scientists can finally test their theories about how the first stars formed, lived, and died — and how their explosions created the elements that make our world possible.

## Try this at home

Pour a glass of water and add one drop of food coloring. Watch how it spreads through the water, swirling and mixing until every drop is tinted. That single drop is like the first star — it released new material that slowly mixed into the surrounding gas. Over billions of years, more and more "drops" (stars) added their colors (elements) until eventually, the water was rich enough to form planets, oceans, and life. The universe works the same way — one star's ashes become the ingredients for the next generation of stars, planets, and eventually, you!

## Fun Fact

The carbon atom in your body, the oxygen you breathe, and the iron in your blood were all forged in the hearts of ancient stars that lived and died billions of years ago. Without those first stars, there would be no elements heavier than helium anywhere — no planets, no life, and certainly no peanut butter sandwiches!
