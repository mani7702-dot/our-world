# Why do 3,000 exploding stars tell us dark energy might be changing?

## The universe that won't stop stretching

Imagine the universe is a loaf of raisin bread baking in the oven. As it bakes, every raisin moves a little farther from every other raisin. For most of cosmic history this stretching was slow. But about 5 billion years ago something strange happened — the loaf started stretching **faster and faster**, like someone turned up the heat. The invisible push behind that acceleration is called **dark energy**, and it now makes up nearly 70% of everything in the cosmos.

## A candle that lights up the far sky

How do astronomers measure something so vast? They use special exploding stars called **Type Ia supernovae** — the controlled demolition of white dwarf stars. When a white dwarf tips past a critical mass, runaway fusion tears it apart in a flash so bright it can briefly outshine an entire galaxy. Because these explosions always reach nearly the same peak brightness, astronomers can use them as **'standard candles'** — like identical light bulbs in the sky whose brightness tells us how far away they are.

By watching thousands of these stellar candles, scientists can trace how the universe's stretching has changed over billions of years.

## 30 years of star explosions

An international team of astronomers has now assembled the **largest collection yet**: 2,884 Type Ia supernovae, all stitched together from decades of observations by telescopes like the Dark Energy Survey, Pan-STARRS, and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey. They re-analyzed old data with new methods, making sure the measurements were consistent across different telescopes and cosmic eras.

The result? When they mapped how fast the universe was expanding at different times in the past, the answer didn't quite line up with the simplest version of our standard model — the one that assumes dark energy is exactly the same today as it was billions of years ago. Instead, the data hints that dark energy may have been **a little different** in the past, gently shifting as the universe aged.

## Two clues point the same way

This new finding joins hands with a separate result from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), which also spotted signs that dark energy might change over time. When two completely independent teams see similar surprises, it's a strong hint that our textbook picture of the cosmos may need an update.

## Try this at home

Stretch a balloon with a few dots drawn on it. Inflate it a little and mark where every dot is — that's the universe at one age. Then blow it up more and mark again. The dots (galaxies) all moved apart. If you stretch a little faster each time, that's like dark energy speeding up. Astronomers see that speeding-up happening in the real sky too — they're just trying to figure out *why* it changes.

## Fun Fact

A single Type Ia supernova can briefly shine as brightly as **a trillion stars** — all from the flash of one exploded star. Yet despite that incredible brilliance, these explosions are used as standard candles because they're reliable: they all reach almost the same peak brightness, just like every AA battery in your house puts out the same voltage.
