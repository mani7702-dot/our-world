When they spiral together, they normally produce a brief (less than 2 seconds) short gamma-ray burst (GRB), followed by fading afterglow. The Einstein Probe satellite (launched January 2024) monitors the sky for fast X-ray transients (FFPs) — bright X-ray flashes lasting from minutes to hours that have puzzled astronomers because their origins are unclear. The event EP250704a/GRB 250704B was detected on July 4, 2025, by the SVOM, Insight-HXMT and Einstein Probe satellites. The initial gamma-ray burst lasted only ~0.5 seconds, but Einstein Probe then recorded bright X-ray emission for nearly ten minutes — the longest-lasting prompt X-ray flash ever observed from a neutron star merger. This is remarkable because a normal short GRB's prompt emission switches off almost instantly; a 10-minute X-ray phase means energy was still being injected into the explosion long after the initial merger. The team — led by graduate student Niccolo Passaleva using the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope (VLT) in Chile — used the VLT's X-Shooter instrument to split the burst's light into its individual components, identifying distinct absorption patterns that allowed them to measure the event's redshift (z=0.6610). This shows the explosion happened roughly 6.5 billion years ago, long before our Sun formed. A key diagnostic: the researchers used VLT's FORS2 instrument to search for a bright supernova — the hallmark of a long gamma-ray burst from a collapsing massive star. No supernova appeared, supporting the neutron-star-merger interpretation. The absence of a supernova + the measured distance + the burst's properties point to a neutron star merger, but the long X-ray duration suggests the merger created a rapidly spinning, highly magnetized neutron star (a magnetar) whose magnetic energy was damped into the surroundings over minutes, rather than the merger forming a black hole that shuts off immediately. If confirmed, this provides a new way to find neutron star mergers: instead of looking for brief gamma-ray bursts, astronomers can now search for long X-ray flashes, which are easier to follow up. The paper was published in Science Bulletin. # How Did a 10-Minute X-Ray Flash Reveal the Birth of a Magnetar?

## A cosmic flash that wouldn't switch off

Imagine a firework that normally fizzles out in less than a second — but this one kept glowing for **ten whole minutes**.

That's exactly what happened in deep space. Astronomers watching with the **Einstein Probe** satellite caught a burst of X-rays that lasted about **a thousand times longer** than the typical flash from colliding dead stars. And by doing a careful detective job, they realized they might have witnessed something incredibly rare: the **birth of a magnetar** — a neutron star with a magnetic field a **quadrillion times stronger** than Earth's.

## How do dead stars collide?

A neutron star is what's left when a star much bigger than our Sun runs out of fuel and explodes. The star's core collapses into a ball just a few miles across but heavier than our Sun — so dense that a single spoonful weighs as much as a mountain.

Sometimes, **two neutron stars** are locked in a slow dance, spiraling closer and closer together. When they finally smash, they send ripples through space called **gravitational waves**. Most of the drama lasts less than a heartbeat, flashing gamma rays for a fraction of a second — a signal astronomers call a **short gamma-ray burst**.

## Why did this flash last ten minutes?

The event — named **EP250704a/GRB 250704B** — was spotted on **July 4, 2025**, by three satellites working together: **SVOM**, **Insight-HXMT**, and NASA/ESA's **Einstein Probe**.

The first gamma-ray burst was over in about **half a second**. But then something amazing happened: Einstein Probe kept recording bright X-ray light for nearly **ten minutes**. That's like a firework whose sparkler keeps glowing long after you thought it went out.

The team raced to observe what happened next. Using the **Very Large Telescope** in Chile, graduate student Niccolò Passaleva split the burst's light into a rainbow of colors. The patterns told him how far away the collision happened: about **6.5 billion years** ago, long before our own Sun was born.

Then came a crucial clue: they looked for a **supernova**—the bright explosion that happens when a single massive star dies. **No supernova appeared.** That rules out one possibility and points squarely at two neutron stars colliding.

## The mystery of the long glow

Why did this merger keep glowing for ten minutes? Scientists think the crashing neutron stars may have formed a **brand-new magnetar** — a city-sized object spinning hundreds of times per second, with a magnetic field trillions of times stronger than ours.

Instead of shutting off instantly like most mergers, the newborn magnetar kept spinning and dumping magnetic energy into space, making the X-ray flash last far longer than ever seen before.

If astronomers spot more of these long X-ray flashes, they'll have a brand-new way to find and study neutron star collisions — and maybe catch the moment a magnetar is born.

## Try this at home

Take two identical batteries and roll them toward each other on a smooth table so their ends collide. One battery stops quickly, but if you add a tiny magnet to one, watch how the collision behaves differently. That's a tiny taste of how two dense, magnetized objects behave very differently from plain ones — just like a neutron star merger that creates a magnetar!

## Fun Fact

In 2004, a giant flare from a magnetar **50,000 light-years away** was so powerful that it briefly changed the upper layers of Earth's atmosphere and was detected by satellites built to watch for nuclear explosions. It's the strongest burst of energy from outside our solar system ever recorded reaching Earth — and it still only made a tiny ripple, showing just how far away these objects are.
