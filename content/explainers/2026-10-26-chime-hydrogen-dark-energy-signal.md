# How did a Canadian radio telescope listen to the Universe's first hydrogen singing?

*Report filed at 22:24 IST from the Our World explainers desk.*

## The whisper of hydrogen

Everywhere you look — at the ground, at other people, even at the screen you are reading this on — you are made of stardust. But long before planets and people existed, the Universe was filled with something far simpler: clouds of **hydrogen**, the lightest and most common element in existence. When those clouds clump together under gravity, they collapse into stars, and stars are born.

What if we could *listen* to that hydrogen? Not with our ears, but with a giant radio ear trained on the sky. That is exactly what a Canadian telescope called **CHIME** (say it like "chime") just did for the first time: it detected the faint radio whisper of hydrogen gas from a time when the Universe was less than half its current age — about **nine billion years old**.

## Why hydrogen whispers at radio

Hydrogen atoms have a tiny quirk: when the single proton and electron flip their orientations relative to each other, the atom gives off a whisper of radio wave at a very specific frequency — the famous **21-centimeter line** (or 21-cm line). On Earth we can catch that whisper with big radio dishes.

But here is the catch: that radio signal from nine billion years ago has been stretched. Because the Universe has been expanding all that time, the 21-cm whisper today has been **stretched to longer, lower-frequency radio waves** — redshifted into the range CHIME is built to see. By tuning in to those stretched whispers, CHIME listens to a younger Universe without needing to spot individual galaxies, which becomes impossible that far back.

## A new, cheaper way to chase dark energy

For decades, astronomers measured how the Universe expands by spotting and counting individual galaxies — one at a time, with huge, expensive telescopes. Each galaxy is a single data point in the great expansion story.

CHIME turns that upside down. Instead of counting galaxies, it **sweeps the sky every day** with four giant, 100-metre-long curved mirrors (imagine four playground slides lying on their sides), catching the combined radio hum of all the hydrogen in huge swathes of space at once. That approach lets it map a far bigger volume of the cosmos at a fraction of the cost.

Why does that matter? Because those maps reveal how matter was spread through space nine billion years ago, and from that scientists can reconstruct **how fast the Universe was expanding back then** — a direct probe of **dark energy**, the invisible force thought to be pushing the cosmos apart faster and faster.

## Try this at home

On a still evening, go somewhere dark — far from streetlights. Face away from any bright lights and let your eyes adjust for ten minutes. The faint, hazy band arching across the sky is the **Milky Way** — our own galaxy seen edge-on. That soft glow is made of hundreds of billions of stars (and their hydrogen), and some of that very same hydrogen is what CHIME listens to. The difference: to a radio telescope, the Milky Way is not a glittering path — it is a whispering river of radio waves.

## Fun Fact

CHIME has no moving parts at all. It does not swivel or tilt. Instead, it lets the **Earth itself slowly spin it a whole new half of the sky every 12 hours** — nature's own mounting.

## for-grownups

CHIME, a radio interferometer at the Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory in British Columbia, Canada, demonstrated detection of the cosmological 21-cm hydrogen signal from redshift z ≈ 0.8–2.5 (when the Universe was ~2.5–7 billion years old). The signal — roughly two percent of the Universe's hydrogen in neutral atomic form at that epoch — was extracted from 94 nights of 2019 observations using new data-processing methods that peeled away terrestrial radio interference and instrumental effects. The hydrogen maps trace the large-scale structure of matter, which astronomers use to measure the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) scale as a "standard ruler" for cosmic expansion, thereby probing whether dark energy is constant or evolving. (2nd source: Wikipedia, Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment — confirms CHIME's design, 400–800 MHz band, 21-cm redshifting, and the dark-energy/BAO motivation.)
