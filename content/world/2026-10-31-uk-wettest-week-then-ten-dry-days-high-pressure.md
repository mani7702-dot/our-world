# How Can It Rain for a Whole Week — and Then Stay Dry for Ten Days?

## 1. A Very Wet Week

At the end of October, the UK had its **wettest week in two years**. Two big storms, named **Austen** and **Boelo**, swept across the country one after the other. The Environment Agency recorded an average of **55 millimetres of rain across England** in the seven days to 27 October — with as much as **87 mm** in the South West. That is a lot of water falling from the sky in just one week!

## 2. Then the Weather Flips Over

But here is the surprising part. As November begins, forecasters say the UK could see as many as **10 days in a row without rain**. How can a country go from floods to dryness so quickly?

The answer is a giant "lid" of air called an **area of high pressure**. When high pressure settles over a place, the air inside it slowly sinks downwards instead of rising. Rising air makes clouds and rain — so when the air sinks, the clouds cannot build. The result is calm, settled, mostly dry weather, with only light winds.

## 3. The Stubborn Lid — and the Fog

Weather experts call this a **blocking high**, because it blocks the usual parade of rainy storms from sweeping in from the Atlantic Ocean. A block can sit in one spot for **days or even weeks**, keeping the weather the same day after day. That is why the forecast is so repetitive: dry, calm, and rather chilly, especially at night.

There is one side-effect to watch for — **fog**. On still, clear nights the ground cools quickly, and the air right above it can cool until its tiny water droplets turn into a low cloud near the ground. Some fog patches may be thick and slow to clear in the mornings, making travel tricky until the sun lifts them away.

## 4. Why the Rain Was Welcome

You might think a dry spell sounds boring — but the rain was actually good news. England had a hot, dry summer, and Wales and most of England are still in **drought**. Scientists say it would take many months of above-average rain to refill supplies to normal. So the wet week helped a little, and more rain may return around **mid-November**.

### Try this at home

Make a simple rain gauge! Cut the top off a clear plastic bottle, turn it upside down like a funnel, and tape it into the base. Stand it outside, away from trees, and use a ruler to measure the water after each shower. Keep a chart for a week — you might be surprised how much falls in one storm.

### Fun Fact

Fog is really just a **cloud that forms on the ground**. If you have ever walked through fog, you were literally walking inside a cloud — just like flying through one in an aeroplane!
