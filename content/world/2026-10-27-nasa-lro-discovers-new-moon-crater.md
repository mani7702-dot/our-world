# How did NASA's Moon camera catch a brand-new 728-foot crater that wasn't there before?

Have you ever wondered what happens when a giant space rock smacks into the Moon? Scientists just found the biggest freshly-made Moon crater anyone has ever seen — and it's huge!

## A cosmic surprise in the Moon's eastern edge

NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) has been orbiting the Moon for more than 17 years, taking pictures of nearly the entire surface. One day, scientist Robert Wagner was looking at a big map of the Moon on his computer screen when something jumped out at him. There was a bright spot surrounded by a dark halo — a pattern that meant something on the surface had recently been dug up.

"It was like finding a needle in a haystack — except the needle was made of moon dust and the haystack was the entire Moon," Wagner said.

By comparing older photos with newer ones, he realized a brand-new crater had appeared. It is named **McGetchin** (after a pioneering lunar scientist), and it is 728 feet wide — about the length of two football fields!

## How big was the impact?

The crater is 141 feet deep — deep enough to hold three yellow school buses stacked on top of each other. Scientists think it was made by an asteroid or comet the size of a three- to six-story building that slammed into the Moon sometime between April and May of 2024.

Impacts this big are extremely rare. Scientists estimate that a crash this size might happen on the Moon only about once every hundred years — or even less often!

## The Moon is like a cosmic shooting range

Unlike Earth, the Moon has no atmosphere to burn up incoming space rocks. That means asteroids and meteoroids can hit the surface directly, making new craters all the time. LRO has spotted more than 1,000 new craters so far — but most are much smaller than McGetchin.

The tiniest craters scientists can see are about 30 feet across, and around 140 of those are made each year by rocks the size of a monster truck tire. But even the smallest impacts add up: LRO has recorded about 100,000 changes to the lunar surface over its 17-year mission!

## A surprising cold spot

What's even cooler than the crater itself? The area around it is four miles wide and stays about 16 degrees Fahrenheit colder at night than the surrounding Moon surface. That's because the impact churned up loose lunar soil ("regolith"), which doesn't hold onto heat as well after the sun goes down.

This discovery shows how dramatically a single collision can reshape the Moon — and why scientists will need to watch out for these changes as they plan future human missions to the lunar surface.

## Try this at home

Fill a shallow baking dish with flour, then drop a marble from a few inches above it. Watch how the flour explodes outward and leaves a circular dent — that's just like how an asteroid makes a crater on the Moon! Try different heights and marble sizes to see how crater size changes.

## Fun Fact

The Moon is constantly getting new craters — but because there's no wind or water to wash them away, they stay there forever. If you could see all the impacts that have happened over billions of years, the Moon's surface would look like a cosmic battlefield covered in millions of round pockmarks!
