# 5 Things You Need to Know About the UK's New Spaceplane Project

What if a trip to space were as easy as catching a plane? In Cornwall, England, a new partnership is making that idea feel a little less like science fiction.

## 1. It Is Not Rocket Science — It Flies Like an Aeroplane

The vehicle is called the **Aurora spaceplane**, made by a company named **Dawn Aerospace**. Unlike a big rocket that shoots straight up and falls back down in pieces, the Aurora takes off from a normal runway — just like a passenger aeroplane. It does not need a launchpad.

It is only **5 metres (16 feet) long** and weighs just over 15 kg worth of payload — but it can carry small scientific instruments, cameras, or experiments into space and bring them back the same day.

## 2. How High? Almost to Space

Space begins about **100 km (62 miles)** above us — that invisible line is called the Kármán line. The Aurora is designed to fly higher than that, reaching altitudes of up to 100 km. But here is the catch: it is **suborbital**, meaning it goes up, dips into space for a few minutes, then comes back down. It does **not** stay in orbit like the International Space Station.

Think of it like a very fast, very high roller-coaster that lets your experiment float weightless for a little while before landing back on the runway.

## 3. Twice a Day, Every Day

Here is the amazing part: because the Aurora is reusable and lands like a normal plane, the designers hope it could fly **two times per day**. That means researchers might book a ride tomorrow morning, run their experiment, and fly again in the afternoon — something that used to take months of waiting for a rocket.

## 4. From Cornwall, With Ambition

On **October 27, 2026**, Dawn Aerospace signed an agreement with **Spaceport Cornwall** — the UK's first commercial spaceport, located at Cornwall Airport Newquay. The project is called **Dash UK** (Dawn Aerospace Suborbital Horizontal UK Launch Programme).

The goal? To give UK scientists and companies a fast, routine way to send small payloads — up to **15 kg (33 lbs)** — into the upper atmosphere and near-space for research, communications tests, and technology experiments.

## 5. Why It Matters

Space has always been expensive and slow. A single rocket launch can cost millions and only happens a few times a year. But if spaceplanes like the Aurora make **regular, airline-style flights**, then school experiments, university projects, and small businesses could all afford to test their ideas in space.

It could also help with **atmospheric research** — studying the air high above Earth to learn more about weather, climate, and the atmosphere that protects our planet.

## Try This at Home

**Build a mini spaceplane glider.** Take a piece of stiff paper or thin cardboard. Fold it into a dart shape, add small wings near the middle (like a plane), and tape a paper clip to the nose for weight. Throw it gently — it should glide and dip, just like a spaceplane dipping into and out of space. Adjust the wing angles to make it stable. Now imagine it flying 100 km up — that is the edge of space!

## Fun Fact

The Kármán line — the official boundary of space — is only about **100 km (62 miles)** above sea level. That means space is closer than many people think. From the ground, it looks impossibly far away, but the Aurora spaceplane could cross that line in about the same time it takes to drive to the next town.
