# What did scientists discover beneath Africa that helps explain how the Moon was born?

## A hidden river of fire beneath our feet

Imagine a river of hot rock, thousands of miles deep, slowly churning beneath your feet. That is exactly what scientists have confirmed is happening **deep beneath Africa** — and the story it tells connects our own planet to the Moon!

Far below Africa, there is a colossal upwelling of hot mantle material called the **African Superplume**. It starts incredibly deep — hundreds, maybe thousands, of miles down — and rises upward, pushing and pulling on the continent above. Thanks to more than 12 years of careful GPS measurements and computer models, researchers now believe this hidden "river of fire" is responsible for mysterious sideways movements along the **East African Rift** — the giant crack where Africa is slowly splitting apart.

## Why is Africa literally coming apart?

The East African Rift is like a giant zipper running down the middle of Africa. If you stand on one side and your friend stands on the other, you are drifting slowly away from each other — just a few millimeters per year, but it adds up!

For a long time, scientists thought this was all caused by forces near Earth's surface — like the continent's own weight and density differences. But GPS data showed something extra: parts of the region were also moving **sideways**, parallel to the rift, in a direction that couldn't be explained by simple stretching alone.

That sideways motion, researchers discovered, comes from the **African Superplume** — a vast zone of hot, rising rock deep in Earth's mantle. As this superplume flows northward, it drags the ground above it, creating the puzzling sideways deformation that GPS instruments detect.

## How does this connect to the Moon?

Here is where it gets truly magical. The same kind of rock that is being stretched and shaped deep beneath Africa also covers our nearest celestial neighbor in brilliant white.

That rock is called **anorthosite** — an igneous rock rich in a mineral called plagioclase feldspar. On Earth, anorthosite forms deep underground when molten rock cools very slowly, letting large, shimmering crystals grow. In southern Madagascar, one particular patch of anorthosite — the **Saririaky Massif** — is so bright it is easily visible from space. Astronauts aboard the International Space Station photographed it on August 28, 2026, and it looks like a glowing white island in a sea of darker terrain.

But here is the amazing part: on the **Moon**, anorthosite is the most common rock of all. It makes up about **80% of the Moon's bright, reflective highlands** — the white parts you can see with your own eyes on a clear night. Scientists believe these lunar highlands formed when the early Moon was covered by a global ocean of molten rock called the **magma ocean**. As that ocean cooled, the anorthosite crystals floated to the top, forming the Moon's first crust.

So the same type of rock that is being slowly pulled apart beneath Africa today is the very rock that once floated to the surface of a molten Moon over 4 billion years ago. One helps us understand the other — the African Superplume teaches us how molten rock moves and crystallizes, and that helps us piece together how our Moon grew its bright, ancient face.

## Try this at home

**Make your own magma ocean!** Fill a clear jar halfway with water. Add a teaspoon of sugar and a few drops of food coloring. Shake it up — when it's swirling, that is like a magma ocean. Now let it sit undisturbed. Over time, the sugar (heavier material) will sink and the colored water will settle into layers. That is like how anorthosite crystals eventually sank or floated to form the Moon's early crust. Observe: do different layers form? Which "rock" ends up on top?

## Fun Fact

The Moon is still shrinking! As its deep interior cools, the Moon's crust crumples up — creating cliffs that are taller than Mount Everest. These aren't caused by tectonic plates like on Earth, but by the Moon slowly deflating like a slightly deflated balloon. Scientists estimate the Moon has shrunk by about 150 feet in diameter over the past 100 million years — and it's still happening!
