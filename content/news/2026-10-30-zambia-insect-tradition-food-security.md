## 1. What if the snack in your pantry could help feed the world?

Imagine reaching into a snack bag and pulling out a crunchy, protein-packed treat that costs almost nothing to make, takes up very little space, and barely hurts the planet. That is exactly what people in many parts of the world have been doing for thousands of years.

In Zambia, a new study found that families who still eat insects are part of a global tradition that scientists think could help feed billions of people — without all the cows, chickens, and pigs that take up so much land and water.

The researchers, John Nyawali and Jasper Knight from the University of the Witwatersrand in South Africa, visited 139 rural households in Zambia's Central province. They wanted to know: how many people still eat insects, what kinds do they eat, and does it really help with getting enough food and good nutrition?

## 2. Meet the flying snacks

The study looked at a variety of edible insects that people in Zambia collect and cook. These include:

- **Mopane worms** — not actually worms, but caterpillars that munch on mopane tree leaves
- **Termites** — often dried and sprinkled into porridge or sauces
- **Flying ants** — also called "honeypot ants" in some places, caught when they swarm during the rainy season
- **Grasshoppers and crickets** — sometimes fried up like tiny crunchy snacks

Many of these insects are loaded with protein, healthy fats, iron, and vitamins like B12. In fact, a single handful of dried mopane worms can give you more protein than a chicken egg — and they grow on trees that already exist in the wild!

## 3. Why insects are better for the planet

Here is why scientists are excited about insects as food:

- **Less land**: It takes about 100 times less land to produce a kilo of insect protein than a kilo of beef
- **Less water**: Crickets need about 2,000 times less water than cattle
- **Fewer greenhouse gases**: Insects produce a fraction of the methane that cows release
- **Food from waste**: Many edible insects can be raised on food scraps — turning waste into protein

The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations has identified over 1,900 insect species that are safe and nutritious for humans to eat. Around 3,000 ethnic groups around the world already do — mostly in Africa, Asia, and Latin America.

## 4. The tradition that is fading

But the study found something worrying: younger people in Zambia are eating fewer insects than their grandparents did. In some villages, children grow up thinking bugs are "just for poor people" and would rather eat rice or bread.

This matters because insects are not just tasty snacks — they are also a safety net. When crops fail or money runs low, families who know how to find and cook insects can feed themselves without spending much money.

The good news? In households where adults had more education, insect eating was actually more common — not less. This suggests that if we teach kids that insects are nutritious, sustainable, and good for the planet, they might want to try them.

## Try this at home

Ask a grown-up to help you visit a local nature center or garden. Look for signs of insects that are safe to observe (never eat wild insects unless an expert says it is okay!). Then at home, try this:

1. **Insect spotter**: Draw a picture of five different insects you see in your neighborhood — a ladybug, a grasshopper, a spider, a bee, a caterpillar
2. **Crunchy comparison**: Next time you eat a crunchy snack (like crackers or nuts), think about how an insect like a cricket might taste. It is all about texture!
3. **Backyard bug hotel**: Build a simple bug hotel using a small box, toilet paper tubes, and dry leaves. Watch what insects move in!

## Fun Fact

A single acre of land can produce 2,000 pounds of crickets using very little water — but only 160 pounds of beef! That means if we grew insects instead of cattle, we could feed the same number of people using just 1/12 of the farmland.
