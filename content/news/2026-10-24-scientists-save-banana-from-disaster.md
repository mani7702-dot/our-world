# Can scientists save the banana from disaster before it's too late?

## The yellow fruit that nearly disappeared once before

Bananas are the world's second-most-eaten fruit after tomatoes. Every year, farmers grow about 139 million tonnes of them. But here is a surprising fact: almost every banana you have ever eaten is a clone.

The Cavendish banana — the kind with the bright yellow skin that fills our fruit bowls — cannot make seeds. It grows from offshoots, which means every single Cavendish plant on Earth is almost identical. This worked great for growers who wanted uniform fruit, but it also means a single new disease could sweep through the entire crop like wildfire through a field of dry grass.

This is not just science fiction. It already happened once. In the 1950s, a fungus called Panama disease wiped out the Gros Michel banana — the variety people ate before Cavendish took over. The fruit did not vanish overnight, but it was replaced. Now history could repeat itself, and this time the Cavendish is on the front lines.

## The invisible enemy spreading across continents

The new threat is called Tropical Race 4, or TR4 for short. It is a soil-borne fungus that invades a banana plant's roots and stops water from flowing, causing the leaves to yellow and wilt. There is no cure. Once the soil is infected, it stays infected for decades.

TR4 first appeared in Southeast Asia and has since spread to Australia, Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America. In December 2025, it was reported in Ecuador — the world's largest banana exporter — sending alarm bells ringing through the global farming community. From there it spread to other Andean countries, threatening the livelihoods of millions of farmers and the food security of entire regions.

Scientists are racing against time. If TR4 reaches Central America and North America, the Cavendish banana could face the same fate as the Gros Michel.

## The breeding race for a super-banana

Researchers are fighting back with three different strategies.

First, a team at Queensland University of Technology in Australia, led by Professor James Dale, created a genetically modified Cavendish called QCAV-4. They used a natural bacterium to add a gene from a wild banana that gives resistance to TR4. The result tastes just like the bananas we know, and it has already been approved for sale in Australia and New Zealand.

Second, Dr. Jose De Vega at the Earlham Institute in England is using gene editing. His team sequences the genomes of wild banana varieties to find the "immune genes" that TR4-resistant plants have. Then they use molecular scissors to give the Cavendish those same protective genes — keeping everything that makes a Cavendish a Cavendish.

Third, a collaboration between Chiquita (the world's largest banana company) and KeyGene launched an initiative called Yelloway. Instead of genetic modification, they used non-GM breeding techniques to create a fully fertile Cavendish line named "Yellowdish." Because it can produce seeds and reproduce sexually, cross-breeding becomes possible — opening the door to new banana varieties with natural disease resistance.

## Try this at home

Next time you peel a banana, remember: you are holding a clone. Ask a grown-up to help you plant a banana from a grocery store in a pot of soil and watch what grows. (Cavendish bananas sold in stores won't produce seeds — they need a living plant to fruit.)

You can also look up your favorite fruits and find out which ones are clones. Potatoes, apples, and pineapples are too! That is why scientists worry about disease — when everyone eats the same variety, a new sickness can spread fast.

**Fun fact:** Bananas are berries! In botany, a berry is a fruit that develops from a single flower with one ovary and contains seeds. Bananas, kiwis, and even avocados are all berries — while strawberries are not.

**For grown-ups:** Scientists estimate that if TR4 continues spreading unchecked, it could reduce global banana production by 50% within the next decade, with the Cavendish virtually disappearing from commercial markets within 20 years. The race is on to ensure this beloved fruit survives.
