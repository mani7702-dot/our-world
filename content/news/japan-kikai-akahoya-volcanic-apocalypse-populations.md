# What happened when Japan's biggest volcano blew 7,300 years ago?

## A mountain that sleeps under the sea

Off the southern coast of Japan, hidden beneath the waves, sits one of the country's most dangerous volcanoes — **Kikai Caldera**. About **7,300 years ago**, it exploded with a force almost impossible to imagine.

The eruption was so big scientists give it a rating of **VEI 7** — that is the second-highest level on the volcano danger scale. (For comparison, Mount St. Helens in 1980 was VEI 5.) This blast was a hundred times stronger than that.

## What happened when it blew

The mountain did not just burp lava. It **exploded upward and outward** with incredible violence, sending:

- **Gigantic waves** — a tsunami that raced across the sea, leaving marks more than 300 km away
- **Thick, choking ash** — meters deep, falling like snow across nearby islands
- **Crushing earthquakes** — so strong they made the ground liquefy, turning soil into liquid
- **A brand-new crater** — the caldera, roughly 20 km (12 miles) wide, formed in a single, terrifying day

Imagine waking up to a sky the color of mud, the ground shaking beneath your feet, and giant walls of water coming from the sea. That is what people living near the eruption 7,300 years ago experienced.

## Did everyone die?

Here is where the story gets surprising. For a long time, scientists argued about what happened to the **Jomon people** — the hunter-gatherers who lived in Japan back then.

One theory said it was total disaster — everyone who lived near the volcano vanished completely. Another theory said, eh, people just carried on like nothing happened.

But the truth, new research shows, is **nuanced**:

- **Some communities disappeared** — places closest to the volcano and the thickest ash saw their populations shrink or move away.
- **Some communities actually grew** — people who lived along the northern coast found new ways to thrive. They turned to the sea for food (fish, shellfish, seals), and their numbers went up, not down.
- **Even faraway places felt it** — communities hundreds of kilometers from the eruption saw their growth stall, probably because the ash cloud cooled the climate for a while and made the weather change.

## How did people survive?

The secret is simple: **people adapted**.

Instead of giving up, the Jomon people made new choices. Coastal families caught more fish and shellfish. Inland families may have moved closer to the shore to share in the sea's abundance. New tools, new food sources, and new ways of working together helped them through the hard times.

> **Fun Fact:** The Jomon people were some of the world's earliest master potters. Their name literally means "cord marks" — because they pressed rope into wet clay to decorate their pottery, leaving distinctive patterns that archaeologists still find today.

It is a powerful reminder that even the biggest disasters cannot break human creativity and resilience. The volcano changed the land, but it could not stop people from finding new ways to live.

## Try this at home

Pour a thick layer of flour into a shallow tray. Sprinkle paprika powder on top — that is your "ash." Tilt the tray slowly. Watch how the "ash" flows and piles up just like real volcanic ash. Then imagine: where would *you* move if you lived near here?
