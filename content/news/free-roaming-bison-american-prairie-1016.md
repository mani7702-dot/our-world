On a crisp October morning in central Montana, a census taker does not count people. The American Prairie reserve — the largest private land conservation project in the United States — just announced that its free-roaming bison herd has reached **1,016 animals**, the first time the count has crossed four digits since the project began more than 20 years ago.

That is more than a number. It is proof that a patchwork of ranches, grasslands, and leased public lands stitched together by one nonprofit can bring a keystone species back to life.

## Why bison matter

Bison — often called buffalo — were once so numerous that early settlers described rivers "black with the backs of buffalo." By the late 1800s, hide hunters had reduced their numbers to a few hundred, and the species nearly vanished entirely.

Today, fewer than 10 percent of all bison are managed for conservation rather than ranching. Most live in small, fenced herds — cut off from the ancient migrations that once shaped the entire Great Plains ecosystem. Without roaming bison, the prairie's plants, insects, and soil miss the trampling, fertilizing, and seed-dispersing work those herds once performed. Bison are a **keystone species**: a single herd can maintain the balance of an entire landscape.

## A herd grows, a legal fight sharpens

American Prairie manages 603,657 acres — about 167,000 deeded and 436,000 leased from public lands. Their long-term goal is even bigger: 3.2 million acres of contiguous wild prairie.

The herd's growth has not been without controversy. In May 2026, federal land managers revoked the reserve's grazing permits, ordering the nonprofit to remove its bison from three federal allotments across northeastern Montana. American Prairie sued, arguing the removal would fragment one of the largest remaining conservation herds in the Northern Rockies.

On September 4, 2026, a federal judge in Billings granted a preliminary injunction, temporarily blocking the removal while the case proceeds. The ruling keeps 1,016 bison — including calves born this spring — free to roam the public-private mosaic of grassland.

## A taste of what a full prairie sounds like

Before American Prairie, the only sounds across this stretch of Montana grassland were wind through wire and the occasional pickup truck. Now visitors report hearing something that hadn't been heard here for generations: **the lowing of bison**.

Alan Zerbe, the reserve's chief conservation officer, told reporters that reaching the 1,000-bison milestone is evidence the rewilding experiment is working — not just for bison, but for everything that depends on a living prairie.

"When we see pronghorn using the same trails the bison carved, when we see grizzly bears ranging farther, when we see the soil holding more water — that is the moment a prairie remembers how to be a prairie again," he said.

## Try this at home

- **Build a bison footprint tracker**: trace your foot on cardboard, cut it out, and paint it. Walk through a sandbox or muddy patch to leave "bison tracks" — then guess which direction your giant herd was heading.
- **Count the grass types**: if you live near a park, kneel down and look at the ground. Count how many different plants grow within a square foot. Bison grazing keeps grasslands diverse — the more kinds you find, the healthier the prairie.
- **Read a bison story**: the American Prairie website has photos and stories of individual bison families — it is a real-time rewilding diary anyone can follow.

## Fun Fact

A single adult bison can weigh as much as **two full-size cars** — yet it runs faster than most humans. While their shaggy heads look heavy, bison can hit speeds of up to 35 mph, which is faster than the fastest Olympic sprinter over short distances. No wonder they survived when so many larger Ice Age mammals disappeared.

## For Grown-ups

American Prairie, founded in 2001, seeks to create the largest contiguous tract of native prairie open to the public by acquiring or leasing 3.2 million acres in north-central Montana. As of its October 2026 calf count, the reserve's bison herd stands at 1,016 — a milestone that underscores how privately funded rewilding can scale. The legal battle over federal grazing permits (BLM revoked three allotments in May 2026; a federal judge issued a preliminary injunction on September 4, 2026) highlights ongoing tensions between federal land-use rules and conservation grazing. Ecologically, bison are a keystone species: only ~7-10% of North America's ~500,000 bison are considered genetically pure and managed for conservation rather than livestock production. American Prairie's herd is part of a broader Northern Plains rewilding network that also supports grizzly bears, wolves, and pronghorn migration corridors.
