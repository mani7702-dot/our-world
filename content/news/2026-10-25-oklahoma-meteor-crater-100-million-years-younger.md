# How did scientists discover a meteor hit happened 100 million years later than they thought?

## A crater hiding under Oklahoma soil

Beneath the flat fields of Oklahoma lies a giant secret — a **30-mile-wide crater** carved by a meteorite that crashed into Earth millions of years ago. It is called the **Ames Crater**, and for decades scientists thought they knew exactly when the space rock struck. But new detective work has revealed that the meteor hit **100 million years later** than everyone believed!

## Tiny crystals that tell big stories

For years, researchers had guessed the age of the Ames impact by looking at tiny fossil teeth buried in the crater's rocks. These teeth came from an ancient eel-like creature called a conodont, which lived about **467 million years ago** during a period called the Ordovian. But here is the twist: those teeth were probably **old fossils that had been sitting around for millions of years** before the meteor actually hit. The impact churned up the ground and mixed the ancient teeth into the new impact debris — fooling scientists!

To solve the mystery, a team from the University of Texas at Austin turned to **zircon crystals** — microscopic mineral grains that act like time capsules. Zircon can lock in exact dates using a natural radioactive clock, and it also preserves tiny patterns from the crushing pressure of a meteor impact. By studying these crystals, the scientists discovered the Ames Crater is actually about **370 million years old** — nearly 100 million years younger than previously thought!

## A possible link to a mass extinction

This new date is a huge discovery because it places the Ames impact right around the time of the **Frasnian-Famennian extinction** — one of Earth's biggest mass extinction events, when about two-thirds of all marine species disappeared from the oceans. By knowing the true age of the crater, scientists can now investigate whether meteor impacts helped trigger these ancient extinction events or whether life was already struggling for other reasons.

The team even worked with NASA to image the zircon crystals, checking them for the special damage patterns that prove they were really changed by a meteor crash. It is like finding a fingerprint that proves the crystal witnessed the giant impact!

## Try this at home

Grab a bag of glitter and a shallow pan of flour. Sprinkle a few grains of glitter on top — these are your "fossils." Now drop a marble from high above to make an "impact." The marble creates a crater and churns up flour, mixing your "fossils" deep into the debris. Now try to find the glitter and guess where the marble landed. This shows how difficult it can be to figure out the true history of a place when things get mixed up — and why scientists need special tools like zircon crystals to sort out the real story!

## Fun Fact

The Ames Crater is also an important source of **oil and natural gas**. When a meteor slams into the ground, it can push organic material deep into the Earth's crust, creating the perfect conditions for fossil fuel formation. So the same impact that happened 370 million years ago may be helping power our homes today!

**for-grownups:** The Ames Crater (24 km diameter) in Oklahoma was previously associated with the ~467.5 million-year-old Ordovician Meteor Event based on fossil conodont evidence. New zircon U-Pb dating reveals the impact occurred ~370 million years ago (Late Devonian), placing it near the Frasnian-Famennian boundary mass extinction. The team verified zircon crystals were impact-affected using NASA cathodoluminescence and electron backscatter diffraction imaging, which detect recrystallization patterns from extreme impact pressures. Published in Meteoritics & Planetary Science.
