# What are 16 galaxies teaching us about the violent life of stars and black holes?

## A time machine made of light

Look up on a clear night and you might see a few smudges of light — but those faint smudges are entire **galaxies**, each holding billions of stars. NASA has just released a new gallery of **16 galaxies** that shows just how busy and dramatic the universe really is. Some glow with sweeping spiral arms, others are shaped by violent collisions, and several hide **supermassive black holes** blasting out jets of energy. Thanks to space telescopes that catch light from millions of years ago, every one of these pictures is also a time machine.

## How we took the photos

These aren't ordinary snapshots. Each image **combines data from several of the world's most powerful telescopes**: NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory, the James Webb Space Telescope, the Hubble Space Telescope, the IXPE X-ray polarimeter, the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, and the NuSTAR telescope. On Earth, too, some of the pictures blend in data from ground-based observatories.

Why mix so many telescopes? Because each one sees something different. **Chandra** spots X-rays — the hottest, most violent stuff, like gas heated to millions of degrees by exploding stars or by black holes gobbling down material. **Hubble** and **Webb** add the optical and infrared light that shows where stars are being born and where dust clouds block our view. Put them all together and you get a full, colorful story of what is really happening inside each galaxy.

## Stories written in starlight

The 16 galaxies don't all look alike, and that is the point. Here are a few of the drama-filled chapters:

- **The Cigar Galaxy (Messier 82)** looks like a tiny comet from Earth, but it is actually an *edge-on* galaxy with **twin starburst regions** launching million-degree gas thousands of light-years into space — a superwind powered by rapid star birth.
- **Centaurus A** sports a **supermassive black hole** at its center that fires a single, colossal jet of particles stretching tens of thousands of light-years into space — visible thanks to Chandra's X-ray vision.
- **The Sombrero Galaxy (Messier 104)** shows a galaxy with a graceful dust lane and a hidden black hole, plus a halo of million-degree gas that Chandra reveals glowing around the whole system.
- **Arp 143** is the aftermath of a **galactic collision**, where two galaxies passed through each other, sending ripples of new star formation racing outward like waves in a pond.

## What this tells us about our own home

These distant galaxies are not just beautiful — they are also clues to our own **Milky Way's** past and future. Galaxies grow, collide, and change over billions of years. By studying the gallery, astronomers can guess how spiral galaxies like ours might look in another few billion years. They can also test theories about how supermassive black holes shape the galaxies they live in — whether they calm star birth or ignite it.

The next time someone tells you space is empty and quiet, point them to this gallery. Space is a place of constant motion, where black holes roar, stars explode, and whole galaxies dance together in a slow-motion waltz that has been going on for eons.

## Try this at home

You don't need a telescope to see a galaxy — but you *can* see how combining different kinds of light tells a fuller story. Shine a flashlight through a glass of water and watch how the light bends and spreads. That is a little like how different telescopes each reveal a hidden layer of the same cosmic scene. For a real at-home galaxy, on a very dark night look for the fuzzy smear of the Andromeda Galaxy with just your eyes — that neighbor is 2.5 million light-years away, and what you see now left it before humans existed.
