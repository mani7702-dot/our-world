# How Many Tiny Moons Does Earth Have Right Now?

## The Mystery of Mini-Moons

You probably think Earth has just **one** moon. But actually, Earth almost always has a few secret, tiny moons called **mini-moons**! These are small asteroids — some no bigger than a car — that wander close to Earth and get trapped by our gravity for a little while before escaping back into space.

Dr. Grigori Fedorets from the University of Helsinki says a typical mini-moon stays in orbit around Earth for about **nine months**, circling our planet roughly three times before it breaks free and returns to orbiting the Sun. The largest one at any given time is usually only about **75 centimeters** (30 inches) across!

## Two Mini-Moons We Know About

So far, astronomers have only **confirmed two** mini-moons:
- **2006 RH120** — discovered in 2006
- **2020 CD3** — discovered in 2020 (about 3 meters wide)

There were also two fireballs seen over the Czech Republic in 2013 and Australia in 2016 that scientists traced back to objects that had been orbiting Earth. And in 2024, astronomers detected **2024 PT5**, an 11-meter-wide rock that became a temporary satellite for a few months before fading from view (though it should reappear around mid-November 2026!).

These tiny visitors might actually be **pieces of our own Moon** that were blasted off long ago by asteroid impacts — they're like breadcrumbs from ancient explosions!

## The Rubin Telescope Game-Changer

The new **Vera C. Rubin Observatory** in Chile is set to revolutionize our search. Its massive 8.4-meter telescope and 3.2-gigapixel camera (the largest camera ever built!) will scan the southern sky every few nights for ten years during its Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST).

Fedorets estimates the Rubin telescope should spot a mini-moon about **once every two years** on its own. But with a new computer system analyzing the data in real time, astronomers could boost that to **two or three discoveries per year**!

Finding more mini-moons could open doors for future space missions too. Since these rocks move slowly and are so close to Earth, they could be perfect targets for **low-cost sample-return missions** — bringing pieces of space rocks back to study in labs right here on Earth.

## Try This at Home

Look up at the night sky on a clear evening. The Moon is Earth's biggest familiar companion, but somewhere in the darkness, there might be a tiny rock no bigger than a grapefruit orbiting our planet too — you just can't see it without a powerful telescope!

You can also try tracking near-Earth objects on NASA's ["Send Your Name to the Moon"](https://moon.nasa.gov) website or the [Minor Planet Center](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net) to see the latest asteroid discoveries.

## Fun Fact

The International Astronomical Union officially calls these objects **"minimoons"** (not "mini-moons")! The term was coined because they're like regular moons but tiny — and they're temporary guests, not permanent residents of Earth's cosmic neighborhood.

## For Grown-Ups

Mini-moons are temporary natural satellites of Earth — small asteroids gravitationally captured by our planet's gravity. The concept is confirmed by both Space.com reporting (Dr. Grigori Fedorets, Europlanet Science Congress 2026) and Wikipedia's "Temporary satellite" and "2024 PT5" articles, which document that only two mini-moons (2006 RH120 and 2020 CD3) have been confirmed, with 2024 PT5 being an 11-meter object that briefly orbited Earth. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory, confirmed by Wikipedia, is a joint NSF/DOE project in Chile with an 8.4-meter telescope conducting the Legacy Survey of Space and Time, expected to dramatically increase mini-moon discovery rates.
