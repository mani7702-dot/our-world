# How did scientists figure out the best way to save the Amazon rainforest?

## 1. The great green library that scientists are racing to protect

The Amazon rainforest is so big it makes its own weather. Spanning about 7 million square kilometers — that is roughly the size of the United States — it is home to **10 percent of all known species on Earth**. Towering trees, colorful birds, sneaky jaguars, and countless tiny insects all call this green blanket home.

But every few years, something shifts in the sky. A weather pattern called **El Niño** changes wind currents high above the ocean, the usual rains hold back, and the forest floor turns dry as dust. Fires begin to spread. Loggers move deeper into the canopy. And the question becomes urgent: if we want to save the Amazon, where should we spend our money and effort?

## 2. The experiment that compared every strategy at once

A team of scientists from the UK, Brazil, Sweden, and Australia decided to find out — by running the world's largest comparison of conservation strategies. They focused on a study area covering **more than 3 million hectares** (about 7.4 million acres — twice the size of Yellowstone National Park!) across two regions in northern Brazil called Santarém and Paragominas.

Imagine spreading a giant grid over the landscape. The researchers placed **381 survey transects** — like straight walking paths for scientists — across 38 catchments and 499 rural properties. Along each path, they recorded which trees were growing, which animals were living there, and how much carbon the forest was storing.

They then asked a powerful question: what would this landscape look like today if the communities and governments had used *only* fire prevention? What if they had focused *only* on stopping logging? What if they had tried hard *only* at replanting cleared land?

## 3. The surprise: protecting trees already standing works best

The answer surprised even the researchers. **Preventing damage to forests that already exist** — keeping fires away and stopping illegal logging — delivered the **largest benefits for both biodiversity and carbon storage**. It kept the most birds singing and stored nearly as much carbon as completely stopping deforestation, all while costing less money.

Active restoration — planting new trees on farmland and cleared patches — was still valuable, especially in places where the forest had already disappeared. But on its own, it was the **most expensive strategy and the least effective**.

"The clear message is: we should prioritize protecting the forests we still have," explained Dr. Leonardo Miranda, the lead author. "Restoration is essential where forests have been lost across large areas, but our study shows that investing in new forests makes little sense if, at the same time, we fail to protect existing forests."

The best approach? **Do all three together** — protect standing trees, stop fires and logging, and restore where land is already cleared. When combined, the strategies reinforced each other, delivering even greater gains for wildlife and our climate.

## Try this at home

**Build a paper-canopy forest:** Gather several sheets of green construction paper or recycled paper in different shades. Layer them loosely on a table to make a "forest canopy." Now take a hair-dryer on cool setting and blow across the papers — this is like wind, which can spread fire through dry leaves below. Discuss: how could you protect this mini-forest? (Answer: keep the paper layers thick and overlapping, like a real canopy that shades the ground!)

## Fun Fact

The Amazon's trees are connected underground by an **underground network of fungi** — scientists sometimes call it the "Wood Wide Web." Through this network, trees share nutrients and send warnings to neighboring trees when danger (like drought or disease) approaches, a bit like passing notes in a classroom!

**for-grownups:** A new study published in Science (DOI: 10.1126/science.adx9928) by an international team led by Lancaster University and Brazil's Embrapa analyzed over 3 million hectares across Santarém and Paragominas in the Brazilian state of Pará from 2010 to 2020. By combining high-resolution satellite imagery with 381 field survey transects and models tracking 160 bird species and 426 tree species, the researchers compared three conservation strategies: preventing deforestation, preventing disturbance (fires and selective logging), and active forest restoration on already-cleared land. Their key finding: preventing damage to standing forests delivered the greatest biodiversity benefits and nearly matched deforestation prevention for protecting carbon stocks — and did so at lower cost. Restoration was more expensive and less effective when used alone, but combining all three strategies produced the best overall results. Wikipedia confirms that the Amazon rainforest covers 60% of Brazil and acts as a critical carbon sink, while also noting that protected areas are locations where resource exploitation is limited to preserve natural values.
