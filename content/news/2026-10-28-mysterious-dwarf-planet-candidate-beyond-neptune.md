# 2026 ED138: The mysterious space rock beyond Neptune that could be a new dwarf planet

## A speck of light that made an astronomer gasp

Picture a lonely world so far from the Sun that sunlight there is **900 times weaker** than on Earth. Now picture it silently orbiting in the icy darkness, more than **1.2 billion kilometers** from us — so far that not even Pluto has wandered this far.

That is **2026 ED138**, and it just became astronomy's newest sensation. The powerful **Vera C. Rubin Observatory** in Chile spotted this mysterious object, and initial measurements suggest it could be a brand-new **dwarf planet** — only the sixth one ever confirmed.

## How big is it, and why does it matter?

2026 ED138 is an icy world about **46 astronomical units (AU)** from the Sun. (One AU is the distance from Earth to the Sun — about 150 million km.) That puts it squarely in the realm of **trans-Neptunian Objects (TNOs)**: space rocks that orbit beyond Neptune's path.

But size matters for dwarf planets. Scientists calculate 2026 ED138 is roughly **600 to 1,500 kilometers** across. That is enormous by cosmic standards — and big enough that scientists already expect its own gravity has squeezed it into a **round, ball-like shape**. That roundness is the key rule: a dwarf planet must be round (thanks to its own gravity), orbit the Sun, and have cleared its neighborhood of debris. 2026 ED138 meets the first two.

## Who found it, and how?

**Matthew Holman** — an astronomer at the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian — discovered 2026 ED138 while searching through early Rubin Observatory data. He ran the images through a computer program he developed with his colleague Kevin Napier that scans **billions of detections** looking for anything that moves.

Holman's first reaction? **"One of excited disbelief."** Objects this large and distant are rare, so he immediately double-checked his work. Fellow astronomer Mario Jurić then found **20 more detections** of the same object in other Rubin data, confirming it was real — not a glitch. Together, the observations traced 2026 ED138's orbit over a **ten-year period**.

## A fossil from the solar system's childhood

Why are distant worlds like 2026 ED138 so important? Because they are **fossils** of how our solar system formed.

2026 ED138 is classified as a **"hot classical" TNO**, with an orbit tilted about **25 degrees** from the solar system's flat plane. Scientists believe objects like this one were tossed into their strange orbits early in the solar system's history — possibly by the slow, graceful **migration of giant planets** like Neptune and Jupiter.

Pluto and Eris follow similar tilted orbits, and they tell the same story: the outer solar system was a chaotic place as the planets shuffled outward. Studying 2026 ED138 is like reading a page from that ancient, swirling chapter of cosmic history. The bigger and rounder these objects are, the more clues they carry about what happened long before Earth even existed.

## Try this at home

1. **Spot the scale** — Stand outside at night. Now imagine that every other star you see is actually a Sun. The nearest confirmed dwarf planet beyond Neptune is *so faint* it would need a telescope the size of a small room to see. That is how far away these worlds are!
2. **Make a tilted orbit** — Use a string and pin on a piece of cardboard. Draw a flat circle (the solar system's plane). Now draw another circle tilted at 25 degrees. That is the path 2026 ED138 takes as it journeys around the Sun once every 200+ years.

## Fun Fact

Only **five dwarf planets** were confirmed before 2026 ED138: Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake. If 2026 ED138 gets the official stamp, it would be the **sixth** — and one of the **largest ever found** beyond Neptune.
