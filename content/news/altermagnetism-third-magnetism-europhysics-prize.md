# What if everything we knew about magnets just got a new chapter?

For more than **100 years**, physicists thought there were only **two kinds** of magnetism in the world.

The first is like the magnet on your fridge — all the tiny magnetic arrows point the same way, and the material sticks to the door. Scientists call this **ferromagnetism**.

The second is sneakier — the magnetic arrows point in opposite directions and cancel each other out. Nothing sticks, but the material is still magnetic in its own quiet way. That is **antiferromagnetism**.

Now, three scientists have found a **third kind** that had been hiding in plain sight. They call it **altermagnetism** — and it just won Europe's most prestigious physics prize.

## The prize-winning discovery

The 2026 EPS Europhysics Prize went to **Jairo Sinova** (Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz), **Libor Šmejkal**, and **Tomas Jungwirth** for proving that altermagnets are a real, separate category of magnetism.

For more than a century, every magnet fell into one of two boxes:
- **Ferromagnets** — arrows aligned, material attracts (fridge magnets, compass needles)
- **Antiferromagnets** — arrows paired and canceled, material stays quiet (used inside phone screens and hard drives)

Altermagnets break the rule. Like antiferromagnets, their arrows cancel out — so the material has **no overall magnetism** and won't stick to your fridge. But unlike antiferromagnets, their electrons behave in ways that were thought to belong only to ferromagnets. It is like finding a third primary color hiding behind red and blue.

## Why a "quiet" magnet matters

At first glance, a magnet that does not stick to anything might seem useless. But that is exactly what makes altermagnets special.

Regular ferromagnets are powerful but slow — their magnetism easily gets scrambled by heat or stray fields. Antiferromagnets are fast and stable, but they are hard to control with electricity.

Altermagnets promise the **best of both**: the speed and stability of antiferromagnets, with the electrical control of ferromagnets. That combination could lead to **next-generation computer chips** that are faster, use less energy, and keep working even in strong magnetic fields.

The key is in how the electrons' spins line up inside the material. In altermagnets, the pattern of spins creates a kind of symmetry that lets electrical currents flow with a built-in spin direction — something physicists thought was impossible without a net magnetization.

## From theory to the real world

The breakthrough started as a theory project. Šmejkal, working at Mainz University between 2016 and 2024, teamed up with Sinova's group to explore what would happen if a certain kind of magnetic structure was allowed. Their math showed that a third category should exist — and it should be all around us, just overlooked.

Soon, experimental groups in Europe, North America, and Asia began hunting for altermagnetic materials. They found them. The theory — confirmed by measuring how electricity flows through these crystals — opened an entirely new frontier in materials physics.

> **Fun Fact:** The word "magnet" comes from a Greek island called **Magnesia**, where people once found naturally magnetic stones. Today, we are discovering new kinds of magnetism right here on Earth — and in labs worldwide.

## What comes next

Altermagnetism is still young science, but it is already one of the hottest topics in physics. Researchers are racing to grow pure altermagnetic crystals, design circuits that use their unique spin properties, and build the first devices that could one day replace the silicon chips in our phones and computers.

For now, the discovery is a reminder that even in a science as old as magnetism, there are still surprises waiting beneath the surface — if you know where to look.
