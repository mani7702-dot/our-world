# Did farmers accidentally set off tsunamis in an Italian lake 900 years ago?

## The shaking that woke medieval Italy

On a cold January morning in the year 1117, something enormous rumbled beneath northern Italy. A single earthquake — one of the strongest ever recorded in the region — rippled across the countryside around Verona. Churches shook, walls cracked, and the ground heaved like a rolling sea.

But the real surprise came moments later, when something unexpected happened to the lakes.

## When lakes turned into giant waves

As the earth shook, entire mountainsides cracked and broke loose. Huge sheets of rock, soil, and trees crashed down the slopes and **plunged straight into Lake Garda and Lake Iseo** below. When that much weight slams into water, it doesn't just make a splash — it creates a **wave** that can race across the lake in seconds.

A 12th-century manuscript kept in the Vatican Library recorded the moment: *"The lakes also rose so much that very few of those living in the villages nearby escaped this danger."*

That is the voice of someone who saw the wave with their own eyes. The water didn't just lap at the shore — it **flooded** whole neighborhoods, sweeping away farms and homes that sat too close to the water.

## Why farming made it worse

Here is the surprising part: the landslides were not just natural. For hundreds of years before 1117, people had been clearing forests and farming the steep mountain slopes above the lakes. Removing the trees and digging terraces into the hillsides loosened the soil, making it easier for the earthquake to send everything tumbling down.

Think of it like building a sandcastle too close to the water — when the tide comes in, it washes away more than just sand. The same thing happened here, except the "tide" was a landslide and the "castle" was entire villages by the shore.

## The danger is still there today

The good news? People today know more about how landslides work and can build protections — drains to soak up excess water, barriers to catch falling rock, and early warning systems. But the same forces that created the medieval tsunamis are still at work: earthquakes still shake the region, and heavy rains can loosen soil on farmland cut into hillsides.

## Try this at home

Fill a baking dish with water and gently drop a stone into one end. Watch how the waves travel all the way across. Now imagine doing that with a mountain’s worth of rock — and a lake full of boats and houses along the shore. That's the power of a landslide tsunami, and why geologists watch steep slopes near water so carefully.

## Fun Fact

Lake Garda is Italy's largest lake, and it was carved by a glacier over 5,000 years ago. So when the 1117 earthquake sent waves across it, that water wasn't just moving through a natural lake — it was racing through a canyon that a mile-high glacier had dug when the last ice age was ending.

## for-grown-ups

The 1117 Verona earthquake struck on January 3 at 15:15 GMT with an epicenter between Zevio and Belfiore (45°22′N 11°10′E), magnitude ~6.8, reaching Mercalli intensity IX. Landslides in Val Lagarina dumped debris into both Lake Garda and Lake Iseo, displacing water and producing flood waves documented by a 12th-century Vatican Library manuscript. Italian Wikipedia confirms "esondazione dei laghi di Garda e d'Iseo" (overflow/flooding of Lake Garda and Lake Iseo) as a natural effect of the earthquake.
