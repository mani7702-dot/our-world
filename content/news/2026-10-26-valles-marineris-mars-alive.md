# 1. The Grand Canyon of Mars — On a Giant Scale

Imagine the Grand Canyon on Earth — it is already impressive at 277 miles (446 km) long and over a mile (1.6 km) deep. Now multiply it by **ten** in length and **four** in depth. That is **Valles Marineris**, a canyon system carved into the rusty red surface of Mars.

At **more than 4,000 kilometers (2,500 miles) long**, up to **200 kilometers (120 miles) wide**, and **7 kilometers (23,000 feet) deep**, Valles Marineris is the **largest canyon in the entire Solar System**. If you flew over it in a helicopter, you would need over **six full hours** just to cross it from one end to the other — longer than many movies!

## 2. A Canyon That Keeps Growing

For many years, scientists thought Mars was completely, totally **dead** — geologically speaking. Unlike Earth, Mars has no tectonic plates that slowly drift and crash into each other. Instead, Mars is a "stagnant lid" world, with a single, unmoving rocky shell.

But then something surprising happened. NASA's **InSight** lander — which sat on the Martian surface listening to "marsquakes" — started picking up rumblings from deep underground. In a study published in *Nature Communications*, scientists from Imperial College London and Columbia University discovered that a marsquake called **S0976a** was caused by **rocks slipping along fault lines** deep beneath Valles Marineris.

This means the canyon's faults — cracks in the Martian crust that formed **billions of years ago** — are still **unstable**. They still move. Mars may not have Earth-style plate tectonics, but it is far from frozen in stone.

## 3. How Was the Giant Canyon Made?

Scientists agree that Valles Marineris began as a **rift fault** — a place where the crust pulled apart, creating a long, deep crack. This is similar to how the **East African Rift** on Earth slowly splits the continent in two.

Around 4 billion years ago, the **Tharsis Bulge** — a massive volcanic region on Mars — began to grow. As it piled up with volcanic rock, the weight became too much, and the crust cracked. Over time, wind and (possibly) water erosion widened and deepened those cracks into the enormous canyon system we see today.

Some channels carved into the canyon walls may have been made by **water or carbon dioxide** flowing along the canyon floor, though Mars is much drier now.

## 4. Mars Is Alive — Just Very, Very Slow

The discovery that Valles Marineris is still tectonically active teaches us something important: **planets do not just switch off**. Mars may be cold and dry now, but its interior still shifts and settles. Landslides rumble down its canyon walls. Rocks grind against each other deep underground.

If you stand on the edge of the canyon and wait, you probably will not see anything move — but over millions of years, Valles Marineris is still being carved a little deeper, a little wider, by the slow, patient forces of geology.

## Try this at home

Take a piece of thick cardboard and draw a long, winding line with a marker. Now, slowly tear along the line — you will see it split into a canyon-shaped gap. Now make the cardboard longer and wider (use more sheets) and try again. The bigger your "canyon," the harder it is to tear perfectly straight — just like Mars's giant rift!

## Fun Fact

The canyon is so enormous that if it were on Earth, you could fit the **United States** inside it from north to south. And its name comes from the **Mariner 9** spacecraft, which first spotted it from orbit in 1972 and gave it the name meaning "Mariner Valleys" in Latin.

## for-grown-ups

Valles Marineris on Mars is the largest canyon in the Solar System, stretching over 4,000 km (2,500 mi) long, up to 200 km (120 mi) wide, and up to 7 km (23,000 ft) deep — roughly four times the length of Earth's Grand Canyon and more than twice as deep. Located along the equator of Mars on the east side of the Tharsis region, it was discovered by NASA's Mariner 9 orbiter in 1972. The most widely accepted theory, confirmed by Wikipedia, is that Valles Marineris formed from rift faults (tensional tectonic forces pulling the crust apart), later enlarged by erosion and collapse of the rift walls — similar to how the East African Rift formed on Earth. This process is tied to the formation of the Tharsis Bulge, which grew from volcanic activity and isostatic uplift during Mars's Noachian to Late Hesperian periods (4.1 to 3.0 billion years ago). For decades, scientists believed Mars was geologically dead — a "stagnant lid" planet with no moving tectonic plates (unlike Earth). However, NASA's InSight (Interior Exploration using Seismic Investigations, Geodesy and Heat Transport) lander, which operated from 2018 to 2022, changed that view. In a study published in Nature Communications led by Jung-Hun Song of Imperial College London and colleagues at Columbia University and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, the team analyzed a marsquake designated S0976a that was recorded on August 25, 2021. This was the only marsquake detected in Mars’s western hemisphere, and it originated on the opposite side of the planet from the InSight lander, beneath Valles Marineris. The seismic evidence shows the quake was caused by rocks slipping along fault lines deep within the crust — proving that faults formed billions of years ago are still unstable and can rupture under stress. Wikipedia confirms that landslides in Valles Marineris can be triggered by tectonic activity, releasing seismic waves.
