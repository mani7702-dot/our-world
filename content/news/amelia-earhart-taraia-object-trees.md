On July 2, 1937, Amelia Earhart and her navigator Fred Noonan climbed into a
silver Lockheed Electra airplane and tried to fly the last leg of their trip
around the world — from Lae in Papua New Guinea toward the tiny, empty island
of Howland, near Hawaii. They never made it. Their radio went silent. And just
like that, one of history's greatest exploration mysteries began.

For 90 years, people have wondered: did they crash in the ocean? Or did they
find another way? One clue that excited scientists for a long time was a
shimmering, shiny object spotted on a map in a quiet lagoon on the Pacific
island of Nikumaroro (also called Gardner Island).

## What the shiny object turned out to be

A research team — with help from Purdue University — travelled to that very
lagoon to see the object up close. They had read satellite images from 2020 and
thought the shiny thing floating in the water might be the remains of
Earhart's plane.

But when they got there, the answer was not what they hoped.

"When we looked at the Taraia Object — it was likely a collection of large
trees deposited along the sandspit," said project director Rick Pettigrew,
speaking from the island. "This mystery has been ongoing for 90 years and the
mystery continues."

By the time the team arrived, parts of the object had already broken apart and
washed away. All that remained was a single Y-shaped piece.

## Why the tree theory fits

The trees-dont-make-flying-cars idea sounds simple, but it is actually based on
solid thinking. For one thing, Purdue says the object can be seen in photos
**from as far back as 1938** — just one year after Earhart vanished. If it
were a fresh airplane wreck, it would not have been sitting there the whole
time.

Nikumaroro sits about 400 miles from Howland Island. Some researchers believe
Earhart may have accidentally navigated there, landed on the reef, and spent
time as a castaway. Others think the Pacific Ocean simply moved some driftwood
into just the right shape.

## How the team got there

Getting to the island is an adventure all on its own. The research crew had to
spend five whole days sailing across the Pacific to reach Nikumaroro. Then,
rough seas and strong winds blew in — they had to wait on their ship for
several days before heading ashore. Once on the island, they spent four full
days searching the sandy lagoon.

**For the grown-ups:** The Taraia Object investigation was led by Purdue
University geologists working with independent investigators. The object
appears in aerial/photographic surveys dating to 1938. While the island-landing
theory gained attention from The International Group for the Conservation of
Earhart's Aircraft (TIGHAR), Purdue's analysis concluded the object is
consistent with driftwood rather than aircraft aluminum/alloy. The search for
Earhart's plane continues, but the castaway-on-Nikumaroro theory has lost
traction after years of on-site excavations found no conclusive metal evidence.
Earhart's Lockheed Electra (serial 106) was registered to her and disappeared
with 96 Imperial gallons of gasoline — enough for roughly 2.5 more flight hours.
