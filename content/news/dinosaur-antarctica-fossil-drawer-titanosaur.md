# A dinosaur bone sat in a drawer for 40 years. What it turned out to be surprised everyone

## A fossil with no name tag

Imagine finding a dinosaur in a drawer — the kind of thing you might expect in a museum vault, but not one that scientists walked past for decades without noticing.

That is exactly what happened in Antarctica. In **1985**, a team from the British Antarctic Survey was exploring **James Ross Island**, an icy finger sticking out near the Antarctic Peninsula. They dug up a fossil, sketched it in a field notebook, wrote "vertebra of large reptile" on the label, and tucked it away in the BAS collections.

The fossil was a small, odd-looking bone — about 10 cm wide. It did not look like much. And so it sat, forgotten, for forty years.

## The bone that spoke "dinosaur"

In 2024, Dr Mark Evans, the collections manager at BAS, was reorganising drawers when he spotted the odd vertebra. "It's only when you start thinking 'what's in this drawer', that sometimes you come across something and you think, 'Ah, this looks interesting'," he said.

Evans knew the bone looked suspiciously dinosaur-like. So he called in **Prof Paul Barrett** from the Natural History Museum in London.

Barrett examined the fossil and pointed to two key features: a hollow on one end, and a rounded bump on the other. Together, they formed a **ball-and-socket joint** — a hallmark of sauropod dinosaurs. "As soon as I saw it, I knew what we were dealing with… it was a dead cert we were dealing with a Titanosaur," Barrett said.

A titanosaur was a long-necked, long-tailed plant-eater — and some were the **largest animals** to ever walk the Earth. They lived during the Late Cretaceous, about 80 million years ago.

## Why this find matters

Antarctica has a very patchy fossil record. The continent is covered by thick ice, and most good fossil sites are on islands along the peninsula — like James Ross Island, where the land is exposed.

Finding the *first* dinosaur bone on the continent tells us that these giant plant-eaters once lived there. Eighty million years ago, Antarctica was warmer than today — covered in lush forests where titanosaurs may have roamed, not unlike elephants walking in today's snowy landscape.

The fossil is now being carefully conserved after its long time underwater (the bone was likely preserved in sediment near the shore).

### Try this at home
Look at your own spine in the mirror or feel your back. Each bone has those rounded bump-and-socket shapes too — that is how we know dinosaurs walked upright. You can also visit the Natural History Museum in London to see a real titanosaurs cast!

**Fun Fact:** The largest titanosaurs, like Argentinosaurus, may have weighed as much as 10–15 elephants combined!
