# NASA's Artemis I Revealed a Surprising Radiation Difference Between Earth and Moon

When NASA launched **Artemis I** around the Moon in November 2022, the uncrewed mission carried more than cameras and communication gear. Inside the Orion capsule, two human-shaped mannequins named **Zohar** and **Helga** were packed with experimental radiation detectors — tiny salt crystals designed to record every cosmic ray that passed through them.

The results, now published in the journal *Radiation Measurements*, reveal a surprising contrast between life in Earth's magnetic bubble and the harsher environment of lunar space.

## Full-body exposure near the Moon

Near the Moon, astronauts would absorb radiation **almost evenly across their entire body**. This happens because the dominant radiation there — **galactic cosmic rays** — are ultra-high-energy atomic nuclei from outside our solar system. They are so energetic that they punch straight through a human body, depositing a little bit of energy along their entire path. There is no "skin-deep" dose and "deep-interior" dose — the particles simply pass through everything.

Think of it like a light, misty rain that covers your whole body more evenly, rather than hailstones hitting just one side of a house.

## Uneven hits in Earth orbit

In low Earth orbit, the story is different. The Earth's magnetic field traps energetic protons and electrons in the **Van Allen radiation belts** — invisible doughnuts of swirling particles encircling our planet. While the International Station orbits safely inside the inner belt at about **400 kilometers up**, it is still exposed to trapped proton fluxes that create **uneven, depth-dependent dose distributions**: high near the skin and bone marrow, lower in the torso's interior.

## The difference is smaller than expected

Here is the surprise: even though astronauts near the Moon are exposed to **more total radiation** than those in low Earth orbit, the difference is **not as dramatic as scientists thought it would be**. 

Low-Earth-orbit astronauts still get a significant dose from **Van Allen belt protons** — but those are energetic particles trapped in Earth's magnetic field. Near the Moon, the galactic cosmic rays are individually more energetic and capable of traveling completely through the body, but they are **less numerous**. The sparse but penetrating cosmic rays end up giving a total dose that is only modestly higher than the dense but localized trapped-proton flux of Earth orbit.

This insight is crucial for planning NASA's **Artemis III** and IV missions, which aim to return humans to the lunar surface by 2027. Understanding how radiation distributes inside the human body — and how much total dose to expect — will determine what shielding designs and mission durations are safe for astronauts making the ~27-day journey to the Moon and back.
