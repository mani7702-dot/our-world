Astronomers have just confirmed the **youngest planet** ever found — a world **less than a million years old**. That sounds old, but in space terms it is a newborn. It is still sitting inside the swirling cloud of dust and gas that it was born from, like a chick still in its nest.

The planet is called **Elias 2-24 b**. It is about as heavy as Jupiter, and it circles a young star roughly 450 light-years away from Earth.

## How do you spot a hidden baby planet?

Baby planets are very hard to see. They hide inside thick, dusty disks around their stars, and the star's own glare drowns them out.

So astronomers used a clever trick. They attached a **coronagraph** to the Keck Observatory telescope in Hawaii — a device that blocks a star's blinding light, a bit like holding your hand up to hide the Sun so you can see something faint next to it.

They then checked **archival data** — old observations kept in a NASA-funded store of telescope images from 2018 and 2020. By watching how a tiny point of light moved over time, the team proved it was really a planet **orbiting** the star, and not just a speck of dust or a distant background star.

## A planet that should not exist yet

Here is the puzzle. Planet-formation models say a giant planet needs about **5 million years** to grow. But Elias 2-24 b is less than a million years old — and it sits about **55 times farther** from its star than Earth sits from the Sun, where building a planet should be even slower.

Earlier, the record for a young planet was held by four worlds orbiting the stars PDS 70 and WISPIT 2, all more than 5 million years old. Elias 2-24 b beats them all.

"It's incredible that with modern technology, we are actually able to see planet formation in action," said co-author Lucas Cieza.

## Fun Fact

Astronomers have confirmed nearly **6,000 exoplanets** (planets around other stars), but almost all of them are billions of years old. Catching one while it is still forming is extremely rare — which makes Elias 2-24 b like a snapshot of our own Solar System billions of years ago.

## Try this at home

You can see the same idea that the coronagraph uses. On a sunny day, hold your hand up to block the Sun's glare while you look at something far away. Your hand works like a coronagraph — hiding the bright star so the faint thing nearby becomes visible.
