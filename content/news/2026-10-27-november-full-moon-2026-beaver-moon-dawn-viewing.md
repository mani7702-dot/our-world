# When Can You See November's Full Beaver Moon?

November's full moon is nicknamed the **Beaver Moon** — and this year skywatchers in the United States get to see it at a special time: as a bright "Morning Star" hanging low in the eastern sky before sunrise, not glowing all night long!

## 1. The Moon Turns Full Just Before Dawn

This year's Beaver Moon officially becomes full at **9:53 a.m. EST on November 24, 2026**. That's too late to see on the night of the 24th here in North America — the moon will already be below the western horizon by the time darkness falls! Instead, the best viewing happens on the **predawn morning of November 23**, when the bright, nearly-full moon arcs high across the western sky before setting. Look for it low on the western horizon as the stars begin to fade.

> **Fun detail:** The Beaver Moon will appear slightly egg-yolk orange just before moonset, because Earth's atmosphere scatters away the bluer light — the same reason sunsets look red! This effect is called **Rayleigh scattering**.

## 2. Why Is It Called the Beaver Moon?

The name "Beaver Moon" comes from **Colonial American and Algonquian traditions** (Algonquian languages were once spoken in the New England area). November was the month when beavers were busy gathering and storing food for the long winter ahead, retreating to their cozy wooden lodges and underwater caches.

> **Did you know?** November's full moon names often start with the letter "B": **Beaver** Moon is the most common, but some older lists also call it the **Blood** Moon or the **Dark** Moon — all reflecting the same mid-winter preparations theme.

Other traditional full-moon names include the Harvest Moon (September/October), the Hunter's Moon (October), the Snow Moon (February), and the Flower Moon (May).

## 3. Is the Beaver Moon a "Supermoon"?

Astronomers sometimes call a full moon a **supermoon** when it happens within about 90% of the moon's closest point to Earth (called perigee). This makes it look up to **14% larger** and **30% brighter** than the smallest full moon of the year.

November 2026's Beaver Moon peaks just hours after perigee, so **yes — it will be a supermoon**! That means it will look a bit bigger and brighter than a typical full moon, especially when it's low on the horizon.

## Try This at Home 🏠

Grab a pair of binoculars (or just your eyes!) and step outside around **5:30 a.m. local time on November 23**. Face west and look for a big, orange moon sliding down toward the horizon. See if you can spot any of the eight planets that were visible in late 2026: brilliant Venus and Jupiter will be obvious, and with binoculars you might even spy Saturn or the red dot of Mars.

Want a creative project? Make a **moon-phase journal** — draw or photograph the moon every night for a week around the full moon and notice how the lit portion changes shape. You'll be following the same curiosity that ancient skywatchers used to track the seasons!

## Fun Fact 🌙

The Algonquian peoples named their full moons carefully — each one marked a different sign of the changing year. November's Beaver Moon comes right after October's **Hunter's Moon** and just before December's **Cold Moon**. That means if you watch the moon all winter, you're following a calendar that's **over 400 years old**!

## For Grown-ups

November 2026's full moon (the **Beaver Moon**) reaches peak illumination at 9:53 a.m. EST on Nov. 24 (9:53 UT, per Space.com's Nov 27, 2026 article). The moon is at perigee within hours of peak, qualifying the event as a **supermoon** (Wikipedia: "Full moon" — perigee-syzygy definition, moon within 90% of closest approach). The "Beaver Moon" name traces to Colonial American/Algonquian tradition (source: Wikipedia "Full moon" — Hockett/Haddock attribution of November moon name to Algonquian languages, recorded 1841). The supermoon size/brightness differential of 14%/30% matches the 90%-perigee definition cited by Wikipedia. A full-moon lunar standstill (high lunar standstill) affects the moon's path across the sky and timing of moonrise/set — the 2007 harvest/hunter's moon pair is a documented recent example. Rayleigh scattering (named after 19th-century physicist Lord Rayleigh) explains the orange color of low-horizon moons and sunsets.
