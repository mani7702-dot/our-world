# 1. How a Patch of Rock in Madagascar Looks Just Like the Moon

*Looking back at Earth from the International Space Station, a bright white lens in southern Madagascar stands out — and it should, because it's made of the same kind of rock that covers the Moon.*

## Why does Madagascar have Moon rocks?

If you could stand on the Moon, you would see vast, bright, whitish plains stretching across its surface. Those plains are made of a rock called **anorthosite** — an igneous rock that formed when ancient magma cooled slowly deep underground. Plagioclase feldspar crystals, which are lighter than the surrounding magma, floated to the top and settled into thick layers. Over billions of years, the lighter material was exposed as the Moon's bright highlands.

On Earth, anorthosite is much rarer — it covers only a few spots around the globe, from eastern Canada to southern India. But in southern Madagascar, there is a striking circular feature where anorthosite has pushed up through surrounding rock. It is so bright and reflective that from space it looks uncannily like a piece of the Moon that has landed here.

## A photograph from 250 miles above

In August 2026, an astronaut aboard the International Space Station was flying over the Indian Ocean when they trained a camera on Madagascar's southeast coast. The photo — labeled ISS075-E-85249 — captures a nearly perfect round lens of pale rock cradled in darker, twisted mountains. The rock is called the **Saririaky anorthosite massif**, and it covers about 100 square kilometers (40 square miles).

Scientists estimate the massif formed at least **600 million years ago**, during a time when Earth's southern continents were still locked together as a giant supercontinent called Gondwana. As Africa, India, Madagascar, Australia, and Antarctica squeezed against each other, the intense pressure and heat reshaped the anorthosite, folding and stretching it into the patterns you can see today.

## Earth rocks help us read Moon history

Here on Earth, anorthosite is useful because it is like a time machine for lunar science. Apollo astronauts brought back Moon rocks, but the samples are limited. By studying Earth's anorthosite — especially the well-preserved layers in Montana's Beartooth Mountains, which match lunar composition — scientists learn how the Moon's crust formed from a global "magma ocean" more than 4 billion years ago.

So the next time you look up at the Moon's bright, shiny face, remember: a piece of that same ancient story sits right here on Earth, in the red earth and grey stone of Madagascar, waiting to be seen — even with your own eyes.

## Try this at home

You can see anorthosite on Earth without leaving the ground! The **Beartooth Highway** in Montana (U.S.) passes roadside outcrops of lunar-equivalent anorthosite. Closer to home, look for the *DigitalGlobe* or *Maxar* public satellite images of Madagascar on Google Earth — zoom in to the southern coast and you can spot the bright round massif yourself. Ask a grown-up to help you search the keyword "Saririaky" on the NASA Earth Observatory website for more astronaut photos.

## Fun Fact

Anorthosite is so light in color that it was once used as **ship ballast** in the 1800s because it was lighter than lead! The name comes from the Greek *an-orthos* meaning "not straight" or "irregular," referring to the rock's unusual crystal structure.
