# 1 — What is SpaceX trying to catch?

Picture a gymnast flying through the air and landing, perfectly, in the hands of a teammate waiting below. Now imagine that gymnast weighs over 100 tons and is falling back to Earth from the edge of space. That is the challenge SpaceX faces with its **Starship** megarocket.

Starship is the biggest and most powerful rocket ever built. Stacked on its launch pad, it stands more than **400 feet (122 meters) tall** — taller than a 30-story building lying on its side. It has two parts: the **Super Heavy booster** (the first stage that lifts off) and the **Ship** (the upper stage that keeps flying after the booster peels away). Most rockets drop their boosters into the ocean and call it a day. Starship aims to do something far harder: **catch both pieces** with giant mechanical arms and set them down gently on the very pad they launched from.

## 2 — How does a "chopstick" catch work?

SpaceX's trick is a pair of arms at the top of the launch tower that look a lot like **giant chopsticks** — hence the nickname. As Starship's two stages finish their flights, each one steers itself back toward home base, fires its engines to slow down, and then the chopsticks swing out and **catch the vehicles mid-air**, like a waiter catching two plates at once.

Catching the booster is the easier half — Super Heavy is heavier but comes straight down near the pad. Catching the upper stage (called "Ship") is the real prize: it arrives from much higher up, traveling faster, and must thread itself precisely between the arms. Elon Musk says NASA's Starship team aims to catch Ship on its **15th test flight**, slated for next month. If they pull it off, it will be the **first time any orbital rocket has ever been fully recovered** — top to bottom.

## 3 — Why bother catching rockets at all?

The answer is reuse. Right now, the most expensive part of launching a rocket is the hardware. If the boosters and upper stages crash into the ocean or burn up, SpaceX has to build a brand-new rocket every time. By catching both stages and flying them again — day after day — the company hopes to bring the **cost per launch down dramatically**.

Musk has said catching these vehicles fast enough could open the door to launching Starship **more than 10,000 times per year by 2030**. That kind of reuse is, he says, "a profound technology milestone in the history of civilization" — because fully reusable rockets may be the only way humans can afford to build bases on the Moon and eventually settle Mars.

## 4 — What happened in the last test?

On **September 28, 2026**, Starship's 14th test flight made history by becoming the rocket's **first orbital mission**. The Super Heavy booster made a controlled splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico, and for the very first time the Ship reached orbit and stayed there. It also released a batch of SpaceX's newest **Starlink Version 3** internet satellites — next-generation spacecraft meant to join a constellation that could one day number around 100,000 satellites in low-Earth orbit.

The September flight did not attempt the chopstick catch, so the upcoming Flight 15 will be the real test of catching Ship. SpaceX has already practiced catching the booster three times successfully on earlier flights, but the upper-stage catch has never been tried. With Musk confident it will work — "I'd say it's more likely than not that this succeeds" — millions of people around the world will be watching the tower arms swing open next month.

## Try this at home

Stand a ruler on its end and let it fall — it wobbles before it drops. That wobble is a bit like how a rocket steers itself. Now imagine trying to land that ruler in the hands of a friend who is standing a few feet away. Rocket science is hard! You can model the idea with a cardboard tube (a marker) and two popsicle sticks taped as "arms." Drop the "rocket" from shoulder height and try to catch it — you will quickly understand why catching a 400-foot rocket at 200 miles up is one of the hardest jobs in engineering.

## Fun Fact

The name "Starship" almost did not stick. Early on, SpaceX called its biggest rocket "BFR" — short for "Big Falcon Rocket." The name was later simplified to Starship partly so that it would sound less like a movie prop and more like the serious exploration vehicle it was meant to become.

**for-grown-ups:** SpaceX's Starship Flight 15, targeted for November 2026, would attempt the first-ever catch of both the Super Heavy booster and the upper-stage Ship by the launch tower's "chopstick" arms. Wikipedia independently confirms Starship's tower-catch system and that Flight 14 (Sept 28, 2026) was Starship's first orbital mission and first deployment of Starlink Version 3 satellites (Group 31-1). The catch would mark the first full orbital recovery of any launch vehicle. Reusability remains the foundation of SpaceX's goal to cut launch costs and enable high flight rates for Moon/Mars operations.
