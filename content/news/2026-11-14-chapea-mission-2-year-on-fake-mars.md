Four people stepped outside in Houston, Texas, on 31 October 2026 and saw the sky for the first time in **378 days**. They had not been in space. They had been living in a sealed, 3D-printed home built to feel as much like Mars as a building on Earth can.

It was the end of NASA's second CHAPEA mission — short for Crew Health and Performance Exploration Analog. The idea is simple to say and hard to do: if humans are ever going to live on Mars, someone has to find out what a year of that does to a person, *before* anyone is a hundred million kilometres from help.

## 1. A house with no windows

The home is called Mars Dune Alpha. It is about **158 square metres** — roughly the size of a large apartment — and it was 3D-printed at NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston.

Inside, there were no windows, no sounds of the outside world, and no video calls with family. The crew could only write emails, and even those arrived late.

The four volunteers moved in on 19 October 2025: **Ross Elder** as commander, **Ellen Ellis** as medical officer, **James Spicer** as flight engineer, and **Matthew Montgomery** as science officer. Each had a job, just as a real crew would.

## 2. Their days looked like work on another planet

Nobody sat around waiting. The crew put on mock spacesuits and went on simulated Marswalks on a sandy indoor "surface". They drove a rover and flew a drone by remote control to look at the ground ahead. They fixed things when they broke, kept up a strict exercise plan, and grew their own food.

They also had to be careful with supplies. On Mars you cannot pop to the shop, so the crew practised living with what was already in the cupboard.

## 3. The 22-minute problem

Here is the part that makes Mars genuinely different from the International Space Station. The space station is close enough that a question and an answer can travel almost instantly. Mars is so far away that a radio message takes **up to about 22 minutes** to arrive — each way.

So a question asked at breakfast might be answered 45 minutes later, or not at all if the reply arrives while you are busy. Mission control cannot talk an astronaut through a sudden problem. The crew has to figure it out themselves.

That is why the crew emailed their families instead of calling. It is also why NASA watches *teamwork* as closely as it watches muscles: a crew that cannot solve its own arguments is a crew in trouble.

## 4. Why NASA bothers with a fake Mars

CHAPEA is a rehearsal for real decisions. The data helps engineers work out how much food, water, air and exercise equipment a Mars crew needs — and how much room a person needs before a small shared home starts to feel too small.

NASA says the lessons feed into plans for a base on the Moon and, eventually, trips to Mars. Two weeks of medical checks and interviews with the crew followed the door opening, and scientists are still working through the results.

One small detail shows how long 378 days really is. While the crew was sealed inside, NASA astronaut Jack Hathaway flew a 237-day mission to the space station and came home again — and they were still in the habitat.

## Try this at home: the 22-minute rule

Pick an afternoon and try living with a signal delay. Write your questions on paper instead of asking them out loud, and let each answer wait 22 minutes before it comes back.

You will notice two things fast. First, you start asking better questions, because a slow answer is expensive. Second, you start solving small problems yourself instead of asking. That is exactly the skill a Mars crew has to have — and it is surprisingly hard when the person you want to ask is sitting in the next room.
