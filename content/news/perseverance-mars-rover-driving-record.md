## The rover that goes the distance

Picture this: a car that has to drive on another planet. No roads, no GPS, no roadside assistance — just red dust, sharp rocks, and a thin air that is nearly all carbon dioxide. Now picture that car **beating the longest drive anyone has ever made anywhere in the solar system** — and doing it faster than the old record-holder by almost three to one.

On October 11, 2026, NASA's **Perseverance** rover crossed a milestone: its odometer clicked past **28.10 miles (45.22 kilometers)** on the surface of Mars, becoming the most-traveled vehicle ever built on another world.

The old record belonged to Perseverance's elder sibling, **Opportunity**, a golf-cart-sized rover that spent nearly 15 years (2004–2018) crawling across the same rusty plains. Opportunity's final tally? **28.06 miles (45.16 km)** — a record it held for almost a decade. Perseverance reached it in just **five and a half years**.

## How it thinks its way forward

What helps Perseverance move so much farther, so fast? A clever piece of software called **AutoNav** — short for "autonomous navigation." Here is how it works.

While Perseverance drives, its cameras snap pictures of the ground ahead and build a **3D map of the terrain**, picking out rocks, sand dunes, and slopes that could trip it up. Then it picks the safest route — all on its own, without waiting for commands from millions of miles away on Earth.

NASA engineers call this "thinking while driving." Each time AutoNav scans the landscape, it analyzes roughly **600,000 points** — more than 30,000 times as many as NASA's very first Mars rover, Sojourour, which checked only about 20 points per decision.

The payoff is huge. More than **90%** of Perseverance's journey has been guided by AutoNav, letting the rover cover as much as **1,351 feet (412 meters)** in a single Martian day. By contrast, Opportunity and Spirit — the rovers that came before — relied on their autopilot for only about **5%** of their driving.

## Where it goes next

Perseverance is not just sightseeing. It is heading toward a valley called **"Idubi"**, just beyond the western rim of **Jezero Crater** — a spot scientists picked because it holds what may be some of the **oldest rocks in the solar system**.

Billions of years ago, Jezero was a lake fed by rivers. Water carved a delta — a fan-shaped deposit of sediment that, on Earth, often preserves signs of ancient life. Mars may have had only microbial life, but those tiny organisms — if they ever existed — could have left traces inside those layered sediments. Perseverance is collecting rock cores there, sealed in tubes, to bring home on a future mission.

For Steven Lee, the rover project manager at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, the record drive is more than a number: "It is an honor to be working on Perseverance at a moment when the technology has evolved to the point that drives like these are routine. We've blown well past the original 12.4-mile plan, and with all mobility systems fully operational, we expect Perseverance to keep adding to this record for many years to come."

NASA approved Perseverance's first mission extension in October 2026, keeping the rover at Jezero's rim through approximately the end of 2028.

## From the Moon to the Red Planet

Perseverance's achievement sits atop a long line of off-world driving feats:

| Vehicle | World | Distance | Years active |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perseverance** | Mars | 28.10 mi / 45.22 km | 2021–present |
| Opportunity | Mars | 28.06 mi / 45.16 km | 2004–2018 |
| Lunokhod 2 | Moon | 24.2 mi / 39 km | 1973 |
| Apollo 17 (humans) | Moon | 22.2 mi / 35.7 km | 1972 |
| Curiosity | Mars | 23.65 mi / 38.07 km | 2012–present |
| Sojourner | Mars | 330 ft / 100 m | 1997 |

Perseverance has now passed every one of them — including the only two humans to ever drive on another world (Apollo 17's Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt, who covered 22.2 miles across the Moon's Sea of Tranquility in December 1972).

### Try this at home

Build a simple "rover" that avoids obstacles like AutoNav does. Tape three or four books of different heights onto a balloon (the books are "rocks" on your terrain). Blow the balloon up so it rolls — but angle it so it knocks over the shortest book first, then the next. Now swap the order: can you arrange the books so the balloon rolls smoothly around the tallest one? That is the same puzzle Perseverance solves thousands of times per day, only with rocks instead of books.

**Fun fact:** Because Mars has almost no atmosphere, the speed of sound there is about **10 times slower** than on Earth — roughly 260 m/s instead of 340 m/s. That matters for rovers: sound and communication delays behave differently, and mission controllers have to think about every maneuver with a 12-minute radio lag each way.
