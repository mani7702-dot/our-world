Right now, somewhere above your head, machines are quietly helping millions of people find their way. On 30 October 2026, India added a new one to that team.

At 23:51 India time, a rocket called **GSLV-F18** roared off the launch pad at the Satish Dhawan Space Centre and carried a satellite named **NVS-03** into space. Everything went to plan: the rocket placed the satellite into its planned orbit, and the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) announced the success soon after.

## India's own version of GPS

Most of us call satellite navigation "GPS," but GPS is just one system — the American one. India has been building its own since 2013. It is called **NavIC**, short for *Navigation with Indian Constellation*.

NavIC is smaller than GPS. Instead of covering the whole planet, it looks after India and a ring of about **1,500 kilometres** around it. That is still a huge area — and it means India doesn't have to rely on anyone else's satellites to know where its trains, ships, phones and delivery trucks are.

## Why one more satellite matters

Here is the surprising part: a navigation system needs at least **four** working satellites overhead at once to figure out a position. Before this launch, NavIC had only three fully working satellites. So sending up NVS-03 was a big deal — it edges the constellation back toward full health.

NVS-03 is the third of a newer, smarter generation. It carries an **atomic clock**, a clock so steady it barely drifts, and it can send a new type of signal that ordinary phone chips already understand. That means one day your phone might quietly tune in to NavIC without you even noticing.

## How a satellite finds *you*

Satellites don't actually see you. Instead, each one broadcasts a time signal — "the time is now exactly this." Your phone listens for several of these signals and measures how long each one took to arrive. Because radio waves travel at a fixed speed, a slightly longer wait means a slightly farther satellite. Combine a few of those distances and — like drawing circles that cross at one point — your phone works out where it must be.

## Try this at home

Stand in a room and clap once. Now have a friend stand a few metres away with eyes closed and guess how far you are, using only how long the clap took to reach them. That tiny delay is exactly the trick satellites use — just with clocks that count billionths of a second.

## Fun fact

The name **NavIC** is a clever double meaning: it stands for *Navigation with Indian Constellation*, but it also sounds like "navik" — the Hindi word for a sailor or navigator.
