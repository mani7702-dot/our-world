# 10 Things You Need to Know About India's Chandrayaan-1, the Moon Mission That Found Water Ice

## India's Giant Leap to the Moon

On October 21, 2008, the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) took its very first step into deep space. With a powerful roar, a Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle — called PSLV-XL — lifted Chandrayaan-1 (which means "Moon craft" in Sanskrit) off the launch pad at the Satish Dhawan Space Center. For India, this was the country's first ever mission to another world.

But Chandrayaan-1 was not just one spacecraft — it carried a **Moon Impact Probe**, a small bullet-shaped lander that would later zoom down to the lunar surface.

## What Chandrayaan-1 Carried

The mission packed **11 scientific instruments** from around the world, including an important camera from NASA called the **Moon Mineralogy Mapper**, or M3. This colorful eye in the sky was designed to map the moon's surface minerals from orbit. Other instruments searched for water, measured gravity, and studied how the sun's wind affects the moon.

## The Hunt for Hidden Water

For decades, scientists wondered: did the moon have water? Most believed it was bone-dry. But in 2009, M3 spotted something amazing — signs of **water molecules in the moon's polar craters**, where sunlight never reaches. This discovery changed everything we thought we knew about the moon and showed that our nearest neighbor holds secrets we are still uncovering today.

## A Short but Brilliant Mission

Chandrayaan-1 dropped its Moon Impact Probe on November 14, 2008, crashing it near the south pole's Shackleton crater. After that spectacular finish, the main spacecraft kept orbiting, beaming data back to Earth. But on August 28, 2009 — just over a year later — contact was lost. ISRO declared the mission over. Though it ended earlier than planned, those 312 days were packed with discoveries.

## Why It Matters

India became only the **fifth** country to reach the moon, joining the Soviet Union, the United States, Japan, and Europe. Chandrayaan-1 proved that a young space program could explore the solar system and make world-changing discoveries. Its water-ice finding also laid the groundwork for future missions — including plans to one day send humans to live and work on the moon.

## Try this at home

On a clear night, look up and find the moon with your eyes or through a small telescope (ask an adult first). Notice the bright, flat areas called maria (Latin for "seas") — ancient lava plains. Imagine that somewhere near the moon's poles, in shadowed craters that never see sunlight, there is ice waiting to be discovered. You can also draw your own "Chandrayaan" using construction paper: make a silver orb for the moon, a tiny spacecraft, and a rocket blasting off from a paper launchpad!

## Fun Fact

Chandrayaan-1 carried **not one, but two** moon impactors! After the Moon Impact Probe finished its job, the spacecraft's own engine was used to perform a controlled impact, letting scientists study the moon's surface composition by watching what dust and rock were kicked up.

**for-grownups:** In the shadowed floors of the moon's polar craters, temperatures stay below -200°C year-round — perfect for holding ice. M3's detection of an absorption feature at 3 micrometres confirmed water molecules on the lunar surface, a finding later confirmed by multiple missions including NASA's LCROSS and LRO.
