# Why Do Some Sea Turtles Skip the Grand Migration and Stay Home?

## 1. The great turtle road trip

Sea turtles are the ocean's ultimate travelers. Using Earth's magnetic field like an invisible map, they navigate across thousands of miles of open water. A single green sea turtle might swim from a seagrass bed in the Caribbean all the way to nesting beaches in the Indo-Pacific — and then do it again, year after year.

Scientists have long studied these epic journeys, tracking turtles with satellite tags and learning how they find their way home across entire oceans. But what if some turtles decided to skip the road trip entirely?

## 2. Homebodies in the Galápagos

In a surprising discovery, a team of researchers led by Dr. John W. Rowe from Alma College found that green sea turtles living around the Galápagos Islands are happy to stay close to home. The study, published in *Frontiers in Amphibian and Reptile Science*, tracked nine adult turtles for up to 418 days each.

These weren't lazy turtles — they were satellite-tagged and watched closely. But here's the amazing part: **none of them ever left the Galápagos Marine Reserve.**

The Galápagos is home to two distinct types of green sea turtle. There are **yellow-morph** turtles, which are usually found across the tropical western Pacific, and **black-morph** turtles, which live in the eastern Pacific near the Americas. The Galápagos is the only place on Earth where both types share the same waters — like a turtle neighborhood where two different cultures meet.

## 3. Staying close, moving between islands

Even though these turtles didn't travel across the ocean, they still explored their local neighborhood. Some swam as far as 25 kilometers (about 15 miles) from shore, and a few island-hopped between different Galápagos islands.

Five of the nine turtles spent most of their time near just one island. Some moved between Isabela and San Cristóbal, while one adventurous turtle named Ainoa visited Santa Cruz and then Isabela. The turtles tended to island-hop only during the cooler months — from April through October — as if they were following the best weather and food conditions.

Each turtle used roughly 1,498 square kilometers (about 580 square miles) of ocean as their personal home range. That's like having a backyard the size of a small city!

## 4. Why don't they migrate like other turtles?

The answer is still a mystery — but the researchers have a few ideas. Female yellow-morph turtles don't lay eggs on Galápagos beaches (they save that for nesting beaches in the western Pacific), so they're expected to eventually make the long journey. But the study only lasted a few years, so longer migrations at other times might just not have been caught on camera.

## Try this at home

**Track your own neighborhood.** Pick a spot in your backyard, park, or even your street. Keep a "territory map" for a week — draw or mark everywhere you or your pet go. Is it a big area or small? Do you visit some spots more than others? Just like the Galápagos turtles, you have your own favorite places!

**Make a turtle migration game.** Draw a map of your house or classroom. Place a "turtle" (a small toy or drawing) at one spot and a "nesting beach" at another. Use a string or ruler to show the path the turtle would swim. Now make some turtles that stay close to home — how does their path look different?

## Fun Fact

Green sea turtles get their name from the usually green fat found beneath their shells — not from the color of their shell! Their shells can be olive, black, or even marbled with patterns. And despite being herbivores as adults, baby green sea turtles start life eating tiny animals and only gradually switch to a seaweed and seagrass diet as they grow up.
