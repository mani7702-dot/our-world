# Why did scientists build a lab in the Lake District's misty hills?

Tucked away in the Lake District of northwest England, where mist rolls over green hills and peat bogs soak up rain like a giant sponge, scientists have just opened a brand-new field laboratory.

The lab sits at **RSPB Wild Haweswater** — a nature reserve in the valley of **Mardale, Cumbria**. It is not a lab full of test tubes and microscopes. Instead, it is a base camp for researchers, students, and conservationists who want to study the **upland landscapes** around them: the peat bogs, the tiny mountain rainforests, and the moorlands that stretch across the hills.

## Why these misty hills matter

The Lake District is famous for its beauty, but its biggest secret is hidden underground.

Under the soft, spongy ground lie **peat bogs** — wetlands where dead plants never fully rot because the soil stays waterlogged. Over thousands of years, this half-decayed plant matter builds up into thick layers of peat.

These peatlands are Earth's **greatest natural carbon store**, packing away more carbon than all the world's forests combined. They also act as giant sponges: when rain falls, the peat soaks it up slowly, releasing clean water into rivers instead of letting it rush downstream and cause floods.

## The lab gives science a home

Before this lab, scientists studying the Lake District often worked from universities far away. Now they can stay right in the valley.

The lab was built with funding from the **Arcadia Fund** — a charitable trust that has given over $1.3 billion to nature and culture projects worldwide. The Arcadia Fund focuses on protecting nature and making information free for everyone.

Dr. Mo Verhoeven, a conservation scientist at **Cumbria Connect**, explained that the region's uplands contain "distinctive environments, including **peatlands and rainforest**, which play a key role in **storing carbon** and managing water."

The new lab will help researchers study how these ecosystems respond to climate change, how to restore damaged areas, and how to train the next generation of environmental scientists.

## Try this at home

- Fill a glass with soil and pour water on top. The water soaks in slowly through the soil — just like peat soaking up rain in the hills.
- Walk in a park after rain. Do you ever see small puddles disappearing into grassy ground? That is the Earth soaking up water the same way the Lake District does.

## Fun Fact

The Lake District was Britain's first national park, created in 1951. Its highest mountain, **Scafell Pike**, is the roof of England at 978 meters (3,209 feet). The valley where the new lab sits — Haweswater — was flooded in 1935 when a dam was built to bring water to Manchester. Today, instead of storing water for people, it helps store carbon for the whole planet.
