Ask a table-tennis player to load a shot with spin, and you will see something odd: the ball curves through the air as if an invisible hand pushed it sideways. That famous trick has a name — the **Magnus effect**. Now scientists have found its close cousin in a place you would never expect: inside a beam of laser light.

## A curve ball in mid-air

When a ball spins as it flies, it drags a thin layer of air around with it. One side of the ball speeds the air up while the other side slows it down, and that difference creates a gentle sideways push. It is why a curling free-kick bends around a wall of defenders and why a spinning table-tennis ball swerves and dips in flight.

## One atom as a tiny sensor

A team at the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI) in Switzerland, working with ETH Zurich, wondered whether light could pull off a similar trick. They trapped a single **calcium ion** — an electrically charged atom — and held it almost perfectly still using electromagnetic fields. Then they swept a tightly focused laser beam across it.

You would expect the ion to react most strongly right at the beam's brightest centre. Instead, the point of strongest interaction sat slightly to one side. The light had given the ion a sideways nudge — an "optical Magnus effect". The trapped ion acted like a tiny sensor that could feel out the shape of the light, measuring a shift of just a few hundred nanometres. (A nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre.)

## A surprise hiding in the light

The team found something unexpected. The size of the sideways shift depends only on the **wavelength** of the light — essentially its colour — and not on how tightly the beam is squeezed. Their results appeared in *Physical Review Letters*. Scientists at the University of Amsterdam had predicted this effect with maths several years earlier; this experiment is the first time anyone has actually seen it.

## Why it matters for quantum computers

Quantum computers that use trapped ions steer their quantum bits, or qubits, with carefully aimed laser pulses. If a laser nudges an ion slightly off-centre, that tiny error could build up. Knowing about the effect helps researchers aim their lasers more accurately. Better still, the gentle forces it creates might one day be used to link qubits together, allowing more complex calculations.

**Try this at home:** Dip a table-tennis ball in water and spin it across a smooth table top. The wet trail curls sideways instead of running straight — proof that a spinning ball drags whatever surrounds it along for the ride.

**Fun Fact:** A single nanometre is about 100,000 times thinner than a sheet of paper — and the sideways nudge these scientists measured was only a few hundred of them.
