# How are zoos and sanctuaries protecting the tiny singing apes called gibbons?

High in the rainforest trees of Southeast Asia, there are some of the happiest singers in the animal kingdom. They are not birds — they are **gibbons**, the tiny "lesser apes" that can weigh as little as a house cat. On **International Gibbon Day**, held every October 24th, conservationists share the urgent work to keep these elegant, brachiating apes safe from harm.

## Who Are These Tree Swingers?

Gibbons are tailless apes found in the tropical forests of countries like India, Indonesia, and Vietnam. There are **20 different species** of gibbon — all of them threatened by people. Their arms are longer than their legs, and they use them to swing gracefully from branch to branch — a movement called **brachiation** — that lets them fly through the treetops at speeds up to 34 miles per hour.

The reason gibbons move so fast is that they sing! Morning choirs of gibbon calls echo through the forest as pairs announce their territory to the whole valley. Their voices sound like something from a fairy tale — soft, musical, and almost human.

## The Pet Trade Danger

Gibbons are small, gentle, and quiet in captivity, which makes them targets for the illegal exotic pet trade. A hunter once brought a tiny, weeks-old gibbon to a village in Borneo, telling a local family it was a "monkey." They quickly discovered it was something special — but baby gibbons belong with their families in the wild, not in homes.

When people buy or adopt pet gibbons, the babies are torn from their mothers, and the mothers are often killed. These traumatized young apes grow up unable to survive in the wild. That is why sanctuaries exist — to care for rescued gibbons and teach them how to fend for themselves.

## How We Can Help

Conservationists in countries like Malaysia and India now work with rescued gibbons, releasing them back into protected forests when possible. Anti-poaching patrols guard the trees where these singing apes live. The most important protection is keeping their forest homes intact — because without healthy canopies, the tiny acrobats have nowhere to swing.

## Try this at home

Next time you visit a zoo, look for gibbons in the ape house. Watch how they move — they are the only animals that swing with both arms at the same time, like a living swing set. You can also help by choosing palm-oil products that are certified sustainable, since palm plantations are a major threat to gibbon forests.

## Fun Fact

Baby gibbons stay with their parents for several years — much longer than most mammals — because they need to learn the complex songs and the safest routes through the treetop highways of their territory.
