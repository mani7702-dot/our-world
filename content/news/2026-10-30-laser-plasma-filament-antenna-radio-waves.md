# 1. Can a Laser Beam Turn Into a Radio Antenna? Scientists Just Made One That Looks Like a Lightsaber

Imagine a radio antenna you could not touch — one made from a glowing beam of light-fired air, floating in mid-air and changing shape with the twist of a dial. Engineers at **North Carolina State University** have just built exactly that. Their new device uses a laser to create a thin column of **plasma**, and for the first time ever, that plasma column has been used to **transmit radio waves**.

And yes — it really does look like a lightsaber.

## 2. What Exactly Is a Plasma Antenna?

Start with a laser. The researchers fire it through the air at a carefully chosen power and width. That energy strips electrons away from air atoms and molecules, turning a thin thread of air into **plasma** — often called the fourth state of matter, alongside solid, liquid and gas.

Because plasma is full of electrically charged particles, it can push and pull on electromagnetic fields in ways ordinary air cannot. The laser-made thread, called a **plasma filament**, ends up with electrons along its edges — much like the outer skin of a metal wire. That gives it everything it needs to behave like an antenna.

## 3. How Do You Plug It In Without Touching It?

Here is the tricky part. You cannot simply clip a wire onto a beam of plasma — metal touching it would distort it. So the team invented a clever workaround: a **metal ring** that wraps around the filament without ever touching it. Acting like a tiny capacitor, the ring receives a radio signal from a generator and creates an electromagnetic field. That field gently interacts with the plasma, making it broadcast radio waves on its own.

In tests using a **30 MHz** radio signal, adding the plasma filament boosted the received signal strength by **2.5 times** — proof the invisible "lightsaber" was really transmitting.

## 4. Why This Could Matter in Space

Antennas usually depend on their length to pick up certain frequencies, and changing that length means moving mechanical parts — which is hard for a satellite. But with a plasma antenna, the laser controls everything. Adjust the laser, and you adjust the filament's **length** and **angle**, letting the antenna sweep many frequencies or aim its signal without any moving pieces.

The researchers call it "the first step, but... a big step." Spacecraft, radar and satellites could one day use antennas made of light.

## Try this at home

Antennas work better when they match the size of the wave they are catching. With an adult, stretch a piece of aluminium foil into a long strip and hold it near a radio while someone tunes the station. Now crumple the foil into a small ball and try again — notice how the signal gets weaker. Bigger or smaller antenna shapes catch different waves, which is exactly the problem the plasma antenna solves by changing shape on demand.

## Fun Fact

Plasma is the most common state of matter in the *universe* — it makes up stars, lightning bolts and even the glowing gas in neon signs. The plasma antenna in this experiment was a tiny, temporary version of the same stuff that lights up the Sun!
