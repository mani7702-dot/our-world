Some butterflies are hard to miss. In the rainforests of Central and South America, one of the first butterflies a visitor meets is a stripy brown-and-grey insect called *Colobura*.

It is everywhere: from the Andes down through the Amazon, across Central America and out into the Caribbean islands. It has been in the notebooks of scientists since 1758, when Carl Linnaeus gave it a name.

For two hundred years and more, nearly everyone agreed on one thing: *Colobura* was a single species. It is not. It is three, and they have been sitting in plain sight the whole time.

## The clue was a caterpillar with no spots

The first crack came in 2001, when Keith Willmott looked again at something an entomologist had noticed back in 1852. The caterpillars of these butterflies are velvety black, covered in yellow spines, and dotted with tiny cream markings: teardrops on some, rings on others.

That old naturalist, Jan Sepp, thought the difference meant two species. Everyone else called it variation. Willmott checked properly and found Sepp was right. There was a second species, *Colobura annulata*. Everyone assumed the puzzle was finished.

It was not. Twenty-five years later, a colleague in Venezuela found a group of hungry *Colobura* caterpillars stripping the leaves off a young *Cecropia* tree. They had the black body and the yellow spines. They had no cream markings at all. Nothing like either known species.

## A colour you cannot see

The adult butterflies were even harder to tell apart. Their wings are almost identical, with only a sliver of difference in one grey stripe on the underside.

Then doctoral student Anisha Sapkota noticed a faint violet shimmer on some museum specimens. Human eyes can barely catch it, but a spectrometer can. She measured the wings and found three slightly different shades of ultraviolet: 376 nanometres, 370 nanometres, and 344 nanometres.

That matters because many butterflies can see ultraviolet. A wing that looks identical to us might look completely different to a butterfly hunting for a partner.

## Three kinds of DNA, three answers

The team pulled DNA from museum specimens collected across the tropics, and from a decades-long survey of Costa Rican butterflies and moths. They read short stretches of genetic code, then whole mitochondrial and nuclear genomes.

Three separate lineages came back. Each matched the caterpillar markings, the wing stripe, and the UV shade. The new species was named *Colobura cryptica*, from the word for hidden. The strangest part is where they live. All three fly in the same forests, at the same time of year, sometimes on the same afternoon. Yet the nuclear DNA shows no sign that they have ever mixed.

## Try this at home

You can see a hidden colour too. With a grown-up, shine an ultraviolet torch on a white flower, a banknote, and a glass of tonic water in a dim room. Banknotes carry security marks that only show up under UV, and tonic water glows blue. Some flower patterns exist for the same reason the butterfly ones do: to be seen by eyes that are not ours.

## One mystery is still open

Nobody knows yet how the three butterflies keep themselves separate. Maybe they recognise each other by those UV shades. Maybe they live in slightly different parts of the same forest. Finding out means going into the rainforest and watching, which is exactly what the team says comes next.
