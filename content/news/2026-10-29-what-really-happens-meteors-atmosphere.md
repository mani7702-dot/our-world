# What really happens when meteors enter the atmosphere?

## When a shooting star is more than just a sparkle

On quiet nights, you might see a quick streak of light zip across the sky — a "shooting star." It looks like a tiny piece of rock simply burning up in a flash. But scientists have discovered that meteors go through something far more dramatic: **seven distinct phases** as they tumble through our atmosphere, each with its own physical process that determines how bright they shine and whether any fragments ever reach the ground.

## Phase 1: The shock wave that comes first

When a space rock hits the atmosphere at tens of kilometers per second, it doesn't just gently heat up. It slams into air molecules so fast that a **shock wave** forms ahead of it. Both the rock's surface and the surrounding gas heat up and begin to glow. This first glow is the streak of light we call a meteor. The faster the rock travels, the more energy it carries — and the brighter the show begins.

## Phase 2: Brightening in the dense air

As the meteor plunges deeper, it enters increasingly dense air. Its brightness intensifies. Some meteors also flicker with repeating changes in brightness, which can tell scientists how quickly the rock is spinning. Among the fastest-spinning objects ever recorded, a complete rotation took just half a second to five seconds.

## Phase 3: From meteor to fireball

By Phase 3, the object has become much brighter — so bright that astronomers call it a **fireball**. Now, instead of simply burning away, the intense airflow starts to **melt** the rock. The surface turns to molten droplets that get stripped away and continue vaporizing after breaking free. Melting alone can remove up to 40 percent of the original rock's mass before the object even reaches its brightest point.

## Phases 4 and 5: The split that decides our fate

At about 60 kilometers (40 miles) above Earth, melting reaches a steady balance — Phase 4. But as the fireball dives into even denser air, rising pressure begins to **fracture** the rock. This marks Phase 5: pieces start separating from the main body, producing repeated bursts of light.

One surprising discovery: rocks start breaking apart **much earlier** than scientists expected. They begin fragmenting when the air pressure on their leading face reaches only about **one-fifth** of the strength measured in meteorites that we later find on the ground. Cracks from previous collisions in space, plus the heating during entry, weaken the rocks enough to explain why they shatter so early.

## Why the pieces land where they do

When rocks break into fragments, the shape of the surviving core matters. If its rear section stays intact, the main body creates a pocket of **reduced pressure** behind it — like a calm wake behind a boat. Smaller pieces get caught in that wake and follow along, which helps scientists predict where meteorites might land.

## Try this at home

On a dark night (no clouds, no streetlights), find a spot away from bright lights and look up for at least 10 minutes. You are now doing what astronomers do — watching for meteors. No special equipment needed! Just remember: every shooting star you see was once a piece of an asteroid, a comet, or even a piece of another planet or moon, traveling through space before its fiery journey through our sky.

## Fun Fact

The word "meteor" comes from the Greek word for "high-lying," because these space rocks burn up high in our atmosphere — usually between 80 and 120 kilometers above Earth. Only the biggest pieces ever make it all the way down as meteorites, and most of those come from asteroids in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.
