Imagine reading a history book where a huge block of pages in the middle has been torn out. You get the early chapters, and then the story jumps far ahead.

Earth's rocks have a gap just like that. Geologists call it the Great Unconformity.

## Rocks are a stack of stories

Most of the time, rock layers pile up like a stack of sandwiches. The oldest layers settle at the bottom, and newer layers land on top. Read the stack from bottom to top and you read Earth's history in order.

But in many places the stack does not line up. In North America, northern Europe and northern China, rocks about 540 million years old sit directly on top of much older rock, sometimes granite more than a billion years old.

Everything in between is missing. Scientists first spotted the gap in the late 1800s, in the walls of the Grand Canyon, and argued about it for about 150 years.

## A slow lift, then a long sink

A study published on 4 November 2026 in the journal Nature offers an answer.

Two scientists at the University of Wollongong in Australia, Santosh Dhungana and Nicolas Flament, built six computer models of Earth's mantle, the hot slow-moving rock deep under the crust we live on.

Every model showed the same thing. Three ancient continent-sized blocks rose slowly: Laurentia (the old core of North America), Baltica (the old core of northern Europe) and North China. They lifted from about a billion years ago until about 540 million years ago.

Then they sank again. As they sank, seas and sediment covered the worn-down surface and sealed the gap in place.

## What does ice have to do with it?

Here is where the story gets icy. Between about 720 and 635 million years ago, Earth went through the Snowball Earth period, when huge ice sheets reached far toward the equator.

The models show the three blocks were standing higher than their surroundings at exactly that time. Higher ground means the ice could reach it and grind it away. Think of a glacier as the world's slowest sander: it scrapes rock down and carries the pieces off.

That is the heart of the new idea. The missing pages were not lost by chance. The land was lifted up, the ice wore it down, and later the sea buried the smoothed surface.

## Why it matters

The rocks that vanished did not disappear for nothing. Erosion breaks rock into grains and dissolved minerals, and rivers and ice carry those toward the sea.

Some scientists think that rush of material may have fed the oceans just before the Cambrian explosion, the burst of new life around 530 million years ago when animals first built hard shells. That link is still debated, but it is a big reason geologists care about a gap.

## Try this at home

You can make your own unconformity in a clear jar.

1. Spoon in a layer of sand and press it flat. That is your old rock.
2. Add a layer of gravel. That is the missing time.
3. Scrape the top with a spoon to wear it down, the way ice would.
4. Pour in muddy water and let it settle into a smooth new layer.

Look at the jar from the side. The smooth layer sitting on the scraped one is a tiny version of the puzzle scientists just solved.
