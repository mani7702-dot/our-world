Imagine dropping a pencil from a tall building and catching it - gently, by the tip - with two fingers. Now imagine the pencil is **124 metres tall** (about 40 storeys), it has just flown around the whole planet, and it is falling fast enough to glow.

That is roughly what happened in Texas on **Saturday, 14 November 2026**, when the company SpaceX caught the falling top half of its giant **Starship** rocket in mid-air.

## 1 - A rocket with two halves

Starship is the biggest rocket ever built. It has two parts stacked on top of each other:

- the **Super Heavy booster** at the bottom, which does the heavy lifting at liftoff;
- the **Ship** on top, which is the part that actually goes into space.

The whole thing launched on **Friday evening, 13 November**, from SpaceX's Starbase site at Boca Chica, Texas.

SpaceX does not want to throw its rockets away after one flight, the way older rockets were thrown away. It wants to catch them and use them again - more like an aeroplane than a firework. So the launch tower is fitted with two huge mechanical arms. Everyone calls them the **chopsticks**.

## 2 - Twenty-two and a half hours later

The Ship did its job in space first. It released **26 small satellites** that will help beam internet down to Earth, then circled our planet **14 times**.

The whole trip lasted **22 and a half hours**.

Then came the hard part. Falling back through the air, the Ship used its engines to flip itself upright and steer towards the tower. The chopsticks swung shut around it, and the Ship hung there, caught.

It was the **first time anyone has ever caught the upper stage of a rocket** as it came back from orbit. The tower had caught the lower booster three times before, but never the top.

## 3 - The other half did not make it

The flight was supposed to catch **both** halves.

It did not. The Super Heavy booster was meant to come back to the tower about six and a half minutes after liftoff, but it triggered an automatic abort instead. It was running low on fuel, so it turned away and came down over the sea, where it burst into flames.

So the score for the day was one catch out of two. Engineers will look at what went wrong with the booster before the next try.

## 4 - Why catching matters

A rocket is mostly a very expensive machine that carries fuel that is used up in minutes. If you can catch the machine and refuel it, you stop building a brand-new one for every flight - and that is what makes space travel cheaper.

Catching the Ship is the harder half, because it comes back from orbit at enormous speed. Now that it has worked once, SpaceX will want to do it again, and again, until it becomes ordinary.

## Try this at home

You can practise a "soft catch" with a rolled-up sock and two wooden spoons.

Hold the spoons upright, a hand-width apart, like a pair of chopsticks. Ask someone to drop the sock from about a metre above. Try to close the spoons around it without squashing it - the sock should stop, not bounce out.

It is much harder than it looks. Real engineers spend years teaching computers to do this, because a rocket cannot be squashed.

**for-grownups:** SpaceX's Starship Flight 15 lifted off from Starbase, Boca Chica, Texas, on 13 November 2026 at 16:47 local time (19:47 GMT). The Super Heavy booster's return-to-launch-site catch was aborted a few minutes after liftoff following a low-propellant condition; the vehicle diverted to a controlled Gulf of Mexico splashdown and was destroyed. The Ship upper stage (Ship 42) deployed 26 Starlink satellites, completed 14 orbits over roughly 22.5 hours, and was recovered by the launch tower's "chopstick" arms at approximately 16:20 EST (21:20 GMT) on 14 November - the first recovery of an orbital Starship upper stage, and the first time a launch vehicle's upper stage has been caught at the pad. Super Heavy had previously been caught on three occasions. The milestone matters for the economics of full reuse: recovering both stages at the launch site removes the need to build a new vehicle per flight and is a prerequisite for the high flight rates the programme targets. Caveats: this is still a test campaign, the booster was lost, and full rapid reuse has not yet been demonstrated; the vehicle is 124 m tall overall.
