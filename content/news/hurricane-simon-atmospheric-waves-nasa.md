# What do waves above a hurricane look like? NASA snapped ripples dancing through the sky

## The storm below

In October 2026, Hurricane Simon grew into a **Category 4 storm** — that means wind speeds hit **250 kilometers (155 miles) per hour**. Simon raced across the Pacific Ocean, passing very close to Mexico's Pacific coast near the towns of Manzanillo and Puerto Vallarta. Even though the storm stayed just offshore, people on the ground still felt its power: howling wind, sheets of rain, and the ocean getting restless.

## The waves above the storm

While Simon churned below, something even stranger was happening **high, high above** the clouds.

NASA satellites took pictures from space, and they caught something you can hardly believe: **waves rippling through the sky**. These are not water waves — they are waves made of *air*. Scientists call them *atmospheric gravity waves*, and when a storm like Simon pumps energy upward, it can shake the sky like throwing a stone into a pond.

You see, a hurricane is made of towering columns of warm, wet air rising very fast. When that air shoots up and hits the thin atmosphere far above Earth, it gives the sky a push. The push travels out in ripples, like the rings you see when you skip a stone across water.

## How did NASA *see* air ripples?

The ripples are glowing faintly — so faint you have to know where to look. That faint glow is called **airglow**, made when invisible gases high in the sky bump into each other and tiny sparks of light come out. It is always there on dark nights, but it is so dim your eyes can barely notice it.

NASA's special cameras — the VIIRS instruments on the Suomi NPP and NOAA-21 satellites — are sensitive enough to spot it. On the night Simon passed by, the Moon was barely lighting up the sky, so the glowing ripples showed up **extra clearly**. The photo looks like delicate, wavy bands of light floating far above the storm, spreading outward like a giant, ghostly spiderweb.

## Why should you care?

Storms and the high sky are connected more than we used to think. Those invisible waves can change how the whole upper atmosphere moves and shivers. By watching them, scientists learn how Earth's weather and space weather dance together — and how big storms like hurricanes reach all the way up into the sky.

So next time you see a ripple in a pond, remember: the same shape is sometimes floating 100 kilometers above a hurricane, glowing quietly in the dark, far above the clouds.

### Try this at home

Blow gently across the surface of a bowl of water and watch the ripples spread. That is the same shape Simon's invisible sky-waves made — except the real ones travel through air, not water, and only NASA's night-vision cameras in space can catch them glowing.
