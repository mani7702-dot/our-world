# How Two Brown Bears Traveled 3,000 Miles from Russian Cages to an Irish Forest

## 1 — What were the bears' lives like before the rescue?

Before their rescue, Bu and Tyapa were two brown bears living in very different kinds of hardship. Bu had spent years forced to perform in a circus, moving on command for crowds who came to watch. Tyapa had never performed at all — instead, he was trapped in a square cage measuring just 2.5 meters by 2.5 meters. The cage had no roof, leaving him exposed to scorching summer heat and freezing winter cold. There was no proper water dish; he could only drink from a hose. In fact, rescuers later discovered that he had been raised to be killed for a taxidermy display.

Both bears were severely malnourished when they were finally freed. Blood tests showed their kidneys were struggling from long-term dehydration, and they showed clear signs of psychological trauma from years of confinement.

## 2 — How did the rescue span multiple countries and conflicts?

Wild Ireland founder Killian McLaughlin first heard about Bu and Tyapa in 2021 and made a promise: he would bring them to safety in Ireland. But the journey was anything but simple.

When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, the original transport route through Ukraine became impossible. A new plan through the Middle East was drawn up — but that route was blocked too, in February 2026, when escalating conflict in the region closed airspace and disrupted flights.

In the end, over 100 people pitched in: volunteers, veterinarians, lawyers, and animal transport experts working across Russia, the United Arab Emirates, and Ireland. Dima and Yana Kim, who run a tiger sanctuary near St. Petersburg, drove 36 hours to Dagestan, cut the bears free from their rusted steel cages, sedated them, and loaded them into secure crates for the trip back to Tiger House, where the bears received medical care and quarantine.

After months of planning, the bears finally traveled from Moscow via Abu Dhabi to Dublin, arriving at Wild Ireland on **3 August 2026**.

## 3 — How are they doing now in Ireland?

At Wild Ireland in Burnfoot, County Donegal, the bears have a very different life. Their enclosure is a forest of trees, streams, and grass — the exact opposite of the tiny cage they knew before.

Chloe McElhatton, a zoologist who now works as part of the bear team at Wild Ireland, explained that one of their favorite enrichment activities is hiding food around the habitat. "We encourage them to search and forage for it themselves," she said. "This promotes their natural behaviors and helps them become more comfortable in that new open space."

She has watched their confidence grow day by day. "Over time, we've seen great changes in both of them — physically and mentally."

Visitors are thrilled too. Liam, a tourist from France, called the bears "very big and very impressive" and said they were his favorite animals at the sanctuary. Regular visitor Chris Roberts, who has been coming since Wild Ireland opened in 2019, said seeing Bu and Tyapa "doing so well, especially considering their backstory and the awful conditions they were once kept in" made his day.

Since arriving at the sanctuary, both bears have gained weight, their coats have improved, and follow-up blood tests show their kidney function has returned to normal. Wildlife experts explain that for bears who have suffered trauma, the first step of recovery is simple: get them back out into the forest, get their paws into the soil and on the grass, and let them start exploring.

## Try this at home

**Build bear habitats:** Use cardboard boxes, blankets, and toy food to create your own bear rescue center. Hide "food" in different spots and encourage a stuffed animal to "forage" — bears in the wild spend hours each day searching for plants, insects, and fish.

**Learn about bear diets:** Brown bears are actually 90% vegetarian! They love berries, roots, nuts, and even honey. Next time you visit a grocery store, look for these foods and talk about how bears find them in the wild.

## Fun Fact

Brown bears can run faster than humans — up to 35 km/h (22 mph)! Despite their size, they are incredibly fast sprinters when they need to be. But they also hibernate for up to 100 days in the winter, living off stored fat without eating, drinking, or going to the bathroom.

## For parents and teachers

This story is a real example of international wildlife rescue. Wild Ireland (founded in 2019 in County Donegal, Ireland) is a legitimate sanctuary that rescues animals from harmful situations. The rescue of Bu and Tyapa involved cooperation between animal welfare organizations in Russia, the UAE, and Ireland — showing how people across the world can work together to help animals in need. Brown bears (Ursus arctos) are classified as "Least Concern" globally by the IUCN, but some local populations are threatened, which is why rescue and rehabilitation efforts matter. - *time: 11:50 IST*
