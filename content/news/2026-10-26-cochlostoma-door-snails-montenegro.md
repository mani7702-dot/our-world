# How did scientists discover snails with built-in doors?

## A tiny door that slams shut

Imagine if you could close a door behind you with the push of a button — no hands needed. That's exactly what some very small snails can do! Deep in the mountains of Montenegro, scientists found two brand-new kinds of land snails that carry a **natural door** built right into their bodies.

## What's the door for?

The door is called an **operculum** (say: op-er-KOO-lum). It's a flat, oval plate made of chalky material that sits on the snail's soft body, right behind its shell. When the snail feels scared or dry, it simply pulls its head and tentacles inside its shell and swings the operculum shut — sealing the opening like a tiny trap door.

This trick helps the snail save water and stay safe. It can hide from predators, ride out dry spells, and even survive being accidentally knocked out of its home during a hike.

## Meet the two new neighbors

Both snails belong to a group called **Cochlostoma** — a family of snails famous for their door-like operculums. They're tiny, about the size of a grain of rice (6 millimeters long), with cone-shaped shells that spiral like a miniature lighthouse.

- **Cochlostoma komarnica** — named after **Komarnica Canyon**, a dramatic gorge in northern Montenegro that scientists are racing to study before it might be flooded by a planned hydropower dam.
- **Cochlostoma dobrido** — named after the **Dobri Do Plateau**, a high meadow area inside Durmitor National Park.

## How were they found?

You might think finding new animals requires expensive labs and fancy equipment — but these snails were discovered by **everyday people** working side by side with scientists.

From 2018 to 2025, a project called **Taxon Expeditions** invited volunteers, students, and local conservationists to explore the rugged mountains of Durmitor National Park and Komarnica Canyon. As they climbed over limestone rocks and peered into crevices, they collected tiny shells and live snails.

Back at the field lab, the team examined each find carefully. At first, the snails looked like ordinary Cochlostoma — but when they **dissected** them (a careful scientific examination) and ran **DNA tests**, the results showed something exciting: these were two species that had never been described by science before.

## Why does it matter?

Europe might feel like a well-explored place, but these discoveries prove there are still **secrets hiding in plain sight**. The limestone cliffs and canyons of the Balkans are home to dozens of snail species found nowhere else on Earth — and many are still waiting to be discovered.

The race to find them is urgent. Komarnica Canyon, where one of the new snails lives, faces threats from a proposed dam that could flood its unique habitats. By documenting what lives there now, scientists hope to build the case for protecting these wild places before they disappear.

## Try this at home

You don't need to travel to Montenegro to spot snails with doors! Go outside after a rain, lift a damp leaf or log (and put it back gently), and look closely. Some garden snails carry a thin, translucent operculum. With a magnifying glass, you can see how they seal themselves in. Remember: observe, don't collect — these tiny neighbors are doing important jobs in your garden by recycling dead plants and returning nutrients to the soil.

## Fun fact

The word **operculum** comes from Latin, meaning "little lid." Snails aren't the only animals with this trick — some turtles, fish, and even certain crabs have built-in doors too!

## For grown-ups + kids to ponder

The operculum of a Cochlostoma snail is made of **calcium carbonate** — the same mineral that makes seashells and bird eggs strong. As the snail grows, it adds new layers to both its shell and its operculum, almost like building a tiny stone house one brick at a time.
