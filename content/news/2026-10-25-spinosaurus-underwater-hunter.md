# How did Spinosaurus become a part-time fish hunter in the ancient seas?

## A dinosaur that went back to the water

For millions of years, animals made the incredible journey from the ocean onto land. But evolution does not always move in one direction. Many creatures later decided to dive back in! A new study from Yale University is revealing how this "second trip underwater" happened again and again in the history of life.

At the center of the story is **Spinosaurus** — a giant dinosaur whose name means "spine lizard." It lived about 113 to 94 million years ago in what is now northern Africa. Unlike the plant-eating, land-based giants like Brachiosaurus, Spinosaurus had a long, narrow snout like a crocodile and grew up to 14 meters (46 feet) long — about as long as a school bus. But here is the big question: did it wade in shallow water like a heron, or did it actively swim and hunt underwater like a seal?

## Teaching a computer to read fossils

Scientists have argued about Spinosaurus's lifestyle for decades because fossils rarely tell the whole story. Bones can suggest different things, and the fossil record is often incomplete. But Caleb Gordon, a researcher at Yale, found a clever new way to find answers.

He built **machine-learning models** — the same kind of AI used in computers — and trained them on living animals where we already know how aquatic they are. By feeding in over 11,000 fossil measurements, photos, and CT scans, the computer learned to connect bone shapes with lifestyle habits. When shown Spinosaurus fossils, the models consistently pointed to a highly aquatic lifestyle.

To double-check, Gordon even used a technique from **World War II** — a statistical method originally designed to tell whether a blip on radar was an enemy plane or a friendly aircraft. He adapted it to ask: "How likely is it that these ancient bones belonged to an animal built for swimming?" The answer was strong — Spinosaurus was built to spend most of its life underwater.

## Other comeback kids of the ancient seas

Spinosaurus was not the only animal in the study to make a splashy return. The team also looked at **mesosaurs** — small reptiles that lived 290 to 274 million years ago. Some scientists thought these creatures were fully aquatic because they gave birth to live young in the water. But the new analysis suggests mesosaurs still needed land, much like modern platypuses that swim in rivers but nest on shore.

Overall, the researchers found that the earliest marine reptiles (those that lived before the dinosaurs) always returned to land to lay eggs or rest. None became truly ocean-bound like modern whales. The secret was in the **front limbs** — animals built for full aquatic life evolved flippers with specific proportions, while semi-aquatic ones kept limbs that worked on land too.

These findings help us understand one of evolution's greatest patterns: the repeated dance between land and sea. And Spinosaurus — the duck-billed, sail-backed hunter of ancient rivers — may have been one of the most committed swimmers of all.

## Fun Fact

Spinosaurus's sail-like back bones grew up to 1.65 meters (5.4 feet) long — taller than a full-grown person is tall! Scientists think the sail may have helped the dinosaur control its body temperature or show off to other dinosaurs, much like a peacock's tail.

**for-grownups:** The original study, "Quantifying the transition to fully aquatic life in vertebrates using machine learning and a WWII-era statistical framework," was led by Caleb Gordon and Bhart-Anjan Bhullar at Yale University and published in *Current Biology*. Additional co-authors include Lisa Freisem (Yale) and Christopher Griffin (Princeton). Funding came from the Yale Institute for Biospheric Studies, the IUCN Crocodile Specialist Group, and the National Science Foundation. The paper analyzed over 11,000 fossil measurements across dozens of institutions worldwide, including the Yale Peabody Museum.
