# Can a private telescope find alien worlds? Lazuli aims to try

## 1. A new telescope with a big mirror

Soon, a brand-new space telescope called **Lazuli** will look out at the stars. It is special because it is the **world's first private space telescope** — not run by a government space agency like NASA.

Lazuli is being funded by **Schmidt Sciences**, a research organization started by Eric Schmidt (the former boss of Google) and his wife Wendy. Scientists announced the plan in early 2026.

Here is what makes Lazuli impressive: its **mirror is 3 meters wide** — that is bigger than the famous Hubble Space Telescope, which has a 2.4-meter mirror! With a bigger mirror, Lazuli can see dimmer and more distant things.

## 2. The hunt for worlds around other stars

Lazuli will carry a special tool called the **ExtraSolar Coronagraph**, or **ESC**. This instrument does something amazing: it blocks the blinding light of distant stars so astronomers can see the faint planets orbiting them.

So far, almost every exoplanet (a planet around another star) discovered has been found indirectly — by watching its star wobble or seeing a planet pass in front of its star. But Lazuli will try to **take actual pictures** of alien worlds by capturing the faint **reflected starlight** that bounces off them. It is like seeing a firefly next to a searchlight!

Researchers picked **27 targets** — all giant planets like Jupiter — for Lazuli's first big observing project. An international team led by scientists from the University of Michigan and the University of Arizona chose worlds that orbit stars of different ages and temperatures, giving us a fuller picture of how planetary systems grow.

## 3. Teamwork with other telescopes

One of the best parts? Lazuli will work **hand-in-hand** with other big telescopes. Some of its 27 target planets can also be studied by NASA's **Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope** and the upcoming **European Extremely Large Telescope** (ELT). When multiple telescopes look at the same world, scientists can learn much more — just like teamwork!

Lazuli is scheduled to launch in **2028 or 2029**. Until then, astronomers are busy planning which of those 27 alien worlds to photograph first.

## Try this at home

Find a flashlight and a small ball (like a tennis ball). In a dark room, shine the flashlight on the ball — the lit side is like the dayside of a planet, and the dark side is night. Now try to see the ball's faint glow while staring straight at the bright flashlight beam. That's how hard telescopes work to spot faint planets next to bright stars!

## Fun Fact

Hubble's 2.4-meter mirror has been seeing the universe since 1990 — but it was never designed to take direct pictures of exoplanets. Lazuli's bigger mirror plus its special coronagraph means it could be the first telescope to routinely photograph Jupiter-like worlds around Sun-like stars in visible light.
