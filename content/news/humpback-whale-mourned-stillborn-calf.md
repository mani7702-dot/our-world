# When a humpback whale mother stayed beside her stillborn calf — what it tells us about whale feelings?

## A silent vigil in the ocean

Imagine floating in the vast blue, your heart heavy with a loss you cannot name. That is what a humpback whale mother may have felt when, in 2025, she was seen beside her stillborn calf off the southern coast of **Gold Coast, Australia**. While cameras rolled, she stayed close for hours — and possibly for days — keeping watch over her tiny calf in the deep blue water.

This is one of the rarest things anyone has ever seen. Whale watchers and scientists rarely get to witness how these giants of the sea respond to such a moment. But this time, they did — and it is teaching us something profound about whale hearts.

## Why this is so special

**Humpback whales** are giants of the ocean — they grow as long as **17 meters** (that is almost as long as five cars parked bumper to bumper!) and can weigh **40 metric tons** (about the same as seven elephants). Every winter, these gentle giants swim thousands of miles to warm, calm waters to give birth, because their babies need safety to grow strong.

When a mother humpback has a calf, she stays by its side. She guides it through the water, teaches it to dive and breathe, and protects it from danger. Scientists have watched mothers and calves travel together for months, even years.

But watching a mother stay beside a **stillborn** calf — one that never got to take its first breath — is something completely different. It is quiet. It is tender. And it may be the closest thing we have seen to a whale saying goodbye.

## What the scientists saw

Dr. Olaf Meynecke from **Griffith University's Whales and Climate Research Program** was one of the researchers who studied this moment. He explained that while whale mothers have been seen lifting dead calves to the surface, this humpback mother showed something different: she stayed close underwater, keeping her body beside her calf's side for hours — and maybe even days.

> *"She remained close to the calf, maintaining eye contact and positioning herself next to her calf over several hours and maybe even days,"* Dr. Meynecke said. *"These observed behaviors were consistent with care-giving or nurturing actions and post-mortem attentive responses documented in socially complex mammals."*

An "escort whale" — a male that swims nearby — was also present, making the scene even more unusual. Sea World Foundation Rescue Boat Captain Andrew Mulville, who witnessed it firsthand, said: *"At first sight, I thought it was just two whales resting on the surface, but their behavior was unusual."*

## What whale brains can tell us

Here is something amazing: humpback whales have **spindle cells** in their brains — the same special brain cells that humans have, which scientists believe help us think about what others might be feeling. These cells are found in animals with very complex social lives: elephants, great apes, and dolphins.

When a whale mother protects her baby from danger — like steering it away from orca whales that might attack — she is showing deep care. When she stays beside her stillborn calf, she may be showing something even deeper: something like love.

This does not mean whales feel exactly the way we do. But it does mean they have strong family bonds, and they grieve when those bonds are broken — just like we do.

## Why documenting this matters

Because moments like these are so rare, every careful observation helps scientists learn more. Dr. Meynecke says we need to keep watching, keep noting, and keep learning: *"This case study highlights the need for continued systematic documentation of rare neonatal mortality events to better understand the cognitive, emotional, and evolutionary significance of post-mortem behavior in large whales."*

What does this tell us? That the ocean is full of quiet, powerful feelings — and sometimes, the biggest creatures in the sea are also the ones with the biggest hearts.

## Try this at home

- **Whale-watching journal:** If you ever visit the coast, keep a journal of the birds and sea life you see. Draw a family of whales with a mother and calf. Ask yourself: what do you think the mother whale is feeling when she protects her baby?
- **Compassion experiment:** Watch people and pets at a park. Draw what a dog's body language looks like when it is happy, scared, or protective of its puppy (if you see one). Just like whale mothers, animal parents have many ways to show love.
- **Deep thinking:** Next time you feel sad about losing something small — like a toy or a shell you found at the beach — remember that even the biggest whales have big feelings too. It is okay to care deeply.

## Fun Fact

Humpback whales migrate **up to 16,000 kilometers** (10,000 miles) each year — that is like swimming from New York to Sydney and back, twice! They make this incredible journey from cold, food-rich feeding grounds near the poles to warm, shallow breeding grounds near the equator, all to have their babies in safety.

### for-grownups

*Published in Discover Animals. The observation was made off the southern Gold Coast, Australia, in 2025. Humpback whales (Megaptera novaeangliae) are baleen whales that can reach 14–17 meters in length and weigh up to 40 metric tons. Their brains contain spindle cells associated with social complexity and cognition. Maternal care is well-documented: mothers escort calves and protect them from orca predation. Gestation lasts 11.5 months, with a single calf born in mid-winter.*
