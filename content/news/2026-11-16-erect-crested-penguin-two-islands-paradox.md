Somewhere in the southern Pacific, about 800 kilometres below New Zealand, there are two groups of rocky islands that almost nobody visits. Between them, they are the only place on Earth where **erect-crested penguins** live.

Scientists just counted every single one of these penguins, for the first time ever, using drones. And they found something that does not make sense at first glance.

On the **Bounty Islands** — a scatter of bare rock totalling about 50 hectares — the penguins have been holding steady for at least 25 years.

On the **Antipodes Islands**, which are roughly 40 times bigger, the penguins have lost **more than 40% of their numbers** since 1995.

Same species. Same patch of ocean. Two completely different stories.

## 1. The penguin with the yellow eyebrows

Erect-crested penguins are about knee-high to a grown-up, with black backs, white bellies, and a stripe of bright yellow feathers above each eye that sticks straight up — that is where the name comes from. In Māori they are called *tawaki nana hī*.

They are also one of the least-studied penguins in the world. They spend winter out at sea, alone, and they only come ashore to breed on those two island groups. If you want to know how many exist, you have to go and count them. That is harder than it sounds: the islands are remote, the weather is brutal, and the cliffs are steep.

So a team from the University of Otago's Tawaki Project did it from the air. Between 2022 and 2024, they flew drones over every breeding colony of the species and photographed the nests, then counted them from the images. No penguins disturbed, no cliffs climbed.

## 2. The surprise was in the size of the place

Here is the first odd thing they found.

The Bounty Islands cover about 50 hectares. That is roughly 70 football pitches, and it is packed: around **27,800 breeding pairs** squeezed onto that little rock.

The Antipodes Islands are about 40 times larger, and they hold around **29,800 pairs**.

So the tiny island holds almost as many penguins as the giant one. For decades, scientists had assumed the Antipodes were the species' stronghold, simply because there was more room. The drone photos showed that the real story is density: on the Bounty Islands, that is roughly **400 nests for every football-pitch-sized patch of rock**.

## 3. Two islands, two directions

Then came the bigger surprise. The team compared their new counts with older surveys going back to the mid-1990s.

- **Bounty Islands:** essentially unchanged for at least 25 years. Around 27,000 pairs, again and again.
- **Antipodes Islands:** down by more than 40% since 1995 — about 1.5% of the population slipping away every year, for three decades.

The researchers gave their paper a wink of a title: *"Schrödinger's Penguin?"* — a nod to the famous thought experiment where a cat in a box is somehow both fine and not fine until you look. For this penguin, the box was the whole species. Open it, and you find two different answers at once.

## 4. Nobody knows exactly why

This is the honest part, and it is worth saying clearly: the scientists do not know for certain what is hurting the Antipodes birds.

Their best guess is a mix of pressures — natural predators, storms that have been getting more violent, and changes in where the penguins find fish and krill at sea. Different islands mean different neighbours, different weather, and different journeys to find food. Any one of those could tip a colony one way or the other.

What the study does show is that a single label can hide a lot. The erect-crested penguin is listed as **Endangered** as a species. That fits the Antipodes population. It does not really fit the Bounty one, which has been quietly getting on with things for a quarter of a century.

The team also warned against reading the stable island as "safe". A population that never grows is still one bad season away from trouble.

## The takeaway

Counting something properly is not boring housekeeping — it is how you find out that a story you thought you knew has a second half. Two islands, one penguin, two fates. Before the drones went up, nobody could have told you that.

## Try this at home

Be a drone counter. Take a photo of a busy patch of ground from above — a garden, a gravel path, a patch of lawn — or use a satellite view of your own street. Now count one kind of thing in the picture: the round stones, the dandelions, the parked cars. Then count the same kind of thing in a patch twice as big. Does the number double? Real ecologists do exactly this, and they have to be careful about which photos they compare, because a count only means something if you counted the same way both times.

## Fun fact

Erect-crested penguins nest on bare rock in enormous, noisy crowds, and the Bounty Islands have no trees, no soil, and no fresh water — just rock, spray, and penguins. Somehow, it is one of the most successful penguin neighbourhoods on the planet.
