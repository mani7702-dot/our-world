# On This Day in Space: When Galileo Said Hello to Asteroid Gaspra?

**On October 29, 1991,** NASA's Galileo spacecraft zoomed past a lumpy rock floating in space — asteroid 951 Gaspra. It was a tiny pit-stop on Galileo's long road trip to Jupiter, but it made history in a big way.

## 1. A Spacecraft's Road Trip to Jupiter

Galileo launched in **October 1989** with one giant job: study the planet Jupiter. But getting there isn't a straight line! Earth, Venus, and Jupiter all move in their own orbits, so NASA had to plan a clever path.

Galileo swung by **Venus** in 1990, looped around **Earth** in 1990 *and* again in 1992, then — on **October 29, 1991** — it passed just under **1,000 miles (1,601 km)** from asteroid Gaspra. Imagine driving past a boulder at highway speed and snapping clear photos of it from only a few car-lengths away! Galileo was travelling at **18,000 mph (about 29,000 km/h)** — faster than 20 times the speed of sound on Earth.

## 2. Gaspra: The Lumpy First Guest

Gaspra is not a round, smooth space ball. It is a jumbled, potato-shaped pile of rock measuring roughly **20 by 12 by 11 kilometers** (about 12 by 7 by 7 miles). Scientists who saw the photos said it looked like something that had "suffered a lot during its history" — a survivor of many crashes long ago.

Because Gaspra sits very close to the inner edge of the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, it is an S-type asteroid — stony and a bit like the building blocks of the planets. Galileo sent back **57 photos**, with the sharpest showing surface details as small as **54 meters** (about half a football field) across. That was enough to map nearly 80% of Gaspra's surface and discover huge flat areas named things like **Dunne Regio** — a sign that Gaspra may have broken off from a bigger parent asteroid long ago.

## 3. Why This Little Flyby Mattered

This wasn't just a photo opportunity. The Gaspra flyby was the **first time any spacecraft had ever visited an asteroid**, opening a whole new chapter in space exploration.

To hit such a small target so precisely, Galileo's team invented **optical navigation** — they took pictures of Gaspra during the approach, measured exactly where it appeared against the starry background, and guided the camera to the right spot. This trick is now used on nearly every planetary mission today.

The mission also showed that asteroids are not smooth bowling balls. They are battered, angular worlds shaped by countless collisions. Later missions — like NASA's NEAR at asteroid Eros, OSIRIS-REx at asteroid Bennu, and Japan's Hayabusa — all built on the lessons learned from Galileo's quick hello to Gaspra.

## Try This at Home
Make a Gaspra flyby! Roll a marble (the spacecraft) across a table scattered with small potatoes or clay lumps (asteroids) and try to toss a crumpled tissue (a camera view) so it lands near one target. Notice how hard it is to aim at something small while moving fast — that is exactly what Galileo's team solved!

## Fun Fact
Gaspra was discovered in **1916** by Russian astronomer **G. N. Neujmin**, who named it after **Gaspra**, a Black Sea resort town that writers like Leo Tolstoy used to visit. So a Russian poet's vacation spot became a space rock visited by an American probe — quite a journey!
