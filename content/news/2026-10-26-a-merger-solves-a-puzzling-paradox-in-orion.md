# How a stellar smash-up solved Orion's age mystery

## A nursery where time seems to run backwards

In the heart of the famous constellation Orion, about **1,400 light-years** from Earth, lies one of the closest stellar nurseries to our solar system. The Orion Nebula glows with a pinkish-red light, lit up by the intense ultraviolet radiation of massive stars in the Trapezium Cluster. Astronomers have studied this region for decades, but they kept running into a puzzle that seemed impossible to solve.

The stars in the cluster are about **1 million years old** — young in astronomical terms. But the dusty disks around them, called **proplyds** (short for "protoplanetary disks"), and even the glowing gas shell of the nebula itself, looked as though they had formed only **300,000 years ago**. How could the surroundings be younger than the stars that created them?

## When stars crash into each other

The answer came from an unexpected place: stellar mergers. At the center of the Trapezium Cluster sits **θ1 Ori C** (Theta-1 Orionis C), the cluster's most massive star. With over **33 times the mass of our Sun**, this O-type star blazes at nearly **39,000 degrees Kelvin** — hotter and brighter than anything you can imagine. Its powerful stellar wind and UV radiation strip material away from nearby protoplanetary disks, creating the distinctive tails that Hubble first photographed in detail in the 1990s.

But new research published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* suggests that θ1 Ori C was not born as a single star. Instead, it formed from a **merger of two companion stars**, triggered by the gravitational influence of a third star in a tight triple system. This kind of three-body interaction, called a **von Zeipel-Lidov-Kozai (vZLK) oscillation**, causes the two stars to spiral toward each other until they collide and merge into one.

## The merger that changed everything

Before the merger, the two lower-mass stars were gentler in their radiation. The protoplanetary disks around nearby baby stars could survive intact for their full million-year lifetimes. But after the merger, θ1 Ori C became a blazing O-type giant — 33 times the Sun's mass — unleashing a torrent of UV light and stellar wind that suddenly began stripping away those disks.

The merger also explains two other curiosities: θ1 Ori C has an unusually **tilted magnetic field** and an excess of **nitrogen** in its atmosphere — both signatures of material mixed up during a violent stellar collision. As lead author Maite Wilhelm from Leiden University puts it, this merger "changed the game" for the entire Orion Nebula.

## The cosmic domino effect

The merger didn't just affect the protoplanetary disks. By dramatically increasing the radiation and stellar wind from θ1 Ori C, it also triggered a faster expansion of the nebula's gas shell — explaining why the shell appears dynamically younger than the stars themselves. Cooler gas from the evaporating disks then mixed in, providing the extra push needed to account for the shell's rapid expansion.

"It's the first time we've witnessed the consequences of a major stellar collision in our own cosmic neighborhood," says co-author Simon Portegies Zwarte. The discovery shows that stellar collisions may be more common than astronomers previously thought, especially in the dense stellar nurseries where massive stars form.

## Try this at home

**Paper folding experiment:** Take two pieces of paper — one thick (like cardstock) and one thin (like tissue paper). Place the thin paper on top of the thick one. Gently blow on the cardstock. The tissue paper should flutter away — that's like the gentle pre-merger star. Now crumple the cardstock into a tight ball (simulating the merger) and blow hard on it. The crumpled ball creates a much stronger gust that blows away more paper. This shows how the merger made θ1 Ori C's "wind" much more powerful.

## Fun Fact

The Orion Nebula is visible to the naked eye as a fuzzy star in Orion's "sword," hanging below the three stars of Orion's belt. If you look on a clear winter night, you're seeing light that has traveled for **1,400 years** to reach your eyes — light that was being reshaped by stellar mergers and the first planets forming at the very moment Earth's early ancestors were just beginning to crawl out of the primordial sea.

**for-grownups:** The Orion Nebula (Messier 42) is a famous stellar nursery in the constellation Orion, approximately 1,344–1,400 light-years away (sources vary between Hipparcos parallax and more recent Gaia measurements). The Trapezium Cluster within it contains θ1 Ori C, the most massive star at ~33.5 solar masses (Wikipedia: Theta1 Orionis C). Protoplanetary disks ("proplyds") in the nebula were first identified by Hubble observations in 1993 (O'Dell, Wen & Hu, ApJ). The age paradox — proplyds and the nebula shell appearing ~0.3 Myr old while the stars are ~1 Myr — is resolved by new research (Wilhelm et al. 2026, A&A) proposing that θ1 Ori C formed via a triple-induced merger, dramatically increasing its luminosity and UV radiation to strip the disks. The merger also explains the star's tilted magnetic field and nitrogen excess. Wikipedia confirms θ1 Ori C1 is an O6Vp star with a B0V companion (C2) orbiting every 11.4 years. The stellar wind from θ1 Ori C moves at ~1,000 km/s and is 100,000 times stronger than the Sun's.
