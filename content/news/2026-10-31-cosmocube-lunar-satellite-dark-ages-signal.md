# How Could a Suitcase-Sized Satellite Hear the Universe's First Light?

## The quietest question in astronomy

Imagine a time before there were any stars at all — a universe filled with nothing but a warm, dark fog of gas. Astronomers call this stretch the **cosmic Dark Ages**, and it lasted roughly **150 million years** after the Big Bang. Nobody has ever directly seen it, because there were no stars shining to light it up.

So how do you study something invisible? The answer is not with your eyes, but with your ears — *radio* ears.

## Listening for a whisper from hydrogen

During the Dark Ages, the universe was full of simple **hydrogen atoms**. Every so often, a hydrogen atom makes a tiny flip and releases a faint radio wave. Scientists call it the **21-centimetre line**. Any hydrogen that released this signal more than **13.5 billion years ago** would have had its wavelength stretched by the expanding universe — so today, that whisper would arrive as very long, low-frequency radio waves.

That's the problem: radio waves between **10 and 50 MHz** are exactly the kind that Earth is bad at hearing. Our planet's **ionosphere** (the charged upper layer of the atmosphere) blocks them, and every FM station, TV tower and satellite on Earth adds its own noisy chatter on top.

## Using the Moon as a shield

The new mission turns our Moon into a **natural radio shield**. A satellite called **CosmoCube** — built in the UK and no bigger than a small carry-on suitcase — will orbit the **far side of the Moon**, the side we never see from Earth.

When CosmoCube slips behind the Moon, the entire Moon sits between the little spacecraft and our noisy planet. For about **40 minutes** out of every two-hour orbit, Earth's radio chatter simply can't reach it. Over a planned **two-year mission**, that quiet time adds up to around **1,000 hours** of listening — a clean window onto the universe's earliest chapter.

CosmoCube will also check itself constantly using a special switcher that flips between the sky and built-in reference parts, cancelling out any noise from its own electronics. Lead scientist Professor Eloy de Lera Acedo, from Cambridge's Cavendish Laboratory, explained: *"There's no other place where you can get the sort of shielding you need to detect such a faint signal, while at the same time looking at the whole of space."*

## Why it matters

If CosmoCube works, it could help answer something nobody has ever measured: how the universe tipped from a dark, nearly empty place into the dazzling universe of stars and galaxies we live in today. Its data may also reveal how mysterious **dark matter** helped pull hydrogen together to form the very first stars.

And it proves a lovely idea — that you don't always need a giant, expensive machine to explore the biggest questions.

## Try this at home

You can make your own "radio shield"! Turn a radio to a quiet, crackly static station. Now have a friend stand between you and the radio, or wrap the radio loosely in a thick blanket. Notice how the hiss gets muffled. That's because something solid is blocking the waves — exactly the trick the Moon plays for CosmoCube.

## Fun Fact

The 21-centimetre line gets its name from its wavelength: **21 centimetres**, about the length of a ruler. But because the universe has stretched out as it expanded, the same signal arriving from the Dark Ages has been pulled out to radio waves of many metres long. Same whisper — just stretched like a rubber band over 13.5 billion years.
