## What a Writer Noticed on the Fourth Floor

The Periyar Arivulagam is enormous. Seven floors, a science centre, and a bill of about 3 billion rupees ($34m) — a brand-new public library in Coimbatore, in the south of India, opened on 16 October by the state's chief minister.

A month later, a famous visitor walked in. Perumal Murugan writes novels that have been translated around the world, including *Pyre* and *One Part Woman*. He went up to the Tamil section, started reading what was on the shelves, and did not like what he found.

He wrote about it afterwards: nearly 1,800 of the books had come from one small, little-known publisher. Many carried author names nobody recognised. Some carried price tags that seemed far too high. The numbers printed on the back that every real book has — ISBNs — looked odd too.

One book, *Naladiyar Vaazhvin Vazhikatti*, cost 1,445 rupees for 300 pages. That is a lot for a paperback.

## The Mistake Anyone Could Have Caught

Here is the clearest example. *Naladiyar* is not a person. It is a very old Tamil poem — 400 short verses, four lines each, about how to live a good life. It is one of the classics of the language.

The book about it called *Naladiyar* a "poet and philosopher" — turning a whole poem into a single imagined author.

Another book in the same collection gave the wrong writer for *Madurai Kanchi*, another ancient poem. And at a different library in Madurai, a book on democracy listed a real, living economist, Jean Drèze, as its author. He told *The Hindu* newspaper that he had never written it.

Those are not small slips. A library is where people go *because* the books are supposed to be checked.

## How to Be a Book Detective

Nobody has proved yet that a computer wrote these books — more on that below. But the warning signs people pointed at are worth knowing, because they work on any book:

- **Look for a real author.** Can you find other books by them? Do they have a website, a photo, a life story? A thin, generic one-paragraph biography is a shrug, not an answer.
- **Check one fact you already know.** If the book gets something easy wrong, the hard stuff is probably worse.
- **Look at the back page.** Real publishers print an address, an ISBN, a year. One of the publishers named in the row operates from an address in Chennai where visitors found no sign on the door.
- **Ask why it costs that much.** A 300-page paperback priced like a hardback needs a reason.

Try it at home: take any fact book off your own shelf, pick one claim, and check it in two other places — a different book, an encyclopedia, a museum website. You will learn quickly which books you can trust.

## What Happens Next (and What We Do Not Know)

The Madras High Court has ordered the state's anti-corruption agency to look into how these books were bought, and ordered bank accounts linked to a publisher frozen. More than 500 of the disputed books have already been taken off the shelves at Chennai's Connemara Public Library, and nearly all were pulled from the new Coimbatore library too.

The state school education minister calls it "India's first AI scam." The previous state government, which ordered the books, says the row is political and wants its own inquiry. Both are arguing about it loudly.

But the honest part: **nobody has shown the actual files**. It is possible some of the books were written by artificial intelligence — invented names, missing information and made-up references are exactly what those tools do when nobody checks — and possible that some were written by people who simply got things wrong. More than 160 writers and researchers have signed a statement asking for the buying records to be published, so anyone can see.

One thing is already settled. Library books are promised to be true. Somebody has to check that promise, and it cannot only be a machine checking a machine.

**For grown-ups:** The case centres on Periyar Arivulagam in Coimbatore (opened 16 October 2026, ~Rs 3bn) and on books supplied by Agal Vilakku Pathippagam and Phoenix Publishing House. Novelist Perumal Murugan flagged ~1,800 titles in the Tamil section — unrecognised authors, inflated prices (Rs 1,445 for a 300-page book), dubious ISBNs, and factual errors (calling *Naladiyar*, a 400-verse classical work, a poet; attributing *Madurai Kanchi* incorrectly). Jean Dreze told The Hindu he did not write a book credited to him. The Madras High Court directed the DVAC to investigate and froze linked accounts; 523 suspect titles were removed from Connemara Public Library, the state ordered an audit of collections, and 160+ signatories demanded publication of procurement records. Minister A. Rajmohan alleges a Rs 17 lakh purchase of 2,000+ copies and calls it "India's first AI scam"; the DMK (which issued the orders in February 2026) calls the row political. Crucially, whether AI generated the texts is unproven — useful for teaching the difference between a strong suspicion and a proved fact.
