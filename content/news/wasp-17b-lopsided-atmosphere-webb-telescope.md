Most drawings of distant planets show them as perfect circles — a flat slice of a glowing orb floating through space. But a new study using the world's most powerful space telescope has revealed that at least one alien planet is more like a **lopsided balloon**: one side of its atmosphere puffs out farther than the other.

The planet is called **WASP-17b**, and it is not small. It is a giant gas planet, about twice the width of Jupiter and half Jupiter's mass — so light it would float in water if you could find a bathtub big enough. It orbits a star in the constellation **Scorpius**, about **1,300 light-years** from Earth. That means the light we are seeing from it now began its journey when the first humans were already living in caves.

## Mapping an atmosphere in 3D

WASP-17b is what scientists call a "hot Jupiter" — a gas giant that orbits crushing close to its star. Its daylight side faces temperatures of over 2,000 degrees, and giant winds scream across its atmosphere at thousands of kilometers per hour.

For years, astronomers could only see planets like this as a flat circle — the average of everything they could measure along the edge. But a team led by Dr. Lili Alderson, working between the University of Bristol and Cornell University, has now developed a clever new trick called **transmission strings**.

Here is how it works. When WASP-17b passes in front of its star (an event called a "transit"), a tiny slice of the starlight filters through the planet's outer atmosphere before reaching us. By watching that starlight at different angles around the planet's edge, the team could measure how far the atmosphere extends at each spot — like slicing a balloon and measuring the thickness of the rubber all the way around.

## The wonky bulge

What they found was surprising. The atmosphere on the planet's **evening side** — where winds carry heat from the scorching day side toward the dark night side — extends **farther out** than the atmosphere on the morning side. It is as if someone blew more air into one half of a balloon.

Think of it like this: the star-facing side of the planet heats the air above it, and that hot air rises and expands. Powerful winds then carry that warm, puffed-up air around to the evening side, where it piles up. The cooler morning side stays more compact. This creates a 3D shape that is almost circular from head-on — but lopsided from the side.

This confirms a prediction that scientists had been calculating for decades: that the extreme weather on hot Jupiters should stretch their atmospheres out in three dimensions, not keep them flat and even. For the first time, we can actually **see** it happening.

## Why a slightly lopsided planet matters

You might think a puffed-out bit of atmosphere is just a curious detail. But it solves a bigger puzzle: for years, telescopes have been confused by measurements of distant worlds that did not quite add up. Some observations looked slightly "off" — and now we know why. The planet is not a flat disk. It is a 3D shape, and its atmosphere wobbles like a slightly deflating balloon.

This matters because every atmospheric measurement — from signs of water to hints of clouds — depends on knowing what the atmosphere looks like all the way around. Without this 3D picture, we have been reading blurry, averaged-out data. Now, the transmission-strings method gives scientists a sharper lens.

**Try this at home:** You can see atmospheric bulging on Earth on a windy day. Watch steam rising from a coffee cup — on a calm day it rises straight up, but if you blow gently across the surface, the steam cloud stretches and tilts. On WASP-17b, every day is a giant windy day, and the whole atmosphere tilts with it.

**Fun Fact:** WASP-17b was the first planet ever discovered with a "backwards" orbit — it circles its star in the opposite direction that most planets spin. Astronomers think a near-collision with another planet early in its history flung it into the wrong direction, like a marble kicked the wrong way on a tilted table.
