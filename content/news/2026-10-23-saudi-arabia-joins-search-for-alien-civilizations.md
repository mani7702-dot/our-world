# Is Another Civilization Trying to Talk to Us?

## A new listening post in the Saudi desert

Far away in Saudi Arabia's northwestern desert, a quiet plateau now hums with tiny electronic eyes turned toward the stars. The Kingdom has joined a global effort called **SETI** — the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence — by installing two **LaserSETI** detectors near the ancient city of **AlUla**. These aren't giant radio telescopes; they are simpler, camera-based instruments that scan large patches of sky night after night, looking for brief laser flashes that could come from a distant technological civilization.

## How LaserSETI watches the whole sky

Most SETI efforts point a big dish at one patch of sky at a time and listen for radio hisses. **LaserSETI** does something different: each detector can image about **75 degrees** of the sky — roughly the width of four full moons side by side — using a wide-angle lens and a grating that splits light into its rainbow colors. That grating is the key trick: starlight spreads into a full rainbow, but a true artificial laser shows up as a single sharp spike at one color. Every clear night the system watches the same wide patch, continuously recording and instantly checking each flash.

## A global network with five sites

Before this, LaserSETI had only four sites — all in the Americas (California, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and Arizona). **AlUla** is the fifth site and the first in Asia. Spreading detectors across more of Earth lets the network cover more of the sky and catch fleeting events that last only milliseconds. **Dr. Franck Marchis** of the SETI Institute says the new site "gives LaserSETI an important new geographic vantage point" that connects the desert plateau to a growing worldwide web of watchers.

The AlUla instruments were installed on **August 19, 2026**, tested, and are now fully operational. Over time, most flashes will turn out to be natural — meteors, lightning, or cosmic-ray hits. But scientists hope one day a signal with a perfectly steady, unnatural color will stand out and alert astronomers worldwide.

## Try This at Home

You can build a simple “sky watcher” without lasers. Tape a small piece of **clear, unmoving photographic film** (or a cheap diffraction grating slide) over the lens of an old digital camera. Point it at the night sky and take a short exposure. The film/grating will split any bright starlight into a rainbow streak — just like LaserSETI does, only for fun. Compare a few nights of photos to see how the same stars always make the same colors, while random flashes make only single colors.

## Fun Fact

**AlUla** is Saudi Arabia's first **UNESCO World Heritage Site**. Long before it had telescopes, it was home to the ancient Nabataeans more than 2,000 years ago. Their rock-cut tombs and temples carved into rose-red cliffs can still be seen today — a reminder that humans have always looked at these same stars and wondered.

**for-grownups:** The LaserSETI detectors at AlUla Manara use commercial off-the-shelf cameras and 3-D-printed housings, with only the diffraction grating and enclosure custom-built. Each unit images ~75° of sky, uses a grating to distinguish stellar spectra from artificial laser lines, and feeds data to an onboard computer for real-time analysis. The AlUla site extends coverage to Asia; the other four sites are in California, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and Arizona. Dr. Franck Marchis (SETI Institute) and Dr. Eliot Gillum (Director, LaserSETI) lead the project. AlUla Manara is on a remote plateau ~74 km north of AlUla, itself ~380 km from Medina, and is planned as Saudi Arabia's first major astronomical observatory with future 4-meter and 2-meter telescopes. Instruments were installed August 19, 2026.
