# Can a Bacterium Really Fight a Virus by Working Backward?

Imagine a guard who normally stands ready at the door with a shield. Now imagine a different guard who keeps the shield packed away — and only pulls it out the moment a burglar appears. Scientists at Gladstone Institutes have just found bacteria that defend themselves the second way, and it turned everything they expected upside down.

## The tiny war inside every drop of soil

Bacteria are constantly attacked by **viruses called phages**. A phage latches onto a bacterium, injects its genetic instructions, and turns the cell into a factory for making more viruses. To survive, many bacteria evolved **retrons** — molecular alarm systems that make an infected cell destroy *itself*. That sounds like losing, but it works: the sacrifice stops the virus from multiplying and spreading to the bacterium's neighbours.

## Why did scientists think the system was broken?

For years, researchers thought they knew the plan. In ordinary retrons, a **poisonous protein is already sitting inside the cell**, held harmless by short strands of **single-stranded DNA** (ssDNA). When a phage attacks, the DNA gets disturbed, the poison slips loose, and the cell shuts down.

The new **"type VI retrons"** didn't fit. When the team looked for the usual ssDNA, they found almost none. "It was very odd, and we wondered if they might be broken," said Karen Zhang, the study's lead author. Yet the bacteria clearly still fought off phages — so the system had to be working somehow.

## The backwards switch

After years of puzzling experiments, the answer emerged. These retrons run the process **in reverse**. Healthy cells hold no weapon at all. A phage arriving flips the switch: the cell begins churning out ssDNA, which grabs a piece of RNA that had been blocking the poison's recipe. That releases the block, the toxin is built, and the infected cell shuts down. The poison itself is remarkably small — it simply pokes holes in the cell's outer membrane.

"It's almost the mirror-image version of how we traditionally view retrons," Zhang said. Senior author Seth Shipman called them "by far the strangest of the retron systems" anyone has come across.

## Why it matters for people

Retrons can deliver DNA precisely, which makes them promising tools for **gene editing** — repairing harmful mutations in human cells. Understanding this backward system could help scientists build better DNA tools, tiny molecular switches, and possibly new ways to fight harmful bacteria.

## Try this at home

Bacteria are everywhere, even in a clean-looking kitchen. Press a fingertip gently onto a slice of bread, seal it in a zip bag, and leave it somewhere warm for a few days. The fuzzy spots are colonies of microbes — each one started as a single cell that divided again and again. (Wash your hands afterwards, and don't eat it!)

## Fun Fact

Retrons were named by combining "**reverse transcriptase**" — the enzyme they use to copy RNA back into DNA — with the suffix "-on," meaning a genetic unit. So the name literally describes the copying trick at the heart of their alarm system.
