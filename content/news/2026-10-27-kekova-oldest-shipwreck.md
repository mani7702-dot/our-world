## How Did the World's Oldest Shipwreck Stay Hidden for 4,500 Years?

Three questions a kid-safe archaeology story answers with pottery, not gold.

### 1. What Did They Find?

Off the rocky coast of Kekova in southern Turkey, underwater archaeologists have found a shipwreck so old it might rewrite the history books. Acting on clues from local seafarers, Associate Professor Hakan Öniz of Akdeniz University led the effort to locate the site in 2024. The wooden ship itself is long gone — seawater dissolves timber over millennia. But what remains is a time capsule: hundreds of ancient pottery jars scattered across the seabed, their shapes and clay still holding secrets from 4,500 years ago.

### 2. How Old Are the Pots?

The cargo tells the story. The jars — large amphorae with two handles and a raised clay "belt" around the middle — are the kind used to carry oil, wine, and tree resin across the ancient Mediterranean. By studying the shape, size, and manufacturing marks on the pottery, archaeologists can date it to roughly 2700–2400 BCE. That puts it in the Early Bronze Age, around the same time the great pyramids of Giza were being built. Before this discovery, the oldest known shipwreck was the **Dokos wreck** off Greece, dated to about 2200 BCE. The new find is thousands of years older.

### 3. Why Does It Matter?

Long before the Romans, Greeks, or even the ancient Egyptians had writing, people were already sailing the seas with purpose. The amphorae found at the Kekova site were made in different regions — some from clay sources in the Central Jordan Valley, others matching styles from across the eastern Mediterranean. This means trade routes were already complex and far-reaching more than four millennia ago. The ship was likely traveling along the Turkish coast, perhaps seeking shelter from a storm, when it struck the rocks.

**Try this at home:** Build a small model boat from a plastic bottle and test how it floats. Then ask an adult to help you visit your local maritime museum — many have pottery fragments from shipwrecks that show how ancient traders carried goods across oceans.

**Fun Fact:** The oldest known anchors are made of giant stones with holes bored through them — sailors tied ropes around the rocks to hold their ships in place. The Dokos shipwreck site had two massive stone anchors found 40 meters from the wreck!

**for-grownups:** The Kekova shipwreck's pottery cargo, dated to 2700–2400 BCE via typology and manufacturing techniques, represents the world's oldest known submerged shipwreck, surpassing the previously-held record of the Dokos wreck (~2200 BCE). A preliminary report has been published in *Journal of Archeological Science: Reports* (Springer, DOI: 10.1007/s11457-026-09532-3). The findings suggest maritime trade in the eastern Mediterranean during the Early Bronze Age was far more extensive than previously understood — contemporaneous with Egypt's pyramid age and the port of Byblos. The ship is tentatively linked to early international maritime networks transporting cedar, silver, oils, and resins.
