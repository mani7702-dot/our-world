# How did astronauts' hearts handle six months in space — and will they be OK on Mars?

A trip to Mars is a very long journey — about six months each way. That means astronauts would spend over a year floating in weightlessness before they could even step onto the Red Planet. Scientists have wondered: when they finally arrive, would their hearts be strong enough to work under Mars' gravity?

New research says: yes, probably!

## Why floating in space is hard on the heart

On Earth, your heart works against gravity every minute of every day. It pumps blood downward from your head and upward from your feet. But in space, there is no gravity to fight. Blood and fluids shift toward the head and chest, and the heart does not have to work as hard.

After just a few weeks floating, the heart can shrink a little and weaken — kind of like how a muscle gets smaller when you don't use it much.

## How 13 astronauts beat the challenge

Researchers from Northwestern University, UT Southwestern, and UT Houston studied **13 astronauts** who had just finished about **six months** living and working on the International Space Station. Before they left, while they were in space, and after they came home — the scientists took detailed ultrasound pictures of each astronaut's heart.

Here is the good news: even though all the astronauts' hearts changed during the first two weeks in space, their hearts **adapted over time**. By the end of their missions, their heart function had recovered toward normal levels.

## The secret weapon: daily exercise

Every astronaut followed NASA's Exercise Countermeasures program. That means working out for up to **two hours per day** — about the length of a movie!

Their daily routine included:

1. **Endurance training** (30–45 minutes) — like running or cycling, to keep the heart strong.
2. **Resistance training** (60–75 minutes) — lifting heavy things, to keep muscles and bones from getting weak.

"The astronauts who kept up their daily workouts came back with much healthier hearts," said Dr. James Thomas, the study's senior author.

## The Mars gravity test

After returning to Earth, the astronauts were tilted at a 22-degree angle — a position that makes their bodies feel the pull of gravity about the same way **Mars' gravity** would (Mars has only about 38% of Earth's gravity).

Every single astronaut passed the test without feeling dizzy or faint — the kind of problem that used to plague returning space travelers.

That means if these astronauts had landed on Mars right after their mission, they would likely have been able to stand up and walk around and do their space-walk jobs without trouble.

## What's next?

This is only the first step. The study had just 13 astronauts, and they were tested with a *simulated* Mars gravity — not the real thing. But for now, the results are exciting. With good exercise habits, the human heart may be ready for the long journey to another planet.

## Try this at home

Lie down on the floor and slowly lift your legs up toward the ceiling. Notice how blood rushes toward your head? That is a tiny version of what happens in space! Now, sit up slowly — your heart has to work harder to pump blood back down. Astronauts do this every day when they return from space.

## Fun Fact

The word "astronaut" comes from ancient Greek words meaning "star sailor." The first humans to walk on the Moon — the Apollo astronauts — were also "star sailors," traveling 384,000 kilometers (239,000 miles) through space to visit our nearest celestial neighbor!

## for-grownups

A team led by Dr. James Thomas at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine tracked 13 astronauts (average age 49) who spent ~6 months aboard the International Space Station. Using echocardiograms taken before, during, and after flight, they measured cardiac filling and stroke volume — both of which declined in the first two weeks of microgravity but gradually recovered toward pre-flight baselines as the cardiovascular system adapted. NASA's Exercise Countermeasures program required astronauts to exercise up to 2 hours per day (30–45 min endurance + 60–75 min resistance). After returning, a 22-degree head-down tilt test simulated the circulatory effects of 38%-Earth Martian gravity. All astronauts passed without orthostatic intolerance — a promising result given that Mars-bound crews must be operational immediately upon landing. The study was published in Circulation (Thomas et al.) and funded by NASA, NCC9-58, and the National Heart Foundation of Australia.
