# SpaceX's Falcon 9 Slows Down as Starlink Satellites Soar from California

## 1 — What blasted off, and where?

On the evening of **October 23, 2026**, a **Falcon 9** rocket thundered into the California sky, packed with **27 brand-new Starlink** satellites. The launch came from **Space Launch Complex 4 East** at **Vandenberg Space Force Base**, a coastal pad that is specially built for rockets heading into polar orbit — the kind that slice from side to side across the planet, perfect for blanketing the whole world in internet beams.

The whole liftoff happened at **6:48 p.m. EDT** (that is 3:48 in the afternoon Pacific time), and it was over in a flash. But the rocket was not done yet — its big first stage had to come back down to Earth for another soft landing.

## 2 — How did the rocket come back down?

Most of us think rockets are wasteful — they zoom up, drop their pieces into the ocean, and build a brand-new one for the next trip. **SpaceX turned that idea upside down.**

After the Falcon 9's upper stage pushed the Starlink stack into orbit, the **first-stage booster** — named **B1063** — flipped around, fired its engines to slow down, and guided itself all the way back to a floating landing pad in the ocean. The pad is a big, drone-controlled ship called the **"Of Course I Still Love You"** (yes, that is its real name — an inside joke from the *Culture* science-fiction novels).

This smooth landing was B1063's **36th flight and landing**. That is like a passenger jet taking its 36th round-trip across the ocean — and landing perfectly every single time. It shows how rocket reuse is now ordinary, routine, and reliable.

## 3 — Why is Falcon 9 flying less?

Even though Falcon 9 is busy, it is also **slowing down**. The October 23 launch was the rocket's **116th flight of 2026**, and about three-quarters of those were for Starlink. But SpaceX is now switching its attention to an even bigger rocket called **Starship** — fully reusable and strong enough to carry the newest, **Version 3 Starlink** satellites that are too large for Falcon 9.

At their busiest, Falcon 9 missions used to fly every two or three days. Now that schedule has stretched, and 2025's record of 165 launches is expected to stand for a while. The message is clear: Falcon 9 did the heavy lifting for years, and now Starship is taking over the big jobs — while Falcon 9 keeps flying the satellites it was built for.

The Starlink constellation itself is growing fast. As of October 2026, it had about **11,150 satellites** in orbit, making up roughly **75% of every active, maneuverable satellite** circling Earth today. That means for every four satellites you can spot moving across the night sky, three of them probably belong to Starlink.

## Try this at home

Fill a balloon with air and let it go — the air rushes out and the balloon darts around the room. The balloon is your rocket, and the escaping air is the engine thrust. Now try to guide the balloon back to a target (like a bucket) without popping it. That is about as hard as landing a 100-ton booster on a drone ship — but the balloon makes the idea easy to picture!

## Fun Fact

The landing ship's name — **Of Course I Still Love You** — is one of four drone ships that SpaceX named after *Culture* novel starships. The others are *Just Read the Instructions* and *A Shortfall of Prophecy*. SpaceX's founder Elon Musk was inspired by the sci-fi series, proving that even rocket science can have a sense of humor.

## For the grown-ups

Falcon 9 B1063 launched 27 Starlink Group 15-15 satellites from Vandenberg SFB SLC-4E on Oct 23, 2026 at 18:48 UTC, then landed on the droneship "Of Course I Still Love You" — its 36th flight and landing. This was Falcon 9's 116th launch of 2026, but cadence has slowed as SpaceX transitions high-volume Starlink deployment to the larger Starship vehicle, which can carry the new Version 3 Starlink satellites that Falcon 9 cannot accommodate. Wikipedia's Falcon 9 article independently confirms that as of October 23, 2026, SpaceX has successfully landed Falcon 9 boosters 656 times, with individual boosters having flown as many as 37 flights.
