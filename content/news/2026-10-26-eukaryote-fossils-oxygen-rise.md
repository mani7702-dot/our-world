# How did oxygen spark the rise of Earth's first complex cells 1.7 billion years ago?

## 1. A treasure hunt in ancient Australian rocks

Deep in Australia's Northern Territory, scientists drilled into rocks that are nearly **2 billion years old**. These mudstones were once soft mud on an ancient seafloor, but over eons they turned to stone. When researchers carefully dissolved the rock and looked at what remained under powerful microscopes, they found something extraordinary: more than **12,000 tiny fossils**, each one a clue to the very first complex cells on Earth.

These fossils come from rocks between **1.75 and 1.4 billion years old** — making them the oldest known eukaryote fossils in the entire world. Eukaryotes are the family of cells that eventually gave rise to all animals, plants, algae, and fungi. Before they appeared, only much simpler cells called prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) existed, with no nucleus or internal rooms.

## 2. Why oxygen was the secret ingredient

Not all cells are the same — and the ancient rocks tell a clear story about what made the difference. The eukaryote fossils were found in sediments that settled in **oxygen-rich waters**, from quiet coastal mudflats to the open ocean. But in layers that formed where oxygen was absent, scientists found only simpler microbes.

This matters because complex cells need a lot of energy to run their internal machinery. Oxygen-powered breathing (called aerobic respiration) produces far more energy than the old-fashioned method used by prokaryotes. Think of it like comparing a steam engine to a bicycle — the extra power lets the cell grow bigger, build a nucleus, and organize itself into something far more impressive.

## 3. A timeline of life's greatest leap

The oxygen that sparked this revolution began appearing in Earth's atmosphere about **2.4 billion years ago** during an event scientists call the Great Oxygenation Event. At first there was only a trickle — but over hundreds of millions of years, oxygen levels grew. By the time the Australian mudstones were forming (1.7 billion years ago), oxygen had become common enough in the oceans to support the rise of complex cells. The eukaryotes that evolved then would eventually split, merge, and diversify into every tree, fish, bird, and person you see today.

## Try this at home

**Make a fossil in a bowl!** Mix together some fine mud or clay with water in a shallow dish. Press small shells, leaves, or even your fingernail into the surface, then let it dry for a day or two. Gently brush away the dried mud to reveal your "fossil" impression. Discuss: what kind of ancient sea creature might have left a trace like this? How do you think scientists can tell the difference between a real fossil and a rock that just looks funny?

## Fun Fact

The oldest rocks on Earth are about 4 billion years old, but the **oldest fossils** we have of complex cells are just under 2 billion years old. That means for the first half of our planet's history, every living thing was a tiny, simple prokaryote — until oxygen changed everything!
