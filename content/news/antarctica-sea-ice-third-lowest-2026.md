# Why did Antarctic sea ice suddenly start shrinking instead of growing?

## The ice that grew for years — then stopped

Around the bottom of the world, something surprising happened. From **1979 to 2014**, the sea ice floating on the ocean around Antarctica crept up by about **1 percent each decade** — the opposite of what was happening in the Arctic, where ice was racing to disappear.

Then, in **2015**, the trend flipped. The growth stopped. And the losses began.

In **September 2026**, Antarctic sea ice reached its annual winter peak — the biggest it gets each year. But that peak was only about **17.59 million square kilometers** (roughly 6.79 million square miles). That made 2026 the year with the **third-lowest winter maximum** in the entire satellite record.

## The two poles, two very different shapes

There is a good reason Antarctica acts differently from the Arctic. The Arctic is an **ocean basin surrounded by land** — the water is boxed in, so the ice stays relatively close to the pole. Antarctica is a **continent surrounded by ocean** — the ice has room to spread out in a giant ring around the land. That means Antarctic ice can grow over enormous distances in winter.

But that same shape is also its weakness. As the ice spreads into warmer southern latitudes, the Sun's summer rays melt it faster. So every year the ice balloons out in September, then shrinks back again by February.

## How do satellites "see" ice through clouds?

Scientists do not wait for clear skies to measure ice — they use **passive microwave sensors**. These instruments detect the natural microwave energy that Earth's surface gives off. Sea ice and open water give off different amounts of this energy, so the satellites can tell them apart even when thick clouds hide everything below.

The record stretches back to **late 1978**, starting with NASA's **Nimbus-7** satellite. Since then, the US **Defense Meteorological Satellite Program** (starting 1987), **NASA's Aqua** (2002-2011), and since **2025** **Japan's GCOM-W** satellite have kept the long watch alive.

## Is this the new normal?

The 2015 reversal was dramatic, and the **2023** season made it starker — that was when Antarctica hit both its lowest summer minimum AND its lowest winter maximum on record. The question now is whether 2026's low maximum is part of a lasting shift, or whether a few cool seasons could push the numbers back up.

Because Antarctic sea ice bounces around so much from year to year, scientists say they need many more seasons of data to know for sure. For now, they are watching — and the microwave satellites keep sending back their quiet, cloud-penetrating signal, season after season.

### Try this at home
Fill a shallow baking tray with a few centimeters of water and a pinch of salt. Place one ice cube on a piece of plastic floating freely on the surface, and another on the bottom of the tray. As the tray ices over in your freezer, watch how the floating piece gets pushed aside by new ice — just like Antarctic ice spreads outward, while Arctic ice (boxed in) behaves differently. (Always get an adult's help with freezers.)
