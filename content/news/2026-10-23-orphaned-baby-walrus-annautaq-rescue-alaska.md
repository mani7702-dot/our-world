# How did a baby walrus survive alone on an Alaskan beach?

## A lonely calf on a far-off shore

On a remote beach in northern Alaska, a tiny Pacific walrus calf was left all alone.

The baby walrus — only about five months old — had been separated from his herd during heavy ocean waves. When the adults swam away, he was suddenly on his own, surrounded by scavenging birds that made survival even harder.

## A name that means "axe"

Two careful investigators found the shivering calf: **Violet Anniskett**, a wildlife research intern from the local community, and **Tony Fischbach**, a scientist with the U.S. Geological Survey.

They named him **Annautaq** (pronounced *ah-now-tuq*), which means "axe" in the Iñupiaq language — an honor for Anniskett's father.

## The long journey to safety

Because walrus calves drink their mother's milk for an entire year and learn how to survive from their moms, wildlife officials decided Annautaq could not be returned to the wild safely.

So began an epic journey. First, local caregivers looked after him for several days while they arranged transport to the **Alaska SeaLife Center** in Seward.

From there, he traveled nearly **4,000 miles** to **SeaWorld Orlando**, one of only four places in the United States built to care for walruses. At his new home, he joins three other walruses — Garfield, Kaboodle, and Uki — who will help him learn, grow, and thrive.

## Growing up surrounded by friends

At SeaWorld, Annautaq gets **six bottle feedings every single day**, plus 24-hour care from veterinarians and animal experts. He'll spend a quiet month in quarantine to stay healthy, then slowly meet his new walrus neighbors.

Two-year-old **Uki** — herself a rescued walrus — is ready to be a big sister of sorts. And **Kaboodle**, who has experience caring for baby walruses, will help show Annautaq the ropes.

Soon, he'll also become an ambassador for his whole species, helping people learn about the challenges Pacific walruses face in our changing world.

## Try this at home

**Make a walrus habitat in a bowl.** Fill a shallow bowl with water, then add blue food coloring and ice cubes to make it cold. Tuck a toy or small figurine (a "walrus") into the water. Ask: where does it rest? Walruses need sea ice to rest between dives — just like your toy needs something to "land" on. Discuss why keeping their icy home healthy matters to these gentle giants.

## Fun Fact

**Walrus tusks** aren't just for show! A walrus uses its long, curved tusks — which can grow up to three feet — to haul its heavy body in and out of the water, and to haul itself onto ice or rock. They also help walruses anchor themselves so they don't drift while they rest.
