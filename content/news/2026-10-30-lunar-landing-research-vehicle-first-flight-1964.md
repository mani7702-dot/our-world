# What Was the Flying Bedstead? NASA's Bizarre 1964 Moon-Landing Trainer

## 1. A Robot That Flew on Earth

Imagine a flying machine that looked nothing like an airplane — no wings, no tail, just four spindly legs and a jet engine pointing straight down. On **October 30, 1964**, NASA took this strange contraption into the sky for the very first time.

It was called the **Lunar Landing Research Vehicle**, or **LLRV** for short — though almost everyone just called it the "Flying Bedstead."

Why did it look so weird? Because NASA's job was to teach astronauts how to land on the Moon! The Moon has only **one-sixth** of Earth's gravity, so a normal airplane wouldn't behave the same way. The Flying Bedstead used a powerful jet engine mounted on a swivel to cancel out **five out of six** of Earth's gravity. To the pilot, it felt almost like flying in slow motion — just like on the Moon!

## 2. The Pilots Who Practiced Moonwalks

The very first person to climb into the flying bedstead was **Joe Walker**, a legendary Air Force test pilot who had already flown the X-15 rocket plane past the edge of space. On that October day in 1964, he lifted off from NASA's **Dryden Flight Research Center** in Edwards, California, hovered ten feet above the dry lake bed, and practiced hovering for about a minute — then did it two more times that same day.

Over the next few years, NASA built even more of these vehicles. And one of the most famous pilots to train in them was a quiet former naval aviator named **Neil Armstrong**.

Armstrong already had a secret: NASA had picked him to lead the first-ever Moon landing — Apollo 11. But before he could step onto the lunar surface, Armstrong needed to master landing a spacecraft that bounced around on rocket power, just like the Flying Bedstead. He practiced again and again, and his training paid off on July 20, 1969, when he guided the Apollo 11 lunar module to a safe touchdown in the Sea of Tranquility.

## 3. Not Without Danger

Flying the Flying Bedstead was not just strange — it was risky. The vehicle was powered by **hydrogen peroxide rockets** that could fire in any direction, letting the pilot move up, down, and sideways. But if the main jet engine flamed out, the whole machine would drop like a stone.

**One day in 1968, it did exactly that.**

Armstrong was practicing in Lunar Landing Training Vehicle #1 when the big jet engine cut out. The spacecraft plummeted toward the ground — but Armstrong's **ejection seat** fired at the last second, shooting him to safety just moments before the machine exploded in a ball of fire. He walked away with a scratched face and a story he'd tell for the rest of his life.

## Try This at Home

You can build a simple "Flying Bedstead" simulator with things around the house:

1. **Hovercraft balloon car:** Tape a balloon to a small toy car so the air comes out the bottom. Blow it up and let go — the car will hover on a cushion of air, just like the LLRV hovered above the ground!
2. **Lunar gravity experiment:** Have an adult hold a helium balloon while you stand on a bathroom scale. Each time you let out some string, you'll "weigh less" — just like you would on the Moon (one-sixth of your real weight).
3. **Find the landing zone:** On a clear night, look for the Moon through a window or outside. Can you spot the smooth, dark patches where the Apollo astronauts landed? Those are ancient lava plains called "maria."

## Fun Fact

The name "Flying Bedstead" was so popular that when NASA later built the space shuttle, they joked that the shuttle's vertical takeoff and landing profile made it look like "a flying bedstead with wings." The nickname has stuck around space history ever since!

## for-grownups:

The LLRV was a Project Apollo program vehicle operated by the NASA Flight Research Center (now Armstrong Flight Research Center) at Edwards Air Force Base, California. It used a General Electric CF6-502 turbofan engine producing ~14,000 pounds of thrust, gimbaled to cancel 5/6 of the vehicle's gross weight (~3,200 kg), simulating lunar 1/6-g conditions. Hydrogen peroxide reaction control thrusters provided pitch, roll, and yaw control. Two LLRVs were built (Serial #1 and #2); Neil Armstrong's ejection on June 8, 1968, occurred during a qualification flight of LLTV #1 (the Lunar Landing Training Vehicle), which was an improved LLRV variant. The Apollo Lunar Module (LM) had a dedicated descent engine (TRW LM Descent Propulsion System, ~10,000 lbf thrust) operating in the vacuum of space. Armstrong's Apollo 11 landing on July 20, 1969, used the LM Eagle; he manually pilots past boulder field to site Tranquility Base. The LLRV/LLTV program trained 12 Apollo astronauts (Apollo 8 through Apollo 17) in lunar landing techniques. Source: NASA Technical Reports, NASA SP-287, "Flight Evaluation of the Lunar Landing Research Vehicle."
