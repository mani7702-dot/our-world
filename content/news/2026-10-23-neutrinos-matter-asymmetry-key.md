## The mystery of the missing antimatter

Look around you. Everything you can see — the air, the ground, the stars overhead — is made of **matter**. But the most famous equation in physics says that when energy turns into particles, it should make **equal amounts of matter and antimatter**. So why is the whole Universe swimming in matter and almost empty of antimatter?

If the Big Bang had made equal shares, every bit of matter would have found an antimatter twin and **annihilated** in a flash, leaving a cosmos full of light but no stars, no planets, and no us. The fact that anything exists at all is one of the biggest unsolved puzzles in science.

## Ghost particles with a heavy secret

The particles that could solve this riddle are called **neutrinos** — near-weightless "ghost particles" that slip through your hand and through entire planets without noticing. Every second, trillions of them stream past you from the Sun and from space.

Most neutrinos are feather-light and never stop to interact. But in the new study, physicists Rishav Roshan and Sudipta Show explored a heavier, **short-lived cousin** of the everyday neutrino. In the very early Universe, these heavy neutrinos would have been born alongside electrons, protons, and their antimatter twins. Because their half-life is long, they kept decaying **well after** most other particles had already paired up and vanished.

When the heavy antineutrino twins decayed, their partners were gone — so their ashes tipped the cosmic ledger just a little more toward matter. It is like a scale that should stay balanced, but one side keeps getting tiny, delayed weights dropped onto it.

## A signature we might detect

The researchers say this "delayed" process would leave two faint traces for astronomers to hunt:

1. A swirl of **primordial gravitational waves** — ripples in spacetime from the Universe's baby years — that today's detectors are just learning to listen for.

2. Subtle changes in how the cosmic neutrino background (the ancient sea of lightweight neutrinos) flows across the sky.

Neither signal is strong enough to confirm the idea right now, but both are on the horizon of what modern telescopes aim to measure.

## Why this matters to you

If the theory holds, it means a ghostly particle you cannot see with your eyes — one that passes through you every moment — is partly the reason you are here to read this. The tiny extra bit of matter it helped create eventually clumped together under gravity to form the atoms in your phone, your pencil, and yes, your own body.

## Try this at home

Take a clear glass of water and drop in one drop of milk. The white streaks that spread through the water are like neutrino "interactions" — most of it stays dark and invisible, but every once in a while something leaves a visible mark. Now imagine that for every drop that shows, ten others passed through unseen. That is how a neutrino-filled Universe can still leave a cosmic fingerprint.

## Fun Fact

Neutrinos come in **three flavors** — electron, muon, and tau — and each one can morph into another flavor as it travels (a trick called "oscillation"). This ghostly chameleon behavior is exactly why physicists think these particles might also be the key to the Universe's greatest mystery.

**for-grownups:** Baryogenesis — the process that created the observed matter over antimatter — requires Sakharov's three conditions (baryon number violation, C/CP violation, departure from thermal equilibrium). Standard Model CKM CP violation is far too small to explain the observed baryon-to-photon ratio (~6×10⁻¹⁰). Roshan & Show ("Delayed Charged Lepton Yukawa Equilibration in Minimal Seesaw," Phys. Rev. Lett. 137, 111802 (2026)) propose that in the minimal Type-I seesaw with two heavy Majorana neutrinos, delayed equilibration of the charged lepton sector — occurring after heavy-neutrino freeze-out but within the temperature window where sphalerons are still active — can generate sufficient CP-violating lepton asymmetry that sphaleron processing partially converts to the baryon asymmetry. The model predicts a long-lived heavy neutrino with an observable impact on the spectral shape of the diffuse supernova neutrino background (DSNB) and on primordial gravitational-wave backgrounds, offering testable signatures for upcoming observatories.
