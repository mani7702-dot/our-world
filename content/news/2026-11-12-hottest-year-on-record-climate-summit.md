## A Record Nobody Wants

Every month, scientists around the world take the planet's temperature in thousands of places — on land, in the air, in the sea. Then they average it all.

This year's numbers are so high that one research group, Berkeley Earth in the United States, told BBC News it is **95% sure 2026 will be the hottest year ever measured**, wiping out the record set in 2024. That is with almost two months of the year still to go.

The UN's weather agency, the World Meteorological Organization, is a bit more careful. It says 2026 will land among the four hottest years on record. Both of them agree on the direction: up.

## Where All That Heat Comes From

Two things are pushing temperatures at the same time.

The first is the one people have been talking about for decades: the extra blanket of gases humans have added to the air, mostly by burning coal, oil and gas. Ordinary years now start warmer than ordinary years used to.

The second is **El Niño** — a natural event where the tropical Pacific Ocean lets go of heat it has been storing. Think of the ocean as a hot water bottle that has been sitting under the sheets for years. El Niño is the moment the cap comes off. This one is unusually powerful: scientists expect it to be the strongest since at least 1950, and it is likely to reach record strength by December.

That is why the heat is not only coming this year. It usually arrives a year later. 2024's record came from the El Niño before this one, and researchers already expect 2027 to break the record again.

## Three Numbers Worth Knowing

- **1.47°C** — how much warmer than the pre-industrial average the world was from January to September 2026, according to the WMO. Pre-industrial means before factories and cars, when the air held less of that blanket. Every tenth of a degree matters to glaciers, reefs and farms.
- **Ten years** — the ocean has now set a record for stored heat ten years in a row. Warm water feeds stronger storms and can bleach coral reefs.
- **2.8°C** — roughly how much warming the world is on track for this century at current rates.

## Meanwhile, in a Seaside Town in Turkey

On Monday, leaders from almost every country flew to Antalya in Turkey for a huge meeting called COP31. It happens every year: governments compare notes, promise money to help countries hit by floods and storms, and argue about cutting the pollution that causes the warming.

The UN's climate chief, Simon Stiell, told the room, "No single COP is a silver bullet." Some leaders used the moment to warn that heatwaves and dry summers are already changing daily life, and the UN weather chief said the planet's numbers give "new meaning to the phrase off the charts."

The meeting runs until 20 November, so there is still time for decisions to be made — or not made.

## Try This at Home: The Hot Water Bottle Test

Fill one jar with water and one with dry sand. Warm both under the same lamp for 15 minutes, then switch the lamp off and check them every few minutes with the back of your hand.

The water stays warm much longer. The ocean works the same way: it holds heat for a long time, then slowly gives it back. That is why the sea shapes weather months after a heat spike — and why a warm ocean is a big deal, not a small one.

Sources differ on certainty, not on trend — worth teaching as a lesson in how science states uncertainty.
