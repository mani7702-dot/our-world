# Will a Nuclear Spaceship Zoom Past Mars in 2028?

Picture this: a spaceship that carries its own miniature sun, gliding silently past Mars with no need for sunlight charging its batteries. That is not science fiction — it is NASA's next big plan.

## Meet SR-1 Freedom

NASA's **Space Reactor-1 Freedom** is being built to be the first spacecraft in history powered by **nuclear fission** that travels beyond Earth's orbit. Most spacecraft today run on sunlight (solar panels) or on tiny radioactive batteries called RTGs. But a fission reactor can make *much* more power — enough to move a whole spaceship with electricity instead of chemistry alone.

Think of it like this: a solar-panel spacecraft is like a boat with a small sail. A nuclear-electric spacecraft is like a boat with a *huge* motor. Both can move you, but the motor gets you there faster and farther.

## What is a fission power module?

At the heart of SR-1 Freedom is a **nuclear reactor** designed by the U.S. Department of Energy's Idaho National Laboratory. This reactor splits atoms (just like a nuclear power plant on Earth) to release heat. That heat is turned into electricity by a system called a **closed Brayton cycle** — basically, a fancy gas turbine that spins a generator.

A company called **Sierra Lobo Inc.** is building the power conversion system. The result? About **20,000 watts** of clean electric power — enough to run dozens of homes back on Earth.

The fuel is a type of uranium called **HALEU** (high-assay low-enriched uranium), which is safe and not the kind used in weapons. The whole spacecraft weighs about **26,455 pounds** (12,000 kg) — roughly the same as five school buses.

## The SkyFall payload

SR-1 Freedom is not just sailing to Mars to test its engine. It is carrying a science mission called **SkyFall**, which includes **three robotic helicopters** that evolved from NASA's famous Ingenuity helicopter.

Remember Ingenuity? It was the little helicopter that made the first powered flights on another planet. The SkyFall helicopters are grown-up versions — bigger, stronger, and ready to fly farther. They will swoop down to Mars' surface to collect scientific data and hunt for signs of **water**, which tells us where future astronauts might be able to refuel and rest.

## Why go nuclear?

Solar power works great close to the Sun. But Mars is *far* from the Sun — about **1.5 times farther** than Earth. By the time sunlight reaches Mars, it is much weaker. A nuclear reactor does not need sunlight at all. It works just as well in the dark.

This means SR-1 Freedom can carry heavier science instruments, fly faster, and reach places that sunlight-powered ships simply cannot. It is a stepping stone to even bigger dreams: powering lunar bases on the Moon and someday carrying humans to Mars and back.

NASA plans to launch SR-1 Freedom in **late 2028**, riding the natural alignment of Earth and Mars that happens every 26 months.

## Try this at home

Fill a clear bottle with water, add a drop of milk, and shine a flashlight through it. The milk simulates how a nuclear reactor's fuel glows when atoms are splitting — you will see the light scatter in the water like a miniature star. (Do not shake it; that shows how particles spread out, like a spacecraft's exhaust.)

## Fun fact

The Brayton cycle power system used on SR-1 Freedom is the same kind of gas-turbine engine that powers some jet airplanes — but instead of burning kerosene, it runs on heat from splitting atoms in space.
