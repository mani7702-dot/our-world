# Is dark energy really unchanging? 2,884 exploding stars weigh in

## A library of exploding stars bigger than ever

Imagine you are trying to measure how fast the universe is stretching over time — but your ruler keeps changing its markings. That is the challenge astronomers face when they study **dark energy**, the mysterious force that seems to be pushing the cosmos apart faster and faster.

To study this, scientists rely on special explosions called **Type Ia supernovae**. These happen when a white dwarf star — the dense, Earth-sized leftover of a star like our Sun — pulls too much material from a companion and blows itself up in a flash so bright it can outshine an entire galaxy for a few weeks. Because every such explosion reaches almost the same peak brightness, astronomers use them as "standard candles" to measure distances across the universe.

## Why 2,884 supernovae are better than one

For more than 30 years, different teams around the world have found and measured these exploding stars. But each team used slightly different telescopes, analysis methods, and corrections — making it hard to compare results. An international group led by the University of Queensland's School of Mathematics and Physics decided to fix that by gathering **2,884 Type Ia supernovae** into a single, unified dataset.

"We rebuilt three decades of astronomical observations into one consistent framework," explains the team. By revisiting older data with the latest understanding of how supernovae behave, and by correcting for effects like cosmic dust and the bending of starlight by gravity (called gravitational lensing), they created the most detailed picture yet of how cosmic expansion has changed over time.

## The surprise: dark energy might not be constant

For nearly 25 years, the leading theory has treated dark energy as a fixed property of space — a cosmological constant that never changes. But the new 2,884-supernova compilation, combined with other cosmic measurements, points to something different.

Instead of confirming the standard model, the results add to hints from other major projects — including the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) — that dark energy may **evolve over time** rather than staying the same. This doesn't mean dark energy is stronger or weaker right now; it may just change at different eras of the universe's history.

Think of it like a car's accelerator pedal. If dark energy were constant, the universe's expansion would keep accelerating at the same rate forever. But if dark energy changes over time, it's more like a pedal that was pressed harder in the past and is gradually easing up — or vice versa. Either way, the universe's fate could be very different from what we expected just a few years ago.

## What this means for the cosmos

No one is saying the old model is wrong — yet. The new dataset still fits the standard picture reasonably well. But the hints that dark energy varies are getting louder, especially as independent projects like DESI and the Dark Energy Survey (DES) report similar findings. When 2,884 explosions start telling the same story, even the most careful scientists take notice.

## Try this at home

You can model how astronomers use standard candles to measure distance! Grab a flashlight and a friend. Have your friend walk away while holding a small LED light of a known brightness. From where you stand, the light will look dimmer the farther away they walk. Measure how much dimmer it gets at different distances, then calculate how far away an identical light must be if you only see a certain brightness. That is the same principle astronomers use to map the universe's expansion — just with supernovae brighter than a trillion suns instead of a tiny LED!

**Fun Fact:** The University of Queensland team's 2,884 supernovae include objects seen when the universe was less than a billion years old, right up to recent times. That means their study spans roughly 90% of cosmic history — a timeline so long that any single project leader would need more than one career just to observe all those supernovae by themselves!
