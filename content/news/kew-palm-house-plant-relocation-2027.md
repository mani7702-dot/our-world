# 4 reasons Kew's giant Victorian glasshouse is sending 1,300 tropical plants on vacation

Picture a building so big it could hold a small village — and inside it, a jungle so steamy that the glass sweats and the iron bones beneath slowly rust away. That is Kew's Palm House, one of the world's oldest and grandest glass greenhouses, and right now it needs a major makeover.

The tricky part? You can't just shut the door and start hammering. Inside live **1,300 tropical plants** — some of them ancient, rare, and delicate. Before any restoration work can begin, every single one of them has to move out, like a giant botanical house move.

## 1. A greenhouse built like a palace

The Palm House was finished in **1848**, and when it opened, nothing like it had ever been built. It was the first big building to use wrought iron as its main structure — a technique that would later make the Eiffel Tower possible. The soaring glass sheets let in sunlight, while the iron frame held up a tropical climate for plants that could never survive outside on a London morning.

## 2. Moisture is both friend and foe

The same hot, steamy air that lets tropical plants thrive is slowly destroying the building. For 175 years, warm moisture has been creeping into the iron joints, causing them to rust from the inside out. The glass panes, too, have grown old and cracked. It is like a bathtub that has been running for nearly two centuries — eventually, something has to give.

## 3. The Great Plant Relocation

Before builders can lift a hammer, the Palm House must be **completely emptied**. That means carefully packing and moving 1,300 tropical plants — including a 175-year-old cycad that is the oldest potted plant in the world. It is like planning the world's most delicate moving day: each plant needs just the right temperature, the right light, and the right amount of water. They will spend the next few years in temporary homes while their house gets a full renovation.

## 4. A greener rebuild for the next 175 years

The £66 million restoration will not just fix what is broken — it will make the Palm House **carbon-neutral** for the future. That means replacing 16,000 glass panes with modern, energy-efficient ones, repairing the rusting ironwork, and upgrading systems so the greenhouse uses far less energy. When it reopens, the plants can return to a home that is both ancient and brand new.

The whole project starts in autumn 2027, and the Palm House will be closed for up to five years. That is a very long vacation for 1,300 plants — but when they come home, their house will be ready for another 175 years of growth.

## Try this at home

Fill a glass jar with hot water, then screw on the lid while it is still steaming. Watch the sides fog up as tiny water droplets form — that is the same moisture that has been slowly rusting Kew's Palm House for 175 years. The warm, wet air loves to condense on cooler surfaces. Now imagine that happening to a giant iron frame every single day, year after year.

> **Fun Fact:** The world's oldest living pot plant lives in the Palm House — a cycad named *Encephalartos altensteinii* that was moved there in **1848**, the same year the building opened. It is over 175 years old!
