## A prediction that gripped the world

On November 13, 2026 — that is, in just a few days — something dramatic was supposed to happen. Not a holiday, not a sports final, but the day human population would supposedly hit infinity.

In 1960, the journal *Science* published a paper with a title no one could forget: **"Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026."** The authors — Heinz von Foerster, an Austrian-American cybernetics professor, plus two of his students — used math to argue that the human population was growing not just exponentially but super-exponentially. At that rate, the numbers would climb toward infinity right on that future Friday.

"Our great-great-grandchildren will not starve," they wrote. "They will be squeezed to death."

The paper grabbed headlines in *Time* and *The New York Times*, inspired *Pogo* comic strips, and got cited in textbooks for decades. It even popped up in a 1971 Supreme Court abortion case as "evidence" of a population crisis.

But here we are, and the world did not end. What happened?

## The math that looked right

Von Foerster and his team had a serious idea hiding behind a shocking headline. They argued that humans are special: instead of just consuming resources, we also create "coalitions" that solve problems. More people packed together meant more brains working together.

So unlike normal exponential growth — where numbers double, then double again — human growth might accelerate *faster and faster*. Plot that on a graph, and it curves up into a towering shape that hits infinity at a finite date: November 13, 2026.

The wild part? Their model matched real population data far better than experts expected. For over thirty years, their "doomsday equation" tracked the actual growth of humanity with surprising precision. Textbooks taught it as gospel.

## Why the clock stopped

Everything started slipping around 1993 — about halfway to the predicted doomsday. The global population began falling behind the forecasts. Why? Because the world did what the authors themselves had suggested: birth rates dropped.

In 1960, the average woman had about five children. Today, that number is closer to 2.4 — and in many countries, even lower. As families chose to have fewer children, the "super-exponential" curve flattened out. The prediction that once seemed bulletproof quietly unraveled.

## What we learned

Before Nov. 13 rolls around and we all sigh in relief, there's a bigger lesson here. The doomsday paper showed how a good scientific model — complete with dense equations — could capture attention and seem right for decades. But it also showed how even rigorous math can be derailed by the single most unpredictable variable of all: human behavior.

Scientists still use population models today, but now they know to treat predictions like doomsday headlines — fascinating, maybe useful, but never a guarantee.

**Try this at home:** Grab a calculator or spreadsheet. Type "2" and keep doubling it: 2, 4, 8, 16... You will reach a million fast. Now try the opposite: start with a big number and subtract a growing amount each step. See how the slowdown changes everything? Real growth is rarely a straight shot.

**Fun fact:** Heinz von Foerster was once a stage magician before becoming a serious scientist. He used that experience to craft the doomsday paper's dramatic title and conclusion — borrowing the trick of "the setup, the extraordinary turn, and the final reveal that returns things to normal." In this case, the reveal was that the world simply grew more slowly than the math expected.
