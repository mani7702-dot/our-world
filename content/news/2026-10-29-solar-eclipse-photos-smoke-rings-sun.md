# What did the 2026 solar eclipse reveal hiding in the Sun's atmosphere?

## A brief window into the invisible corona

On August 12, 2026, a total solar eclipse swept across parts of Europe and Africa. For just a few minutes, the Moon slid perfectly in front of the Sun, blocking its blinding light and revealing the Sun's ghostly outer atmosphere — the corona — in stunning detail. Now, the first photos from that eclipse have been released, and they show something incredible: a giant hole in the Sun's magnetic field and mysterious "smoke rings" swirling through the solar corona.

## The giant coronal hole

The clearest feature in the new eclipse photos is a large **coronal hole** — a dark, cavernous region where the Sun's magnetic field lines splay open into space instead of looping back to the surface. Coronal holes look dark in X-ray and extreme-ultraviolet images because they are cooler and less dense than the surrounding corona. But their emptiness matters: they are the **source of the fast solar wind** — streams of charged particles racing outward at up to 800 km/s (about 500 miles per second), more than twice as fast as the slow solar wind that flows everywhere else.

These high-speed gusts buffet Earth's magnetic field as the Sun rotates, sometimes triggering beautiful auroras and sometimes causing geomagnetic storms that can affect satellites and power grids. Because coronal holes can last for weeks or even months, scientists can forecast these space-weather effects far in advance.

## The mysterious smoke rings

The most puzzling discovery in the eclipse photos is a series of **vortex-like "smoke rings"** floating in the corona. These twisted plasma structures — formally called coronal vortices — were first spotted in eclipse photos from 2008, but until now, scientists could only guess at what formed them.

The new images, captured by the **Solar Wind Sherpas** project led by Professor Miloslav Druckmüller of Brno University of Technology, show the smoke rings in remarkable detail. The researchers now believe the rings form because of **temperature differences** inside the coronal plasma. Hot material flows along the Sun's magnetic field lines, and when streams of different temperatures meet, they create swirling vortices — like eddies in a river where warm and cold water collide. These vortices then stretch along the magnetic field lines, stretching hundreds of thousands of kilometers out into space, connecting the solar wind to our own doorstep.

## Why eclipses beat space telescopes

You might wonder: why do we need a solar eclipse at all? Can't telescopes in space see the corona?

Space-based telescopes like NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory use instruments called **coronagraphs** that artificially block the Sun's disk. But Earth's atmosphere blurs those artificial edges, and the artificial occulting disk can never be as clean as the Moon's shadow. During a total eclipse, the Moon provides a **perfect natural occulting disk** — and the corona appears sharper and more detailed than any coronagraph image can match. That is why eclipse chasers and solar scientists alike consider total eclipses the ultimate laboratory for studying the Sun.

## Try this at home

Fill a bathtub with water, then add a few drops of food coloring near one edge and a few drops of milk near the opposite edge. The milk makes the water cloudy (the "plasma"), while the food coloring shows how different-density fluids flow. Watch as the colored streams meet and begin to spiral — that is your mini version of the Sun's smoke rings, caused by the same temperature and density differences playing out on a solar scale.

## Fun Fact

The Sun's corona is about **3 million degrees Celsius** (5.4 million °F) — millions of degrees hotter than the Sun's surface, which is only about 5,500 °C. Scientists still don't fully understand what heats the corona to such extreme temperatures, but the smoke rings and coronal holes seen in eclipse photos are helping them narrow down the candidates.

## for-grownups:
Coronal holes are regions of open magnetic field in the Sun's corona that emit low levels of UV and X-ray radiation, appearing dark in extreme-ultraviolet imagery. Wikipedia confirms that the fast solar wind (450–800 km/s) originates in coronal holes, while the slow solar wind (250–450 km/s) flows elsewhere in the corona. NASA's Solar Wind pages confirm that solar wind speeds reach 800 km/s over coronal holes. The "smoke ring" vortices observed during the August 12, 2026 total solar eclipse were identified by the Solar Wind Sherpas project (coordinated by the University of Hawaii) using high-resolution composite photography by Miloslav Druckmüller of Brno University of Technology. These coronal vortices were first reported in 2008 eclipse imagery and are now confirmed to arise from temperature-gradient-driven plasma flows along magnetic field lines. The total solar eclipse provides superior coronagraphic resolution compared to space-based coronagraphs because the Moon's shadow creates a clean, sharp occulting disk — unlike the diffraction-limited artificial occulters used on satellites like SDO.
