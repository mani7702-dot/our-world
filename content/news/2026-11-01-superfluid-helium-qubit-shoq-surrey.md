## Could a Weird Form of Liquid Helium Make Quantum Computers Less Glitchy?

Quantum computers could one day solve problems that ordinary computers find impossible. But they have a big weakness. The tiny parts that store their information are incredibly sensitive — a stray electric charge or a bit of electromagnetic "static" can scramble everything.

Now a team at the **University of Surrey** in the UK has an unusual idea. What if the building block of a quantum computer was made not from a solid metal circuit, but from a **strange liquid that flows without any friction at all**?

## A quantum bit with a slippery twist

Ordinary computers store information as **bits** — switches that are either 0 or 1. Quantum computers use **qubits**, which can be a blend of both at once. That is what makes them so powerful, and also so fragile.

Most qubits today are built from **superconducting circuits**. They work well, but they are easily disturbed by **electromagnetic noise** — think of the static that makes your hair stick to a balloon. Even a tiny disturbance can spoil the information.

The Surrey team tried a different approach. They propose a qubit built from **superfluid helium** — a special form of helium that can flow with **zero friction**. The catch: it only behaves this way at extremely cold temperatures, close to **-273°C** (absolute zero).

## Why no charge is a big deal

The clever part is that superfluid helium is **charge-neutral** — it carries no electric charge. Because of that, the team says their design could be **naturally protected** from some of the electromagnetic noise that bothers today's qubits.

Their proposed device is called the **Superfluid Helium Oscillator Quantum (SHOQ) device**. In it, the superfluid's motion is **quantized** — it can only slosh in certain allowed amounts, a little like stairs you can only stand on step by step, never between them.

"The maths tells us that it should work," says lead author **Dr Priya Sharma**. "We have taken what we already know about superfluid helium and quantum technologies and turned that into an educated design." Their calculations suggest the device could have error rates around **100 times lower** than standard superconducting qubits.

## Teamwork, not replacement

The researchers do not think their invention would replace today's quantum computers. Instead, it could work **alongside** them. Different kinds of qubits could each do the jobs they are best at.

"Superfluid helium gives us a fundamentally different type of quantum hardware to explore," says co-author **Dr Eran Ginossar**.

"This is the first reported design for a superfluid-based qubit," the team notes — but so far it exists only on paper. The next step is to **build a working prototype** and test the predictions.

## Try this at home

You can see the "no friction" idea for yourself. Slide a book across a carpet — it drags and stops quickly. Now slide a toy car across a smooth, icy-feeling table — it glides much farther. Superfluids take this to the extreme: a superfluid stirred in a bowl would keep swirling **forever**, because nothing inside it ever slows down.

**Fun fact:** Superfluid helium can creep *up the inside walls* of a container and escape over the top — it refuses to stay put!
