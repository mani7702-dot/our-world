Goudier Island, a small rocky island in the bay, was used for whaling between 1911 and 1931, then established as the British Antarctic Survey's 'Base B' (Port Lockroy) during World War II as part of Operation Tabarin. The base closed in 1962 and was renovated by the British Antarctic Survey starting in 1996. Since 2006, the UK Antarctic Heritage Trust (UKAHT) has operated Port Lockroy as a museum and post office during the Antarctic summer (October to March), making it the world's most southerly operational post office. Each season, a team of six volunteers live and work on Goudier Island, conserving the historic buildings, maintaining the museum, and sorting thousands of postcards and letters for visitors and researchers. About 1,000 gentoo penguins (Pygoscelis papua) nest on the island each summer. Gentoo penguins are one of only three penguin species whose populations are growing, thanks to their flexible diet of fish, krill, squid, and cuttlefish. The post office is open from late October to mid-March; mail is transported north by ship to the Falkland Islands for global distribution. Port Lockroy sits at approximately 64°49'S, making it the southernmost permanently staffed location in Antarctica that is open to the public."

# Who runs the world's most southerly post office — and why is a penguin the boss?

## A postcard from the bottom of the world

For most of us, posting a letter means walking to the end of the street. But for George Clarke, it means flying 8,000 miles to the most southerly post office on Earth — and sharing his workspace with 1,000 noisy penguins.

Every year from late October to March, George swaps his Bristol flat for a tiny island in Antarctica called **Goudier Island**. There, inside a red wooden hut that has stood since the 1940s, he sorts thousands of postcards as the island's postmaster.

The penguins? They are the unofficial building inspectors.

## Penguins, paintbrushes, and preservation

George isn't the only worker on Goudier Island. He lives and works with a team of **six volunteers** who spend five months of the year at **Port Lockroy** — the world's southernmost operational post office.

Their job goes far beyond stamping mail. They are also museum managers, building conservators, and wildlife monitors. Together, they keep the historic hut standing strong against the harsh Antarctic wind, repair rusting tools, and count penguin chicks so scientists can track how the population is doing.

The island is home to about **1,000 gentoo penguins** who waddle right up to the post office door. "The penguins are noisy and smelly," George says with a laugh, "but the people are fantastic."

## How does the mail get home?

Unlike your local postie, there are no delivery vans at Port Lockroy. Once all the postcards are sorted, weighed, and bagged, the team has to ask passing ships to carry them north.

"We look at which ships are heading towards the Falklands," George explains. "Then we call them up on the radio and ask, 'Would you mind taking some postcards with you?' And they're always happy to help."

From the Falkland Islands, the mail travels around the world — carrying a special postmark that proves it came from one of the coldest, windiest, and most beautiful places on Earth.

## Try this at home

Want to write to the southernmost post office? During the summer season (October to March), you can send a postcard to **Port Lockroy, Antarctic Peninsula** and it will arrive back in the world with a special Antarctic postmark. Ask an adult to help you look up the address!

### Fun Fact

Port Lockroy sits at **64°49' south** — that's closer to the South Pole than to the equator. The tiny hut where George works was originally built during **World War Two** as part of a secret British mission to claim Antarctica for the Allies. Today, it hosts the only post office in the world where penguins wander through the mailroom!
