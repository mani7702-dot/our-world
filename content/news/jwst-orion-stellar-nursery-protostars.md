## A cosmic nursery hides behind a veil of dust

If you look up at Orion the Hunter on a crisp winter night, you might spot a fuzzy "star" in his sword. That fuzzy patch is the **Orion Nebula** — a glowing cloud called a *stellar nursery*, where new stars are born.

But the nebula has a secret: most of the action happens inside thick, cold clouds of gas and dust that block visible light. It's like trying to watch a movie through a thick blanket.

## How JWST sees through the blanket

The James Webb Space Telescope has a superpower: **infrared vision**. Infrared light slips right through the dusty blanket that hides the nursery's baby stars, revealing what's happening inside.

JWST recently stared at a small slice of the vast Orion A complex — a region called **OMC-2** — that lies 1,267 light-years from Earth. By the time that light reached us, humans had only just invented the telegraph, so imagine how far away that really is!

The region JWST examined spans just 2.5 by 1.5 light-years — tiny compared to the full cloud, but close enough to see incredible detail.

## Baby stars shoot out speedy jets

Inside OMC-2's cold clouds, gravity slowly tugs on pockets of gas and dust. As a pocket grows denser, gravity wins and the cloud collapses — this is how a **protostar** forms.

But here's the amazing part: as the collapsing cloud spins faster (conserving angular momentum), it flattens into a spinning disk called an **accretion disk**. Powerful magnetic fields in the disk's inner region channel some of the material into super-fast **jets** that blast out along the baby star's rotation axis — like cosmic fireman's hoses shooting out at hundreds of thousands of miles per hour.

When those jets crash into the surrounding dust and gas, they light up in sharp, glowing streaks that JWST's infrared eye captures as colorful wisps across the image. Most appear reddish, while younger protostars still getting their feet wet blaze a brighter white.

## What the grownup stars will become

A few of the bright dots in JWST's image — marked with six symmetrical spikes from the telescope's mirrors — have already cleared away their birth blankets. These are **pre-main-sequence stars**, the grown-ups of this cosmic nursery. They show us what tomorrow's OMC-2 protostars will grow up to look like.

## Try this at home

Find the Orion Nebula this winter: look for the fuzzy middle "star" in Orion's sword. With a small telescope or even good binoculars, you're seeing a stellar nursery 1,267 light-years away! To simulate how dust blocks light, shine a flashlight through a foggy jar of water — visible light scatters, but if you could see infrared, the "stars" behind the fog would become clear.

## Fun Fact

The brightest stars in the Orion Nebula form the **Trapezium Cluster** — four stars that look like a tiny trapezoid even through small telescopes. These hot, young stars are the ones lighting up the entire nebula.
