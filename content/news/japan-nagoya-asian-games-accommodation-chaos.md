Japan is famous for running big events smoothly — the 2020 Tokyo Olympics went almost flawlessly, and football fans still talk about how punctual and polite everything feels. So when the **2026 Asian Games** kicked off in **Nagoya** and the wider **Aichi Prefecture** in September, the chaos that followed surprised a lot of people.

## A room problem from day one

Before the first event had even started, athletes and officials were already complaining. There were simply **too few hotel rooms**, and the ones that existed were not great quality either.

Organizers had decided to **save money** by skipping the old-fashioned "athletes' village" — a big gated neighbourhood of flats that most big games build. Instead, they put people in **hotels** and **container-style units** spread across the city. The plan looked neat on paper, but it left very little room for mistakes.

The mistake came fast: as the weeks went by, more and more sports decided to join the party late. That pushed the total number of guests up by about **2,000**, from 15,000 to **17,000**. Suddenly the city's bedrooms were booked out. Organizers said there was "simply nowhere" to put the extra people, and told late-added teams to make their own arrangements. A day later they changed their tune, apologising and promising everyone would get a bed.

## The Italian cruise ship to the rescue

When hotel rooms ran out, Japan borrowed an idea from maritime history: park a **cruise ship** in the harbour. That ship had **1,500 cabins**, enough to house a small army of athletes. A top official from South Korea joked that tall basketball players were struggling to "stretch their legs out" on the container beds — a fair complaint when your sport makes height an advantage.

It was a creative fix, but it did not hide the fact that the planning had fallen behind. At a press conference, a deputy director of the Games admitted the team had faced a "range of unforeseen issues" — the polite way of saying things had got messy.

## Why it matters (and how they fixed it)

Professor Kozo Tomiyama, who studies big sports events at Osaka University, says the trouble started when organizers could not handle surprises. His worry: if the chaos damages how other countries see Japan's ability to host, future events might look elsewhere.

On the bright side, organizers said they listened fast. Feedback was coming in from comment fields online, where some people were calling it the "worst Games in history" and demanding a full investigation. Rather than ignoring the complaints, the team promised to take on board every suggestion and make improvements. By the second week, hotel chaos reports had mostly stopped — the cruise ship was full, the container beds had extra mattresses, and the last-minute teams finally had somewhere stable to sleep.

## Fun fact

Nagoya has never hosted the full Asian Games before — only parts of them. The 2026 edition was also notable for being the first "decentralised" Games, with events spread across Nagoya, Tokyo, Osaka, and several smaller Aichi towns, rather than gathered in one compact zone.

### Try this at home

Ask an adult to help you build a tiny city out of cardboard boxes. Then add one new resident (a toy figure) for every friend who shows up **late** — without planning ahead, watch how quickly you run out of roofs, beds, and even dinner chairs. That is the same problem the Nagoya organizers faced!
