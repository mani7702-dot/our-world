Cimberley Frost knew something was wrong because the flat went quiet.

Her budgie Luna had been chattering at the family while they ate in the living room. Then the noise stopped. When Cimberley looked in the kitchen, the window was open - blown wide by the strong winds that followed Storm Boelo - and the little green bird was gone.

Luna lives in Northampton, England. She is eleven months old, and this was her first time ever outside.

## A beak in a pipe

For a while, nobody knew where she was. Then a driver passing along a road about a mile (1.6 km) from the flat noticed a small bird hopping down into a drain at the side of the road, and rang the RSPCA.

On 26 October, RSPCA inspector Richard Durant and trainee animal rescue officer Rosie Cowie went to look. They peered into the drain - and a tiny beak was peeking out of a pipe.

"I gently grabbed her and popped her in a pet carrier," Rosie said. It was her very first rescue.

## Why go down instead of up?

When a small bird is frightened, the safe thing to do is not to fly higher. It is to get into cover - something dark and narrow where a beak cannot follow. A drain looks exactly like that from above, which is why the RSPCA inspector called it Luna's "hidey hole".

The trouble is that cover is not food. Budgies in the wild eat seeds of grasses and small plants, picked up off the ground, and they need fresh water every day. A street drain has neither. Luna was lucky in one very specific way: a driver happened to be watching at the exact moment she hopped down.

## The reunion

Back home, Cimberley left the window open in case Luna came back, played budgie sounds outside to guide her, and posted that she was missing.

That last part worked. Someone got in touch to say the RSPCA had picked up a budgie nearby, and officer Ellie Grindod brought Luna home. "The house was so quiet without her," Cimberley said. "Now she's back to being lady of the house."

## If a pet bird ever gets out

The RSPCA's lost-pet advice is the same whether the animal has feathers or fur:

- Ring nearby vet surgeries and animal hospitals and ask if anything has been handed in.
- Register the pet as lost on a lost-and-found service such as Animal Search UK or Petslocated, and check the "found" listings too.
- Post on local social media - that is exactly how Luna was found.
- Ask neighbours to check sheds, garages and outbuildings.

And the boring, brilliant fix: check the cage latch, and keep windows shut on windy days. Luna's owner put it best - "It's her first adventure, and hopefully her last."

**For grown-ups:** The budgerigar (*Melopsittacus undulatus*) is a small parrot, 17-18 cm long, native to the dry inland of Australia. Wild budgies are nomadic: flocks follow rainfall and food, sometimes gathering in the tens of thousands after a good season, and they feed almost entirely on seeds of native grasses and herbs taken from the ground. Captive breeding since the 1840s has produced the white, blue, yellow, mauve and grey colour forms sold as pets, but a pet bird keeps the wild bird's two calls - a warbling contact chirrup and a sharp "zit" of alarm - and its wild instinct to drop into cover when threatened. That instinct explains the drain: a frightened psittacine flees downward into a dark cavity rather than upward into open sky, which is also why escaped pet birds are so often found under cars, in hedges, or in drains. Luna's case is a reminder that a lost pet bird is not usually far away; it is usually hidden, silent, and within a few streets. It is also a reminder of the limit of that strategy - a seed-eater with no access to grass seed or clean water is on a clock, and finding her depended entirely on a stranger looking down at the right second.
