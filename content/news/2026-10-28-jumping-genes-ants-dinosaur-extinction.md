## Did the asteroid that killed the dinosaurs help ants take over the world?

You have probably watched an ant carry something ten times its own weight across a pavement. It looks unstoppable. It is.

Ants are the biggest family of social insects on Earth, with **more than 15,000 species** — from leaf-cutters in rainforests to tiny ones living inside acorns. They run tunnels under cities, farm fungus, and raid picnics on every continent except Antarctica.

A new study suggests part of the reason they got so good at this is hidden in their DNA, in scraps of genetic code that can copy and paste themselves. And the timing is strange: those copy-and-paste bursts seem to line up with the moment the dinosaurs disappeared.

## First, what is a "jumping gene"?

Your DNA is a long instruction book for building and running your body. Most of it is carefully organised — genes that make muscles, eyes, or the stuff that carries oxygen in your blood.

But a big share of the book is made of **transposable elements**, often nicknamed **jumping genes**. These are stretches of DNA that can lift themselves out of one spot and paste themselves into another. Sometimes they copy themselves first, so the number of copies grows.

Imagine a sentence in a book that can photocopy itself and glue the copy into a random page. Do that enough times and the book gets messy — but occasionally a copy lands somewhere useful, and the book says something new.

Scientists used to call this kind of DNA "junk". They don't anymore.

## What the scientists actually did

A team led by Dr Lukas Schrader at the University of Münster in Germany, working with researchers in several countries, compared the DNA of **163 ant species**, drawn from **12 of the 16 ant groups** alive today. That let them trace what the jumping genes had been doing over the past **100 million years**.

Two patterns came out:

- **Ant groups with more jumping genes today are also the ones with more species.** The messiest genomes belong to the biggest families.
- **The bursts happened at the same time in different groups.** Several major ant lineages had their own separate explosions of jumping-gene activity in the early part of the age of mammals — right around **66 million years ago**. Those bursts came *before* those groups split into thousands of species.

That date is the eye-opener. Sixty-six million years ago is when a huge asteroid struck Earth and ended the age of the dinosaurs. Forests changed, whole groups of animals vanished, and the survivors found empty space to grow into — including the space under the feet of the animals that were left.

## Why smelling well helped

Ants barely use their eyes to talk to each other. They use **smell**. A chemical signal on another ant's body tells a worker whether that ant is a nestmate or an intruder, whether a trail leads to food, or whether the queen needs anything.

The study found that the gene families involved in this chemical communication grew alongside the jumping genes. More tools for smelling, more ways to be a different kind of ant.

The team's idea is that the chaos after the asteroid left room for new species — and jumping genes may have supplied fresh genetic material for evolution to sort through. It is a real mechanism, not a magic wand: genes that made a colony better at finding food or defending a nest could spread.

**One honest caveat.** The study shows a strong **pattern**, not a smoking gun. The researchers found that jumping-gene numbers match species numbers, and that the bursts happened just before the big expansions. That is a strong clue — but nobody has watched the asteroid make an ant genome jump, because it happened 66 million years ago.

## Try this at home

**The copy-paste game.** Write a short sentence on a piece of paper: *The ant carried a crumb home.* Now copy one word from that sentence and write the copy in a random new place. Repeat five times. Your sentence is now mostly duplicates — messy, and a bit silly.

Then try it once more, but this time only keep a copy if it makes the sentence more interesting. That is close to what happens in a real genome: plenty of copies do nothing at all, a few cause trouble, and every so often one turns out to help.

**Ant watch (no touching needed).** Put a tiny crumb of plain biscuit on a pavement or patio and sit a few steps back for ten minutes. If ants find it, watch how they move. When two ants meet, they tap antennae together — that is them swapping smell information. You are watching the sense the study says got extra gene support.
