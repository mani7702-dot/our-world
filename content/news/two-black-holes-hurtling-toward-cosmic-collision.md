# Two monster black holes could smash together in just 100 years

## A galaxy with not one, but two cosmic vacuum cleaners

At the heart of a distant galaxy called **Mrk 501** lives something most people do not even know exists: a **supermassive black hole**. It is a blob of space so dense — millions of times heavier than our Sun packed into a ball smaller than a city — that not even light can escape once it gets too close.

But now, astronomers have discovered something even stranger. That same galaxy is hiding a **second** monster black hole, orbiting its bigger sibling like a dance partner. And if the two keep circling closer and closer, they could **crash together in as little as 100 years**.

## What is a black hole's "jet"?

Black holes are messy eaters. When a black hole pulls in gas and dust, the material does not fall straight in. Instead, it swirls around like water going down a drain, heating up to millions of degrees. Before anything crosses the point of no return, the crushing gravity and magnetic fields fling some of that material **straight out** in narrow beams called **jets**.

These jets shoot into space at **nearly the speed of light** — faster than anything you have ever seen. Some are so bright that astronomers can spot them from across the entire universe.

## The surprise in the data

Astronomers have studied Mrk 501 for decades and always saw the same single jet — a bright beam pointing almost directly at Earth. It was easy to spot.

But when a team led by **Silke Britzen** from the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy dug into **23 years** of radio-telescope data, they found something hidden in plain sight: a **second jet**. It points in a different direction, which is why no one noticed it before.

Even better — over the course of just a few weeks, the team watched this second jet **move**. It seemed to pop out from behind the main black hole, sweep counterclockwise in a slow arc, then hide again. The pattern repeated, like a clock that ticks once every **121 days**.

That rhythm — that steady, repeating motion — is the signature of something incredible: a **binary dance**. Two black holes, each with its own jet, orbiting each other as they slowly spiral inward.

> **Try this at home:** Hold two magnets on the ends of your finger and thumb, then bring them close. Feel how they push and pull? That is a tiny taste of what two black holes do to each other across the vast emptiness of space.

## When will they crash?

Black holes are heavy, and heavy things orbiting each other slowly lose energy — first to the stretching of space itself, then (once they are very close) to ripples called **gravitational waves**.

At today's rate, the two monsters in Mrk 501 will keep tightening their orbit. Depending on exactly how heavy each one is, they could **merge in as little as 100 years**. That sounds like forever, but in the life of a galaxy it is the blink of an eye.

And here is the exciting part: when they finally collide, they will send ripples through space-time itself — like a stone dropped in a pond, but across the whole universe. Future space telescopes like **LISA** (a gravitational-wave observatory launching soon) will be able to "hear" that crash, even though it happens 100 years from now.

## Fun Fact

The galaxy Mrk 501 is so bright in gamma rays that it has its own nickname: **"the brightest hard-TeV source in the sky."** It has been studied intensely for decades — and it still had a secret.

## For Grown-ups

Supermassive binary black holes are expected to form when galaxies collide and their central black holes sink to the center of the new galaxy. But spotting a close pair while it is still orbiting — before they merge — is rare. The detection of a second, differently pointed radio jet in Mrk 501, tracked over ~23 years of multi-frequency radio observations, provides direct evidence for such a system. The orbital modulation period is ~121 days; an independent analysis (arXiv:2210.15884, Magallanes-Guijón & Mendoza 2022, J. Galaxies) found a ~224-day periodic signal in the same source. Both point to a supermassive binary in Mrk 501, a prime target for future gravitational-wave missions like LISA. Results accepted for publication in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*.
