# How Solar Orbiter Traced Mysterious Magnetic Kinks Back to the Sun

## A giant S-shaped kink in space

Imagine the Sun is constantly blowing a stream of invisible, super-hot particles toward Earth and beyond. This stream — called the **solar wind** — carries with it the Sun's magnetic field, like ribbons dancing in an invisible breeze. Sometimes, those magnetic ribbons get twisted into giant **S-shaped kinks** called **switchbacks**. These features are so large that a spacecraft could spend an hour and a half sailing right through one — yet their shape and origin remained a mystery until now.

In a breakthrough published in *Nature Astronomy*, scientists on the **Solar Orbiter** mission traced a switchback all the way back to its birthplace on the Sun, revealing secrets about our star's restless magnetic personality and the space weather that can affect satellites and astronauts.

## Following the chemical fingerprint home

The switchback that gave up its secrets was spotted on **March 2, 2022**, when ESA's Solar Orbiter was cruising through space about halfway between Earth and the Sun. Its **Solar Wind Analyser (SWA)** instrument sampled the particles inside the kink, looking for clues about where they came from.

The key was in the chemistry. The switchback contained unusually high levels of charged **oxygen** and **carbon** ions — particles that had been stripped of their electrons by heat exceeding **one million degrees Celsius**. That temperature can only exist inside the Sun's **corona**, the super-hot outer atmosphere. As researchers cross-referenced the timing with images from NASA's **Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO)**, they mapped the plasma back to a dark, cooler patch on the Sun's surface called a **coronal hole** — a region where magnetic field lines fling open, allowing solar wind to escape.

## The snap that slingshots plasma to space

Coronal holes are where the Sun's magnetic field lines stretch out like highways into space. Nearby, closed loops of magnetic field arch back down to the surface. When an open field line and a closed loop meet, something dramatic happens: the lines **snap apart and reconnect** in a process called **interchange reconnection**. During this cosmic "Velcro pop," the hot plasma that was trapped in the loop gets slingshotted out into space — creating the switchback feature.

This discovery reconciled two competing theories about how switchbacks form. Some scientists thought they were born from waves and turbulence deep in the solar wind itself, while others believed they originated at the Sun. The Solar Orbiter data shows it is **both** — reconnection gives the switchback its kick from the Sun, then waves and turbulence govern how it travels through space toward us.

## Try this at home

Stand in a quiet room with a friend. Hold one end of a Slinky spring taut and have your friend hold the other. Twist one section of the spring so it kinks, then let go. The twist ripples down the spring — that ripple is like a magnetic switchback traveling through the solar wind. You can also try this with a garden hose: turn the water on, then give the nozzle a quick flick. The kink you see racing down the hose is a tiny version of what Solar Orbiter saw in space!

## Fun Fact

The switchback Solar Orbiter studied was enormous — about **2.75 million kilometers** across. That is roughly **six times the distance from Earth to the Moon**, yet it is still smaller than many hurricanes on Earth. The Sun produces thousands of switchbacks like this every day, meaning the entire solar system is filled with these invisible S-shaped magnetic kinks!

## For the grown-ups

ESA's Solar Orbiter spacecraft, during a serendipitous encounter on March 2, 2022, flew through a giant magnetic switchback roughly halfway between Earth and the Sun. Using its Solar Wind Analyser (SWA) instrument, researchers led by Jesse Coburn (CNRS/LPP, France) sampled the plasma within the switchback and discovered elevated levels of charged oxygen and carbon ions that had been stripped of electrons at temperatures exceeding one million degrees Celsius — a signature only possible in the Sun's corona. Cross-referencing with NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory confirmed the plasma originated near a coronal hole, where open magnetic field lines allow solar wind to escape. The team identified interchange reconnection — where open and closed magnetic field lines snap and reconnect — as the formation mechanism, reconciling two competing theories by showing reconnection launches the switchback at the Sun while waves and turbulence shape its later journey. The switchback measured approximately 2.75 million kilometers (about 6 Earth-Moon distances), and the findings were published as "On the Coronal Origin of Magnetic Switchbacks in the Solar Wind" in Nature Astronomy (DOI: 10.1038/s41550-026-02928-0). Switchbacks were first discovered by the NASA-ESA Ulysses mission and subsequently studied by Parker Solar Probe beginning in 2018.
