## A surprise on a rainy road

Fire salamanders are one of the best-known amphibians in Europe: glossy black skin, bright yellow splotches, a slow walk, and a taste for damp woodland. People have been writing them down in books for more than 250 years. If any animal in Europe had been fully described, you would think it was this one.

Then Bernat Burriel-Carranza, an evolutionary biologist at the Natural Sciences Museum of Barcelona, started carrying an ultraviolet flashlight on his evening field trips. On a rainy night in Spain he found a fire salamander crossing a road, pointed the beam at it, and saw something nobody had reported before.

Along the animal's sides and belly, its yellow spots lit up in a scatter of green-blue points, like a tiny constellation sitting on a wet road.

The team went looking properly, in Spain and Germany, from April 2024 to November 2025. Their results were published in the journal Royal Society Open Science.

## It is glowing, but not making its own light

There is a word for this, and it is easy to mix up with a different one.

**Bioluminescence** is when an animal makes its own light with a chemical reaction inside its body. Fireflies do this. So do many deep-sea fish.

**Biofluorescence** is different. The animal cannot glow in the dark on its own. It needs a light shining on it. Special molecules in its skin soak up light we cannot see, called ultraviolet, and then re-emit that energy as light we can see, in green and cyan.

Turn the ultraviolet light off, and the glow stops.

So the fire salamander was never hiding a lamp. It has been wearing something like a high-visibility jacket that only shows up under a light most animals cannot make. That is why 250 years of scientists looking at salamanders in ordinary daylight missed it completely.

## Glowing goo

The glow was strongest along the flanks, throat and belly, sitting right on top of the yellow spots, and it lined up with the salamander's skin glands.

That turned out to be the interesting part. When the team tested the slippery secretions from those glands, the secretions glowed too. According to the coverage of the study, they were still glowing more than 24 hours after leaving the salamander's body.

The researchers also looked at preserved specimens under a microscope and found the fluorescent compounds inside gland tissue and in the blood. That suggests the glowing molecules are not painted on the surface like a sticker; they travel around inside the animal. Only a few other amphibians are known to do this.

## Why glow at all?

Here is the honest answer: nobody knows yet.

The scientists have two main ideas. The first is a warning. Fire salamanders are genuinely poisonous, their skin makes a toxin that makes predators spit them out, and a glow that matches their yellow spots might work as a reminder in the dark: this one tastes terrible. The second is a kind of signpost, helping salamanders find each other or choose a mate on a forest floor at night.

Both are plausible. Neither has been tested yet. Being able to say "we do not know" is part of how science works, and this is a good story to keep in mind the next time someone tells you that everything about a common animal is already known.

## The fire part is a myth, sort of

The name has nothing to do with glowing. For centuries people believed salamanders could survive fire, or were even born in it. The likely reason is simple: salamanders like damp, dark places, including piles of logs. When someone picked up an armful of firewood and threw it on the fire, out ran a salamander. It looked like the animal had come out of the flames.

Fire salamanders are not fireproof, and please do not test that. They are also poisonous to eat, so the right move with any wild amphibian is the same: look, do not touch, and wash your hands if you accidentally do.

## Try this at home

You can see biofluorescence without a salamander, using things from the kitchen and a blacklight, which is an ultraviolet torch. Ask a grown-up first, and never shine an ultraviolet light at anyone's eyes or your own, because ultraviolet is exactly the kind of light that sunburn comes from.

1. Pour tonic water into a clear glass and put it somewhere dark. Shine the blacklight on it. It glows pale blue.
2. Draw on paper with a yellow highlighter, let it dry, and shine the light on the drawing.
3. Try a banana, a leaf, honey or olive oil, then compare which ones glow and which just sit there looking normal.

Tonic water glows because of a molecule called quinine, which absorbs ultraviolet light and re-emits it as blue light. That is the same trick the salamander's skin is doing: absorb the invisible, give back the visible.
