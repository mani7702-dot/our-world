# Could a coral-eating starfish actually help a reef?

## Meet the reef's prickliest muncher

The **crown-of-thorns starfish** is one of the most feared animals on a coral reef. It is covered in sharp spines that hold a mild venom, and it eats coral. When these starfish show up in huge numbers, they can strip a reef down to bare white skeleton.

So reef managers spend a lot of effort removing them. That is why a new study from the University of Sydney sounds so surprising: in small numbers, this same starfish may be quietly helping reefs stay healthy.

## What the scientists actually saw

A team led by Dr Shawna Foo studied feeding scars at One Tree Island Research Station on Australia's Great Barrier Reef. They did not look at a starfish outbreak. They looked at places where fish and other predators keep starfish numbers naturally low.

The result: live coral tissue was still there after almost **75 percent** of the starfish meals they examined. The starfish did not wipe out a whole coral colony in one sitting. It ate a patch and moved on, leaving living coral around the edge of the wound.

The team then followed those scars for about 22 weeks. Algae moved in first. Later, about **26 percent** of the scars showed coral growing back over the damaged spot.

## Why the slow-growing corals are happy

Not every coral grows back. The fast-growing branching corals — the *Acropora* family — stayed covered in algae. The corals that regrew were mostly slower kinds, like *Montipora* and the boulder-shaped *Porites*.

That matters because fast-growing corals can spread out and take over the space on a reef. When a starfish nibbles them back a little, it frees up room for slower corals. More kinds of coral in the same place means a more **diverse** reef, and diverse reefs tend to be sturdier when trouble arrives.

Scientists call this idea the *intermediate disturbance hypothesis*: a little bit of disturbance, now and then, can stop one species from ruling everything.

## The important catch

This does **not** mean starfish outbreaks are good. When their numbers explode, the reef loses far more coral than it can regrow, and that is still a serious problem.

What the study changes is the finish line for reef managers. Once an outbreak has been brought under control, the goal may not be to remove every last starfish. It may be better to protect the fish that eat starfish and let the reef find its own balance.

## Try this at home

Take a sheet of paper and draw a coral colony as a big blob of dots. Now shade in a small circle in the middle and leave the edges untouched. That is what a single starfish meal looks like. Count how much of your coral is still alive — and notice how different the reef looks if you imagine hundreds of those circles overlapping at once.

## For grown-ups: the real mechanism

The study, published in *Ecology and Evolution* (DOI 10.1002/ece3.74285), tracked discrete feeding scars left by individual *Acanthaster* cf. *solaris* from a low-density population at One Tree Reef between 2022 and 2024. Average scar size did not vary by season; roughly 72 percent of scars retained living tissue remnants around their perimeter. Scars were initially colonised by turf algae, with crustose coralline algae appearing by week 10; by week 22, about 26 percent showed regenerating coral tissue, predominantly in *Montipora*, while *Acropora* scars remained algal-covered. The authors frame this as sublethal predation that limits fast-growing taxa, consistent with the intermediate disturbance hypothesis, and caution that the sites were protected, low-density populations and that outbreak dynamics remain a major cause of Indo-Pacific coral loss. It is a refinement of culling targets, not an argument against controlling outbreaks.
