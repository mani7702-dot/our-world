# What Makes the Taurid Meteor Shower's Slow Fireballs Light Up Halloween Skies?

After Halloween, while jack-o'-lanterns fade outdoors, something magical takes their place in the night sky: the **Taurid meteor shower**. Unlike the frantic flash of July's Perseids, the Taurids move at their own gentle pace—about 28 kilometers per second—making them the **slowest of all the year's major meteor showers**. This slow speed is exactly what makes their occasional fireballs so dazzling, lingering in the sky like glowing embers drifting across the dark.

## 1. A Comet with a Memory

Every Taurid starts with a visitor from the depths of the solar system: **Comet 2P/Encke**, the second comet ever discovered by humans. It swings around the Sun every 3.25 years—about 1,200 days—the shortest orbital period of any known comet. Over thousands of years, Encke has gradually broken apart, leaving behind a long, thin stream of dust and pebble-sized debris that stretches across the inner solar system.

When Earth glides through this stream each autumn, that cosmic dust slams into our atmosphere at 28 km/s. The friction heats each grain to thousands of degrees, turning it into a tiny shooting star that glows greenish or golden as it falls. Because the particles move so slowly, they burn for longer than faster meteors—giving viewers more time to watch the show.

## 2. Where and When to Watch

The Taurids appear annually from **September 23 through December 8**, with the best viewing usually in **late October to early November**—right after Halloween. They take their name from the **radiant point in the constellation Taurus**, the bull that stands proudly in the eastern sky after midnight, near the famous Pleiades star cluster.

On a typical night you might spot about **5 meteors per hour**—a modest, patient display rather than a dazzling storm. But the reward comes with patience: every now and then, a Taurid grain grows bright enough to become a **fireball**—a meteor that outshines even the planet Venus and can cast faint shadows under a clear, dark sky.

## 3. Ghostly Green Fireballs in the Autumn

What truly sets the Taurids apart is their habit of producing these slow, brilliant fireballs. Since 2005, astronomers have recorded over a hundred bright fireballs from the Taurid stream—far more than many busier showers. These ghostly greenish streaks can last several seconds, sometimes splitting or leaving glowing trails that persist for minutes.

Scientists study these fireballs like clues in a mystery. By measuring their speed, brightness, and trajectory, researchers can figure out what the original comet fragments were made of and how they broke apart. It's a way of reading the story written in stardust—10,000 years ago, a giant comet roughly 40 kilometers wide began crumbling into the very particles that light up our October skies tonight.

### Fun Fact

The Taurids are actually two showers in one—the Northern and Southern Taurids—whose radiant points are so close together in Taurus that they blur into a single autumn display.

### Try this at home

After the Moon rises (or on a night with no Moon), find a dark spot away from streetlights. Lie back on a blanket and let your eyes adjust for 15 minutes. Look toward the east or southeast. The Taurids are slow enough to track with your eyes—you might even catch more than one at once if you scan a wide patch of sky!
