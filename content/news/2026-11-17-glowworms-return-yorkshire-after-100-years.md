Somewhere in North Yorkshire there is a patch of old limestone quarry that went dark for about a hundred years. No tiny green lights in the grass. No glowworms at all.

This summer, the lights came back. Not many - volunteers counted up to **twelve** glowing females over several weeks at Nosterfield Nature Reserve. But twelve is twelve more than a century of nothing.

## The shed in Bristol

The story starts a long way from Yorkshire, in a garden shed in Bristol.

An ecologist named Pete Cooper collected a small number of glowworms from an approved site, then spent years learning how to keep them alive and breed them - in his spare time, without being paid. He and a group of volunteers who call themselves the "glow-getters" worked out the tricky parts: what to feed the babies, how warm to keep them, how not to lose them.

It is harder than it sounds. Glowworm babies are hunters with one favourite food: snails and slugs. Keeping hundreds of them fed means keeping hundreds of snails alive too. So the shed was less of a cosy lantern factory and more of a very careful snail restaurant.

The first batch of young glowworms went out onto the reserve in **2024**. Then nothing happened. And nothing was supposed to happen - glowworms spend about two years as babies in the grass before they grow up.

## Two summers later, twelve little lamps

Summer 2026 was the first moment those 2024 babies could possibly have grown up and started glowing. And they did.

Volunteers walked the grassy edges of the reserve on different nights and counted up to twelve shining females. It is the first time the species has been seen lit at Nosterfield in more than a century.

"It's a really great first indicator of initial success," Cooper told The Guardian.

There are another **1,900** young glowworms waiting in the shed, due to be carried out to the reserve later this year. That is a lot of snail dinners.

## Why the girl glowworms do the glowing

Here is the surprising bit. The glowworm you spot in the grass is almost always a **female**, and she cannot fly. She climbs a grass stem, switches on her tail, and waits.

The males can fly, but their light is faint. So the females put on the show, like tiny landing lights saying *over here*.

The light itself is not hot at all. It is made by a chemical reaction inside the glowworm's body - a chemical called luciferin reacting with oxygen - and it gives off almost no heat. That is why it is called cold light.

Also, a small naming problem: glowworms are not worms. They are beetles, cousins of fireflies and lightning bugs.

## Try this at home: the glow-in-the-dark test

You can see how cold light works with a glow stick or a glow-in-the-dark star sticker.

1. Charge a glow-in-the-dark sticker under a lamp for a minute, then take it into a dark cupboard.
2. Touch it to your cheek. Notice it feels completely normal - not warm at all.
3. Compare that with a torch bulb you have left on for a while. That one is hot.

Living things that make their own light, like glowworms, fireflies and some deep-sea fish, do it with chemistry instead of heat. Almost all of the energy becomes light, which is why a glowworm can shine for hours without burning itself.

## The real test is 2030

Twelve glowing females proves something important: the young glowworms survived, found food, dodged the mowers and the floods, and grew up on the reserve.

But it does not prove the colony can look after itself. For that, the babies of these wild glowworms need to grow up and glow on their own - and that will not happen until around **2030**.

Until then, the volunteers keep counting, keep mowing the grass the right way, and keep a shed full of snails in Bristol.
