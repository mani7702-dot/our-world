# How a mountain crash made the world's second-tallest tsunami wave

## The day the mountain fell

On the morning of August 9, 2025, the sun was just starting to rise over Southeast Alaska. Three kayakers — Sasha Calvey, Billy White, and Nick Heilgeist — were camping on a tiny island called Harbor Island, tucked between two of Alaska's most-visited fjords: Tracy Arm and Endicott Arm. They had been paddling the Inside Passage for 78 days, and they thought they had picked a safe spot — well into the woods, far from the water's edge.

At 5:45 a.m., without warning, the ground roared.

## What happened in those seconds

A **mountain crashed into the water**.

Not a little bit of rock and dirt sliding down — but a massive block of solid bedrock, the size of several football fields lined up side by side, breaking loose from the mountainside above Tracy Arm. Geologists estimate **tens to hundreds of millions of cubic meters** of rock fell all at once, hitting the fjord where the South Sawyer Glacier ends in tidewater.

When that much solid rock hits water, the momentum doesn't just make ripples — it launches the whole surface of the fjord upward. The wave shot **1,580 feet (481 meters) straight up the opposite mountain wall** — that is, higher than a 100-story building laid on its side. It is the **second-tallest tsunami wave ever measured**, behind only one other wave in recorded history.

## The warning signs scientists missed

Michael West, Alaska's state seismologist, said the mountain didn't fail without warning. In the days before the landslide, his instruments picked up **thousands of tiny earthquakes** — so many that they looked like a continuous hum rather than individual shakes. "This landslide had a very clear precursory sequence," West said, "and that is not something we have often observed."

The signs were there: heavy summer rain soaking into cracks in the rock, the South Sawyer Glacier retreating rapidly (it had pulled back more than a third of a mile in just two months), and those small tremors rattling through the mountain like a drumbeat getting faster.

## Why no one got hurt

Luck played a big role. The landslide happened at **5:30 in the morning** — too early for cruise ships or tour boats to be in the narrow fjord. But even the kayakers, who were camped safely above the tide line, felt the force. The wave that swept through Harbor Island stripped away almost all their gear. One kayak floated a quarter mile offshore. Another rested on a cliff. The third ended up in a tree.

A passing charter yacht, the *Blackwood*, answered their distress call on marine radio and rescued them, bringing them blankets, hot showers, and a tour up Tracy Arm to see the damage — where **Sawyer Island stood nearly bare, with just one lone tree still clinging to its peak**.

## A growing danger

Scientists say events like this — huge bedrock landslides triggered by glacier retreat and heavy rain — are becoming more common as Arctic glaciers shrink. The same thing happened in Norway in 2018 and in Alaska's Kenai Fjords last year. Each time, the pattern is the same: a mountain that held itself together for thousands of years suddenly lets go, and the water pays the price.

"The same processes that create this hazard — glacier retreat, steep terrain, dynamic geology — are also what make these landscapes so compelling," scientists wrote. The question is no longer whether these places are dangerous, but how to **warn people in time**.

### Try this at home

Fill a baking dish with water, then drop a heavy book at one end. Watch how the wave spreads across the surface — that is a tiny version of what happened when millions of tons of Alaskan rock hit the fjord.

### Fun Fact

If you dropped a rock the size of a marble into a swimming pool, the splash would be over in a second. But when a mountain-sized rock hits a fjord, the wave keeps going for **hours**, resonating like water sloshing in a bathtub. Scientists even detected it on tide gauges **75 miles away in Juneau**.
