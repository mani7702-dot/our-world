# How is NASA building the telescope that will photograph Earth's cosmic twins?

## Why can't our telescopes take pictures of Earth-like worlds yet?

Since the first **exoplanet** was discovered in October 1995 — a world called 51 Pegasi b orbiting a Sun-like star — astronomers have confirmed over 6,000 more worlds beyond our solar system. But here is the big problem: almost all of them were found using indirect methods. Most of the time, we can only **feel** an exoplanet tug on its star or watch the star dim slightly as the planet passes in front. We have never taken a real photo of a true Earth twin.

Why? Because a planet is like a firefly next to a searchlight. The star it orbits is millions of times brighter than the planet itself. From Earth or even from space, the star's glare **completely washes out** any image of its planets. It's like trying to spot a ladybug sitting on a car's headlights from across a football field.

## What is the Habitable Worlds Observatory?

That is exactly the challenge NASA's new **Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO)** is built to solve. Scientists call it the "Holy Grail of exoplanet research" — finding a world with oceans, rocks, and maybe even signs of life, and actually **seeing** it with our own eyes.

HWO is a next-generation space telescope that will launch sometime in the 2040s. It will replace the James Webb Space Telescope as our primary space observatory, and it carries one very special gadget: a **coronagraph**. This instrument acts like a cosmic sunshade — it blocks out the star's blinding light so the faint planet next to it can finally be visible.

## How are engineers designing this super-telescope?

NASA's **Systems Engineering Team** spent months studying how to build HWO. They drew up three different design ideas, called **Exploratory Analytic Cases** (or EACs for short):

- **EAC1**: A 6-meter (20-foot) wide telescope with its mirror off to one side, so nothing blocks the center
- **EAC2**: A 6-meter (20-foot) wide telescope with a perfectly round mirror, also off-axis
- **EAC3**: An even bigger 8-meter (26-foot) wide telescope with its mirror in the center

The team found that the **off-axis designs** (where the mirror is shifted to one side) give astronomers a clearer, unobstructed view — and they picked a **larger mirror size** to gather more light, just like a bucket gathers more rain. These early designs build on what NASA learned from the James Webb Space Telescope and the upcoming Roman Space Telescope, using the same model-driven engineering methods.

## What makes HWO different from James Webb?

The James Webb Space Telescope — launched in 2021 — is amazing at studying atmospheres of distant worlds, but it mostly watches stars and galaxies. **HWO** is designed from the ground up to **directly image Earth-sized planets**. It will use its coronagraph to mask the star's light and take actual snapshot-style pictures of worlds that could have liquid water and conditions for life.

This means that before HWO launches, scientists and engineers will spend the next decade refining these designs — making sure every mirror, every instrument, and every software model is ready for humanity's first real glimpse of another world that might look like our own.

## Try this at home

Draw a picture of a star in the center of a dark piece of paper. Then draw a tiny dot very close to it — that is your exoplanet. Now, cover the star with a small piece of paper with a hole in the middle (the hole is your coronagraph). Look through the hole — can you see the tiny dot? You have just simulated how HWO's coronagraph blocks starlight to reveal hidden worlds!

## Fun Fact

NASA scientists estimate that in our galaxy alone, there are **billions** of Earth-sized planets. But so far, we have only directly imaged a handful of gas giants (Jupiter-like worlds). HWO — along with other upcoming telescopes like the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope — aims to change that, potentially taking the first-ever snapshots of worlds with oceans, clouds, and weather just like Earth.
