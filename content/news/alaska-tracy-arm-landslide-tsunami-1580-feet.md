# How did a mountain landslide launch a 1,580-foot wall of water?

## The day everything changed

It was just after 5:30 in the morning on August 9, 2025, in a quiet corner of Alaska. Passengers on the *Hanse Explorer* had been taking photos of the **South Sawyer Glacier**, one of the great rivers of ice that spills down from the mountains into Tracy Arm. Then they headed back down the fjord.

Twelve hours later, the mountain beside them let go.

A massive piece of bedrock — millions of tons of solid stone — broke free from the slope above the water. It tumbled down the mountainside and crashed into the fjord with a force that shook the whole valley. The splash was unlike anything anyone had ever seen: water and debris raced up the **opposite wall of the fjord**, climbing **1,580 feet** into the air — higher than the top floor of Taipei 101, one of the tallest buildings in the world.

That wave was a **tsunami**, and it was the second-tallest one scientists have ever measured anywhere on Earth.

## Why did the mountain fall?

Landslides happen in Alaska all the time — the mountains here are young, steep, and always moving. But this one was special in two ways:

First, it happened right where the **South Sawyer Glacier** ends, in a place called a "terminus." Over the two months before the slide, the glacier had pulled back more than a third of a mile, calving off huge chunks of ice. When ice disappears, it stops holding the mountainside in place — like pulling the plug from a bathtub, the rock suddenly has nothing to lean against.

Second, and most exciting for scientists, there were **warning signs**. For days before the collapse, small earthquakes rattled the area — thousands of tiny tremors, like a drumroll. Then, in the hours right before the slide, those little shakes turned into a continuous **seismic hum**, the mountain literally singing as it came apart. Rain had been falling, too, pushing water into cracks and loosening the rock even more.

If there had been an early-alert system, scientists think those signs would have been enough to tell ships to stay away.

## The wave that reached far and wide

When all that rock hit the water, the momentum turned into a wave. But Tracy Arm is a narrow, steep-walled fjord, so the water didn't spread out the way ocean waves usually do. Instead, it raced sideways up the far wall in a single, towering wall.

Captain Christine Smith was anchored near the entrance of a nearby fjord when she saw something strange: the water level suddenly dropped, then rose again by about **10 feet** — a tell-tale sign that a big landslide had just happened somewhere upstream. She called a seismologist friend, who confirmed it within minutes.

The wave was so strong it left marks **75 miles away in Juneau**, where tide gauges picked up the ripple hours later. When scientists and rescue crews reached Tracy Arm the next day, they found trees stripped from the shoreline, islands scoured down to bare rock, and an eerie layer of mud and wood left high on the cliffs — nature's own measuring tape showing exactly how high the water had climbed.

## What scientists are learning

This wasn't just a one-off disaster. The same pattern — rapid glacier retreat right before a giant landslide — has now been seen from **Alaska to Greenland to Norway**. As the planet warms and icicles melt faster, these steep, ice-backed mountains are becoming unstable.

That makes them fascinating AND dangerous places. They are the same dramatic landscapes that draw tourists on cruise ships... and now scientists are racing to build **early-warning alerts** that could save lives. Because while a tsunami this big is rare, the clues that come before it are written in the language of small quakes, melting ice, and falling rock — if anyone is listening.

### Try this at home
Fill a tall glass with water and drop a pebble in — see how the wave climbs the other side? Now imagine doing that with a mountain the size of a skyscraper. You can't, but you can visit your nearest large lake or fjord (if you have one!) on a windy day and watch how waves behave differently in narrow vs. open water. It's the same physics that made Tracy Arm so dramatic.

**Fun Fact:** The 1,580-foot run-up at Tracy Arm is the second-highest tsunami wave ever recorded on Earth — only the legendary 1958 Lituya Bay landslide in Alaska (which sent water 524 meters / 1,720 feet up a mountainside) was higher.
