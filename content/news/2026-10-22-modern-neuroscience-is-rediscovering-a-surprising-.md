# 1. When your brain guesses before you even see it

## Your head is a guessing machine

Close your eyes and picture your brain for a moment. Now imagine it is *guessing* — constantly guessing what will happen next, even before your eyes show you the answer. That is exactly what modern neuroscientists now believe: your brain is less like a camera recording the world, and more like a super-fast guesser that is always one step ahead of what it sees.

For most of the 20th century, scientists thought the brain simply took in information from the eyes, ears and skin, then worked on it. But a new understanding — called the **prediction paradigm** — says the opposite: the brain mostly predicts, then checks whether it was right. Every second you are awake, you are living inside a guess that your brain has already made.

## What Freud noticed 130 years ago

Here is the surprising part. Sigmund Freud — yes, the same Freud from the couch — described a very similar idea more than a century ago. He suggested that the mind does not sit quietly waiting for events; it pushes its own expectations, wishes and fears into view, colouring how it reads every situation. What Freud called *projection* — reading your own feelings back at someone else — is, in modern words, the brain letting a familiar guess shape what it "sees."

Imagine you have often felt left out, so you start expecting people to ignore you. A friend's quiet hello might suddenly feel cold, even if they meant nothing by it. Your brain filled in the blank before your friend even spoke. That is a prediction at work — and it is the same loop Freud was sketching in talk of unconscious expectations.

## Why some bad feelings stick around

If the brain prizes its guess about the world, then a wrong or unhelpful guess can be hard to shake. A person who always expects to be criticised may keep reading neutral faces as frowns, and so the expectation feeds itself, day after day — which helps explain why some mental habits are so tough to break. Both Freud's ideas and the prediction model agree: the mind craves stability, so it keeps choosing the *familiar* guess even when reality looks a little different.

Therapy, in both views, is about gently offering the brain new experiences — new "guesses" that turn out to be safe — so old patterns can slowly loosen their grip.

## Try this at home

Look at a familiar object — say, a cup — and tell yourself you will see something you have *never* noticed before about it. The cup is the same, but your brain is changing the lens. Do this a few times and notice how much of what you "see" is really what you *expect* to see.

## Fun Fact

The brain uses roughly **20 times more neurons to send predictions downward** than to carry raw signals upward from the senses — so in a very real wiring sense, our heads are built to guess first and check later.
