# How Can We Keep Moon Habitats Cool?

Living on the Moon sounds exciting, but the temperatures there are anything but friendly. In the sunlight, it can reach 127 degrees Celsius (260 degrees Fahrenheit) — hot enough to fry an egg. In the shade, it plunges to minus 247 degrees Celsius (minus 413 degrees Fahrenheit) — colder than the deepest freeze on Earth. Because one day on the Moon lasts 29.5 Earth days, each habitat would face two weeks of scorching heat followed by two weeks of icy cold. So how could anyone stay comfortable?

Engineers at the University of Pennsylvania may have found a simple but clever answer: layers of Mylar — the shiny plastic used in emergency blankets and food wrap.

## 1. Stacking Mylar Like a Thermal Sandwich

The Penn team created what they call **hybrid layered insulating film stacks**, or **HyLIFS**. They stacked hundreds of ultra-thin Mylar sheets — measuring 2.5, 5, and 12.5 micrometres thick — at densities of 80 to 400 layers per millimetre. Each stack was then tested in a vacuum chamber that mimics the Moon's environment, facing temperature swings and even pressure equivalent to 200 pounds pressing on a fingertip-sized spot.

## 2. Why the Medium Layers Won

Surprisingly, the team expected the thinnest (2.5 µm) layers to win, but they were disappointed. The thinnest sheets let certain microscopic particles touch too easily, which actually helped heat spread through the material. The thicker 12.5 µm layers were tougher, but it was the **medium 5 µm** layers — packed at 200 layers per millimetre — that struck the best balance. Their internal particles stayed just far enough apart to block heat flow while remaining strong enough to handle bumps, squishes, and years of use.

## 3. Building Blocks for the Future

NASA hopes to send humans back to the Moon by the mid-2030s and eventually build a permanent base — likely near the south pole, where water ice hides inside shadowed craters. Materials like HyLIFS could line the walls of inflatable habitats, keeping astronauts toasty in the cold and cool in the heat without breaking or tearing when astronauts brush past them.

For now, the Penn researchers are working on scaling the idea up. If it goes from lab to factory, it could one day wrap lunar bases the same way a space blanket wraps a runner's shoulders — simple, light, and remarkably effective.

## Try This at Home

Fill two identical containers with hot water. Wrap one in several layers of household aluminum foil or a shiny emergency blanket. Leave them side by side and check the temperature every 10 minutes. The wrapped container should stay warmer longer — just like a mini version of what could keep astronauts comfortable on the Moon!

## Fun Fact

Mylar was invented by chemist **John W. Hoey** in 1940 at DuPont. It was first used as a replacement for silk in military parachutes during World War II — long before it became the go-to material for space blankets and NASA missions.

## For the Curious Grown-Up

The Moon has no atmosphere to trap or carry heat, so temperatures swing wildly between day and night. Traditional space insulation (multilayer insulation) is fragile and tears easily. The HyLIFS design was published in **ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces** by researchers including **Igor Bargatin** and **Danielle R. Levin**, who tested layer densities from 80 to 400 layers per millimetre in vacuum conditions.
