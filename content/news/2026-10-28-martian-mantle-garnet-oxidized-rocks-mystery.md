# Why are Mars' rocks rustier than they should be? A deep-mantle garnet holds the answer

## The red planet's chemistry mystery

Mars is often called the "Red Planet" because of its rusty, iron-rich surface. But scientists have been puzzled by a deeper mystery: the rocks that fall off Mars and land on Earth as **meteorites** appear to be surprisingly **oxidized**—full of oxygen-bound iron—even though Mars' deep interior, sitting next to a giant iron core, should be the opposite: a **reducing** environment where oxygen gets stripped away. It is a bit like finding a rusty nail in a room full of pure metal, with no explanation for where the rust came from.

## Mars' hidden gemstone turns the tide

The answer, researchers say, may lie in a deep-mantle mineral called **majorite**—a type of garnet that only forms under crushing pressure far below the surface. On Mars, majorite crystallizes at the very bottom of the mantle, right above the iron core, where pressures reach about **18 gigapascals** (180,000 times Earth's sea-level pressure).

Scientists led by Hideharu Kuwahara and Ryoichi Nakada of Ehime University pressed tiny synthetic Martian rocks between diamonds at **2,150–2,200 °C** to mimic these crushing conditions. When they blasted the samples with X-rays at Japan's SPring-8 synchrotron, they discovered something striking: majorite acted like a **sponge for oxidized iron**, trapping between **11% and 21%** of the iron in its crystal structure in an oxidized form—far more than the surrounding minerals.

As Mars' interior slowly cooled, majorite pulled oxidized iron out of the deep, metallic environment and locked it away. Over billions of years, mantle convection carried these iron-rich crystals upward, and Mars' volcanoes—including the giant shield volcanoes like **Olympus Mons**—spewed oxidized lava onto the surface. That rusty material is exactly what we see today, and what ended up in meteorites that fell to Earth.

## The same secret in Earth's mantle

This discovery helps explain not just Mars, but Earth too. Our planet is about twice as wide as Mars, so our mantle runs even deeper, where a different mineral called **bridgmanite** dominates. Like majorite, bridgmanite can hoard oxidized iron—but when the mantle rises and pressure drops, it **transforms into majorite and releases that iron**, gradually oxidizing the rocky zone between the deep mantle and the surface. Understanding how these deep minerals store oxygen helps scientists piece together how rocky planets form, evolve, and hold onto—or lose—their atmospheres over billions of years.

## Try this at home

Fill a clear glass with water and add a pinch of salt. Drop in a small piece of iron (like a paperclip). Leave it for a few hours—you will see orange-red specks (rust) appear, showing how iron changes when it meets oxygen and water. On Mars, that same rusty chemistry was set in motion deep underground, by crystals forged in crushing heat and pressure.

## Fun Fact

Majorite garnets from Earth are so tough that scientists have found them locked inside ancient meteorites—travelers from space that broke apart and fell to Earth millions of years ago. The name "majorite" comes from the mineral's discovery in a meteorite from Australia in 1968, where it formed under the shock pressure of a cosmic impact!

**for-grownups:** The study resolves a long-standing contradiction: Martian meteorites are highly oxidized, but Mars' deep interior should be reducing (oxygen-poor) due to its iron core. Majorite crystallizes at ~18 GPa above the core-mantle boundary and acts as an "iron sponge," capturing 11–21% oxidized (ferric) iron in its crystal structure during high-pressure experiments at 2,150–2,200 °C. As the mantle convects, majorite-bearing rocks rise and volcanism at Olympus Mons and other shields releases oxidized material to the surface and into space. The same mechanism applies to Earth: deeper bridgmanite releases ferric iron when it transitions to majorite during upwelling.
