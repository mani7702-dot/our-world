# How citizen scientists are watching the coastline change — one photo at a time

## 1. Beaches that grow and shrink like breathing

If you have ever walked along a beach, you may have noticed something: some days the water comes right up to a line of rocks, and other days there is lots of dry sand. Beaches are always on the move. Waves push sand away from the shore during storms, and calm seas slowly bring it back. Over weeks, months, and years, the whole coastline shifts — and scientists want to track every change.

But here is the problem: a scientist cannot be at every beach all the time. That is where **you** come in.

## 2. A phone cradle that turns you into a scientist

The project is called **CoastSnap**, and it started in Australia in 2017. Here is how it works:

You walk up to a beach that has a CoastSnap station. There is a small wooden or metal **cradle bolted to the ground**, and a sign that shows you where to rest your phone. You slide your phone into the cradle, open the free CoastSnap app, and take a photo of the horizon. That is it. No fancy equipment — just your everyday camera phone.

The cradle is the clever part. It makes sure every photo is taken from almost exactly the same spot and angle. That means the images line up perfectly when scientists compare them over time — like matching pages in a photo album.

## 3. Software does the hard part

After you upload your photo, special software gets to work. It flips your tilted phone view into a perfect **bird's-eye view** of the beach, like a drone shot. An algorithm then draws a line along the water's edge and corrects for the tide level — because the water moves in and out twice a day!

With hundreds or thousands of these photos over months or years, scientists can see:

- How much sand was washed away during a big storm
- Whether a beach replenishment project (adding sand back) is working
- How rising seas are slowly eating away at the coast

Today, there are **more than 200 CoastSnap stations in 21 countries**, from England to Australia to the United States. It is the largest land-based coastal monitoring program in the world — and it is powered by people who just love the beach.

## 4. What kids can see

At Praa Sands beach in Cornwall, England, one of these stations sits right by the water. When enough photos pile up over a year, anyone can visit the project's maps and watch their local beach breathe — growing wider in summer, shrinking in winter, always changing.

You do not need to be a grown-up scientist. Beach cleanups, tide pooling, and even just walking the shore with an adult already teach you the same thing: nature is never still, and the shapes of our coasts are written in sand.

## Try this at home

Make your own mini shoreline tracker! On a trip to the beach (or even a lake or riverbank), pick a fixed spot — maybe next to a big rock or a driftwood log. Place a stick in the sand as your "cradle" marker. Take a photo from the exact same spot every visit, and try to keep your phone at the same height and angle. Over a few trips, you will start to notice where the waterline moves. You can even draw the high-tide line on a printout of your first photo and compare it to later ones.

## Fun Fact

The word "coastline" sounds fixed, but the Pacific coast of the United States moves an average of **1.5 meters (about 5 feet) per year** — that is roughly as fast as you can walk in one minute! CoastSnap helps scientists measure even smaller changes, one phone photo at a time.
