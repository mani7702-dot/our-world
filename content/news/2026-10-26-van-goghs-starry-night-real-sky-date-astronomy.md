# How did astronomers crack the code of Van Gogh's 'The Starry Night' sky?

## A sky that has puzzled us for 137 years

Ask anyone to picture "The Starry Night," and they will see a swirling, dream-coloured sky filled with bright stars and a glowing crescent moon. Painted by Vincent van Gogh in 1889, the work is one of the most recognisable paintings in the world.

But here is the question that has kept both scientists and art lovers awake at night: **is that sky real?** Did Van Gogh copy the actual stars above southern France, or did he paint a sky pulled from his imagination?

## Matching stars with maths

For decades, experts guessed. In 1984, art historian Albert Boime and astronomer George Abell tried to line up the stars, and later Harvard's Charles Whitney gave it another shot. But they disagreed, and no one could agree on a single night.

Now a new study has finally cracked the puzzle — and it only took a clever bit of star-mapping maths.

Kim Jeong-hyun and his team treated the painting like a photograph of the sky. Using a technique called **Procrustes analysis** (the same kind of shape-matching that astronomers use to compare galaxy images), they matched the bright points of light in Van Gogh's swirls to the real positions of stars on the night sky.

The clue that solved it all was the **Hyades cluster** — a V-shaped group of bright stars in the constellation Taurus. The team found that the cluster of stars painted near the top of the canvas fits the real Hyades perfectly, but only if the viewer was looking toward the pre-dawn eastern sky on **one exact morning**: **July 22, 1889**, just before sunrise, from the window of the asylum at Saint-Rémy-de-Provence.

## What else is hiding in the sky

The Hyades matched. But so did two other clues:

- **Venus** — the brightest "star" in the painting, just to the right of the tall cypress tree, is exactly where Venus would have appeared before dawn that morning. (The ancient "morning star" that Van Gogh wrote about in a letter to his brother Theo.)
- The **waning crescent moon** also lines up with the calendar — July 22, 1889 was indeed a morning when a thin crescent moon hung in the eastern sky.

## A question of dates

Here is the twist: art historians believe Van Gogh painted "The Starry Night" in **June 1889**, weeks before the sky the team identified. The researchers do not say the historians are wrong — instead they suggest that Van Gogh may have **conceived** the idea in June, then painted it after he observed the July 22 dawn sky, working from memory and sketches.

What the study does show is that Van Gogh was a careful observer. Every time he looked up from his asylum window that summer, the sky would have looked slightly different as Earth journeyed around the Sun — and July 22 was the night the stars lined up just right.

The study was published on October 22, 2026, in the journal *Scientific Reports*.

## Try this at home

Draw five bright dots on a piece of black paper with a yellow crayon — that is your own Hyades cluster. Now turn the paper so the cluster sits in the top corner, and add a thin silver crescent Moon nearby. You have recreated the same sky Van Gogh saw! Now imagine you are looking at it just before sunrise from a window — what time of year would it be? (Hint: late July!)

## Fun Fact

The Hyades is the closest open star cluster to Earth — those bright stars are all siblings, born together from the same cosmic cloud about 625 million years ago. The brightest of them all, **Aldebaran**, looks like it belongs to the cluster, but it is actually much closer to us and only appears to sit in the Hyades by chance!

**for-grownups:** The study (Kim et al., 2026, *Scientific Reports*) used Procrustes analysis to compare the 5-star shape of the Hyades cluster in Van Gogh's painting against simulated pre-dawn skies from Saint-Rémy-de-Provence on dates between June and August 1889. July 22 produced the best match by every metric: cluster position, size, tilt, field-of-view, plus co-incidence of Venus and a waning crescent Moon in the eastern sky. The Hyades (Greek for "rain-bringers") is an open cluster of ~150 stars at ~150 light-years, dominated by the bright orange giant Aldebaran (which is a fore-ground field star, not a cluster member). Venus as the morning star was documented by Van Gogh in his 1889 letters.
