Two weeks ago, a new eye opened in space.

High above Earth, the European Space Agency's brand-new Sentinel-3C satellite — launched September 15th on a Vega C rocket from French Guiana — has started sending home its very first pictures. And what it sees looks like a postcard from another world.

In one image, the whole boot of Italy stretches from the snow-capped Alps down to Sicily and the sparkling Mediterranean. In another, the Canadian Arctic spreads out in icy blues and whites — snow-covered islands dotted with deep, rocky fjords where the sea meets the sky.

## What does Sentinel-3C do?

Sentinel-3C is like a super-powered camera in space. It carries two main instruments:

1. **A color camera** (called the Ocean and Land Color Instrument) that takes detailed pictures of Earth's surface — like the ones you see above.
2. **A radar altimeter** that bounces radar signals off the ground and sea to measure heights — how tall a glacier is, how high a river floods, or how thick sea ice gets in winter.

Flying about 814 kilometers (506 miles) above us — that's more than twice the altitude of a commercial airplane — the satellite orbits Earth every 101 minutes. That means it can photograph almost the entire planet every two days.

## Why does it matter?

These aren't just pretty pictures for your fridge. The data Sentinel-3C collects helps scientists track things we can't easily see from the ground:

- **Ocean health**: Measuring water temperature and color tells us where coral reefs are bleaching or where algae is blooming too much.
- **Ice and glaciers**: Height measurements show whether ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica are shrinking — a key sign of climate change.
- **Floods and droughts**: Radar can see through clouds to measure how high floodwaters rise or how dry a drought-stricken field has become.
- **Marine pollution**: Color cameras spot oil spills, plastic patches, and algae blooms from space before anyone smells them.

## How long will it keep working?

ESA is still in the process of "commissioning" — which means running tests and fine-tuning everything — for the next five months. But already, just two weeks after launch, Sentinel-3C is working better than anyone expected.

It joins two older siblings — Sentinel-3A and Sentinel-3B — keeping the mission running without interruption. When one satellite needs rest or repair, another is always watching.

And in 2029, Sentinel-3D will launch to keep the family going even longer. The satellites may have names like numbers, but they're really our window to the whole wide world below.

## Try this at home

You can browse real satellite images yourself! Visit [Earthdata Search](https://search.earthdata.nasa.gov) and type in any place you've heard of — your hometown, a national park, or even a faraway island. Zoom out and try to spot rivers, coastlines, and mountain ranges just like Sentinel-3C would see them.

## Fun fact

The Sentinel-3 series was designed to be like a relay race — each satellite hands off the baton to the next. This means we've been watching Earth continuously since 2016, collecting more than **1 billion kilometers** of elevation data (that's like measuring the distance to the Sun and back!) and tracking everything from sea-level rise to forest health. Sentinel-3C is the newest runner in that race.
