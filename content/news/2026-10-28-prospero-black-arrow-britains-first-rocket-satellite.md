# Why was Prospero, Britain's first home‑grown satellite, launched on a last‑ditch rocket?

## A rocket built for a nation

In October 1971, Britain did something it has only done once before or since: it launched
its own satellite using its own rocket. The rocket was the **Black Arrow**, a three‑stage
vehicle standing nearly 13 meters tall and based on the smaller Black Knight research rocket.
The satellite riding on top was called **Prospero** (also known as X‑3, and once called
**Puck**). On **October 28, 1971** at 04:09 GMT, from the Woomera Range Complex in South
Australia, the final Black Arrow roared off the pad and — against the odds — succeeded.

## Why launch a satellite that was meant to stay on the ground?

The story begins a little unhappily for rocket scientists. By 1971 the British Ministry of
Defence had already decided to cancel the Black Arrow programme, judging it too costly to
keep compared with buying rides on American rockets. But the team that had built Prospero
was not ready to give up. When they learned the rocket would be scrapped, they asked if they
could launch the satellite anyway — as a final demonstration of what British engineers had
made. Officials agreed, and Prospero became the mission's farewell gift to the programme.

The gamble paid off. Prospero went into an elliptical orbit stretching from about **522 km**
to **1,300 km** above Earth, and it worked. Even so, the flight had a close call: after the
satellite separated, the rocket's final Waxwing stage kept thrusting a moment too long and
 bumped into Prospero, snapping off one of its four radio antennas. Luckily the rest of the
satellite was fine.

## What did Prospero actually do in space?

Prospero was not a communications satellite — it was a classroom made of solar cells. Its
job was to test the technology that future communications satellites would need. Among its
experiments were two different designs of **solar cells**: one lightweight cell with a
special mount, and another that swapped the usual glass cover for a cheaper, cerium‑oxide
coating to see if it would last. It also carried a tiny **micrometeoroid detector** to
measure how many microscopic space rocks were buzzing around — a real hazard for any
spacecraft. The lightweight solar cells turned out to be a success, though the cerium‑oxide
cover did not fare as well.

From a tracking station in the Falkland Islands, controllers talked to Prospero regularly
for two years, until 1973. After that they switched the satellite off — but not for good.
Each year, on its anniversary, ground stations woke Prospero back up and listened in, and
they kept doing that until **1996**, nearly a quarter of a century after the rocket that
launched it had been cancelled.

## Try this at home

Roll a sheet of paper into a tube and tape a paper nose cone on top. Drop it from shoulder
height and watch how it tumbles. Now tape on two thin cardboard fins — does it fall straighter?
Real rockets use fins and gimbaling nozzles to steer through the sky, while satellites like
Prospero use small thrusters or spinning motion to turn. Can you make a falling "satellite"
that spins so it stays pointed the same way as it falls? That is how many real satellites
keep their solar panels facing the Sun.

## Fun Fact

Prospero is still up there. Travelling 800 km above us, it is too faint for the naked eye
most nights, but at perigee (its closest point) it reaches a brightness of around
magnitude **+6** — visible through ordinary binoculars as a steady, star‑like point that
migrates slowly across the sky. If you spot a "star" that doesn't twinkle and creeps along
over the course of an hour, you may have found a piece of Britain's space history.

## for-grownups

Prospero was the fourth and final flight of the Black Arrow R‑3 launch vehicle. It was
technically Britain's first successful orbital launch on a domestically developed carrier
rocket (the earlier, failed Orba X‑2 launch had not reached orbit), making the UK the sixth
nation to orbit a satellite on its own rocket. The 66‑kilogram spacecraft was built by the
Royal Aircraft Establishment in Farnborough and conducted materials and micrometeoroid
experiments to inform the design of future British communications satellites. After two
years of primary operations (1971–1973), Prospero entered a long hibernation, though it was
re‑activated annually on its launch date through 1996. It remains in low Earth orbit and is
not expected to re‑enter until about **2070** — roughly a century after its birth. In 2021
the Scotland‑based startup Skyrora announced plans to rendezvous with Prospero using a
space tug and bring it home for deorbit, presenting the first private mission to capture a
historical satellite. Prospero's launch from Woomera remains the only time a UK‑built
rocket has placed a UK‑built satellite in orbit.
