# What happens when a hurricane runs out of warm water?

## When a hurricane gets a new identity

In early October 2026, Hurricane Isaias carved a path of rain and wind across the Atlantic — first hitting Florida as a Category 2 storm, then marching east across the ocean like a giant, churning engine. But hurricanes are picky eaters: they need **warm ocean water** (at least 26°C deep) to stay alive. Once Isaias drifted too far north, over colder seas, something almost magical happened. The storm didn't fade — it **transformed**.

## The birth of a wind monster

As Isaias left the tropics behind, it began a process scientists call **extratropical transition**. Picture a caterpillar becoming a butterfly, but for storms. The warm-core hurricane — powered by warm water — gradually reshaped itself into a **baroclinic cyclone**, a system that feeds on the temperature differences in the atmosphere instead.

The result was a **comma-shaped behemoth**. These storms earn their name from the way their clouds stretch into a long, curved tail that, from space, looks like a backwards "C" or a question mark drawn by a giant. It's one of the most beautiful and dangerous shapes in meteorology.

NASA's **NOAA-21 satellite** caught the transformed storm as it **brushed past Iceland on October 14, 2026**. The VIIRS instrument — the same camera that spotted Hurricane Simon's sky-ripples weeks earlier — snapped the full, sweeping view: a swirling mass of white clouds with a long, trailing arm of rain and wind stretching across the North Atlantic.

## How fast was the wind?

Iceland felt the full punch. A gust of **205 kilometers per hour (128 miles per hour)** was recorded near the famous Eyjafjallajökull volcano — the same peak that grounded European flights with volcanic ash in 2010. Far offshore, a small island reported **sustained winds of 150 km/h (93 mph)** for hours.

These aren't average breezes. These are winds strong enough to snap trees like matchsticks and fling debris like missiles. But the real shocker is hidden in the numbers: the storm's air pressure **plummeted by more than 50 hectopascals in just 24 hours**.

## A "bomb" storm

When a storm's pressure drops faster than 1 hectopascal per hour at high latitudes, meteorologists call it a **"bomb"** — explosive cyclogenesis. It's like the atmosphere lit a fuse. The faster the pressure drops, the faster the winds spin, and the more destructive the storm becomes.

The North Atlantic in October is one of the best places on Earth to watch this drama unfold. The Gulf Stream pours warmth and moisture into the air, while cold air from the north crashes in from above. That clash — warm ocean against cold sky — is what makes these storms grow so fierce.

## From space, beauty and fury

What NASA's satellite showed from 500 miles up was breathtaking: a perfectly formed comma of cloud and shadow, with the storm's core clearly visible as a dark eye-wall of swirling wind. The image is a reminder that hurricanes and extratropical cyclones — though they start differently and end differently — are both expressions of the same truth: the atmosphere is always trying to balance itself, and sometimes the results are spectacular.

## Try this at home

On a cold night, fill a jar with hot water, screw on the lid, and watch the steam fog the glass. Tilt it slowly — that curling "tail" of steam mirrors how cold and warm air masses twist together in an extratropical cyclone. For older kids: check a weather map online (weather.gov or the Met Office) when a storm is nearing — look for that tell-tale comma shape in the clouds.

## Fun fact

The word "cyclone" comes from the Greek *kyklon*, meaning "moving in a circle" — but the storm's spiral shape is actually a trick of two things working together: the **Coriolis effect** (which makes everything in the northern hemisphere curve to the right) and **conservation of angular momentum** (the same physics that makes ice skaters spin faster when they pull in their arms). Together, they turn a straight breeze into a spinning giant.
