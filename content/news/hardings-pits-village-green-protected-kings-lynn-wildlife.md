# How did a community save a wild green space from a supermarket — and win?

Three hundred years ago this patch of earth was just a clay pit.

Then it became a landfill.

Then neighbors decided it could be something better — a wild place where birds nest and children can explore — and they fought for thirty whole years to keep it that way.

## A place that grew by accident

Harding's Pits sits beside the River Great Ouse in King's Lynn, England. No one planted it. It grew up the way the best wild things do — by accident, then by care.

A clay pit dug out gravel and clay. Then people dumped rubbish there. Then, in the 1960s, someone had the idea to make it a public space instead.

Volunteers kept showing up. They mowed paths. They planted more than 400 trees in a single day. They left brambles and rough ground alone on purpose, because that is where the lizards and the birds like to hide.

## The supermarket that never came

In 2004, a developer wanted to build a supermarket and some houses right over Harding's Pits. Neighbors formed a group and said: "No thanks."

They spent years filling in forms and going to meetings. They kept walking their dogs there. They kept watching the sparrowhawks dive for rabbits in the long grass.

In the end, the supermarket plans were dropped.

But the neighbors did not stop. They wanted a promise written in law — a guarantee that the land would stay wild.

## Village Green — a legal shield

Last year, they asked the local council to give Harding's Pits something called "Village Green" status. That is a fancy name for a legal shield: it means the land is protected so future owners — or future councils — cannot build on it.

On October 20th, 2026, the shield was approved.

Now Harding's Pits is protected by law. About four hectares — that is roughly the size of five and a half football pitches — will stay a wild green space forever.

## Home to more than birds

Walk through Harding's Pits and you might see:

- Over one hundred kinds of birds, from robins to redwings
- Three kinds of reptiles basking on sunny rocks (especially lots of little lizards)
- A community orchard where apples grow
- A circle of young oak trees planted by artists (part of something called Beuys' Acorns)
- Blackberry bushes that everyone is welcome to pick from

The site is managed by volunteers who mow paths and fix seats so everyone can enjoy it safely.

## What this means

When grown-ups want to build a new road or a big store, they have to prove the land is "empty." Harding's Pits is not empty — it is full of stories, roots, wings, and neighbors who love it.

Village Green status means that future children will also get to chase dragonflies there.

### Try this at home
Look for a small wild spot near you — an overgrown corner, a tree by a fence, a patch where dandelions push through. Sit quietly for five minutes and write down or draw three living things you notice. Then ask a grown-up if you can visit again next week.
