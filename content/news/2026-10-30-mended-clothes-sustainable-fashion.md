# Why are mended clothes the new must-have fashion?

## The hole-y revolution

Imagine finding a cozy jumper at a thrift store, only to see a small hole in the
sleeve. In the past, most people would have set it aside — a stain or tear meant the
clothes were "ruined." But today, that same jumper might just become the start of a
creative adventure.

A growing movement called **visible mending** turns torn and worn clothing into one-of-a-
kind art. Instead of hiding damage, people embrace it — stitching on colorful patches,
embroidering cute animal shapes over holes, and adding dazzling geometric patterns where
the fabric is thin.

## Sewing stories, not hiding mistakes

The magic of visible mending is that every scar on a piece of clothing becomes part of its
story. A moth hole might get covered by a tiny felt ladybug. A worn elbow could bloom
into a bright flower. Far from being embarrassing, the mends become decorations that
show the garment was loved and cared for.

This style draws on traditional techniques from other cultures. In Japan, a craft called
**Sashiko** creates beautiful white stitches on blue fabric, originally to reinforce
clothing so it would last longer. Another Japanese method, **Boro**, layers patches and
stitching over and over until a garment is too strong to tear again. Both turned
practical repair into an art form.

## Big brands are joining in

What started in small community workshops has caught the attention of major clothing
stores. Some now offer free repair services right in their shops. Customers can bring in
a favorite jacket with a tear or a pair of jeans with a hole and have it stitched back
together — sometimes with decorative flair.

One outdoor brand found that about one in five repairs its customers request are designed
to be eye-catching rather than hidden. A premium clothing store reported that roughly a
third of shoppers choose the decorative option. Even budget-friendly fashion chains are
getting involved, teaching traditional repair skills through in-store workshops.

The message is simple: instead of buying brand-new clothes to replace something torn,
you can give your old favorites a second life — and make them look even more special
along the way.

## Try this at home

Ask an adult to help you practice on an old piece of fabric. Try running a simple
straight stitch — even if it is not perfect, you are sewing, just like the sashiko
artists of long ago. You can also draw a small patch (a star, a heart, a bug) and glue it
over a hole with help. Every great mender started with a single stitch.

## Fun Fact

The word "sashiko" means "little stabs" in Japanese, because the needle stabs through
the cloth again and again to make those neat running stitches. The traditional colors are
white thread on blue fabric — like a snowy path on a indigo summer river.
