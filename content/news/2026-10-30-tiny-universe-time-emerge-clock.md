# Can time exist without a clock? Scientists built a tiny universe to find out

## An experiment that bends time itself

What if time is not a fixed, universal clock ticking away in the background of everything? What if it is something that *emerges* from the way things change and move inside the universe? To test this idea, a physicist at the University of Birmingham built a miniature universe right here on Earth—and watched time appear from nowhere, without so much as a single clock.

## Building a universe from 24,000 atoms

The "tiny universe" was made of about **24,000 atoms** cooled to temperatures so low they are just a few billionths of a degree above **absolute zero**—the coldest anything can possibly be. At these extreme temperatures, quantum effects take over, letting researchers study behaviors that are normally impossible to observe.

Inside a carefully controlled quantum system, two laser beams created a barrier that split the atoms into two regions: a bright, observed side and a dark, unobserved side. Atoms could move between the two, causing the bright region to **expand and contract** in cycles. Each expansion mimicked the **Big Bang**—the moment the real universe began—and each contraction resembled a hypothetical **Big Crunch**, when the universe might one day collapse back in on itself.

## Time emerges from change, not a ticking clock

Most of us think of time as something that simply *passes*, like sand flowing through an hourglass. But in some theories of physics—especially those trying to blend quantum mechanics with gravity—**time may not be a basic ingredient of reality at all**. If there is no built-in clock, how can events happen in a particular order?

Professor Barontini's team found a surprising answer using a concept called **entropy**—the physics word for disorder. Instead of measuring seconds, they tracked how the atoms spread out and clumped together inside the miniature universe. When the particle distribution was changing, their internal sense of time moved forward. When it stayed the same, that internal time effectively **stopped**.

Physicists call this "entropic time," and it behaves a lot like our everyday experience of time: it moves in one direction (that is, the **arrow of time**, the reason we remember the past but not the future), and it can even speed up or slow down depending on how things are changing.

## Why this matters for the real universe

The experiment did not recreate the actual Big Bang or Big Crunch—it was a simplified quantum model. But it proved something that had been hard to test: **a quantum system can have its own meaningful sense of time without needing a clock outside itself**.

This gives scientists a new tool for studying one of physics' biggest puzzles: *Where does time come from?* Future experiments could test more complex systems, perhaps even simulating aspects of black holes or comparing different theories about how the early universe unfolded.

By building a universe from just 24,000 atoms, researchers have taken a small but important step toward answering a question that has challenged thinkers for centuries: what makes time flow?

## Try this at home

Grab two clear cups and a handful of coins or small stones. Pour the coins back and forth between the cups one at a time, moving faster or slower. The *order* and *rate* of your transfers are like the internal changes that create a sense of time. Now try pouring them all at once—there is no steady "flow." This simple version of what Barontini did with atoms shows how time can be tied to change, not a clock.

## Fun Fact

The idea that time might not be fundamental goes all the way back to the **Wheeler-DeWitt equation**, a famous attempt to describe the universe without time—a "timeless" quantum state. Barontini's experiment is the first time anyone has tested this idea in a real, controllable lab setting.
