# Why does exercise 'rewire' the nerves that run your heart?


When you run, swim, or ride a bike, you can probably feel your heart beating faster. We often say exercise "strengthens" the heart. But scientists at the University of Bristol in the UK just discovered it does something even more surprising: it actually **rewires the tiny nerves that control your heartbeat** — and it changes the two sides of your body differently.

## A hidden "dimmer switch" for your heart

Your heart beats all day without you thinking about it. A network of nerves called the *autonomic nervous system* acts like your body's autopilot, speeding your heart up when you need more oxygen and slowing it down when you rest.

Deep in your lower neck and upper chest sit two small clusters of nerves called the **stellate ganglia**. Think of them as the heart's dimmer switch — they send "go faster" signals that help set your heart rate.

Scientists used to assume exercise changed these two clusters in exactly the same way. This new study showed that assumption was wrong.

## Exercise changes each side differently

The research team ran rats on a treadmill for ten weeks of moderate aerobic exercise, then compared their heart-control nerves to rats that didn't exercise. Using advanced 3D imaging, they found dramatic differences between the two sides:

- On the **right side**, the nerve cluster grew about **four times as many neurons** as the untrained rats had.
- On the **left side**, the neurons didn't multiply — instead, they nearly **doubled in size**.

Two sides, two completely different changes, all from the same simple exercise.

## Why this matters

Dr. Augusto Coppi, who led the study, explained that these nerve clusters act "like the heart's dimmer switch," and regular moderate exercise remodels that switch in a side-specific way. This could help explain why some heart treatments work better on one side of the body than the other — and one day help doctors aim treatments more precisely for problems like irregular heartbeats or "broken-heart" syndrome.

The research was done in rats, so scientists don't yet know if human hearts rewire in exactly the same way. But it's a lovely reminder that exercise doesn't just make you stronger on the outside — it quietly reshapes your body on the inside too.

**Try this at home:** Next time you get your heart pumping — running around, dancing, or riding a bike — take a moment to notice how it speeds up when you move and slows back down when you rest. That's your heart's own autopilot doing its job, helped along by those hidden nerve switches.

---

*For the grown-ups:* This work, published in the journal *Autonomic Neuroscience*, used 3D stereology imaging to reveal asymmetric neuroplasticity in the stellate ganglia of rats after 10 weeks of treadmill training — the right side showed a ~4x increase in neuron count, while left-side neurons hypertrophied to nearly double size. Early-stage animal findings; human follow-up studies are planned.
