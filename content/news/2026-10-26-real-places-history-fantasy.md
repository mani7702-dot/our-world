# 10 Real Places Whose Histories Sound Like Fantasy Novels

Time: 18:39 IST

Have you ever read a fantasy novel and thought, "That place could never exist in real life"? It turns out reality has a way of being stranger — and more amazing — than any story. All around the world, real places were built, lived in, and abandoned in ways that would make a novelist jealous. Here are ten of our favorite places where real history outdoes fiction.

## 1. A Mountain Town Where Stairs Are the Streets — Sewell, Chile

Perched more than 2,200 meters (7,200 feet) up in the Andes, Sewell is a ghost town built right into a steep mountainside. Red, yellow, and blue buildings hug the slope, connected by a maze of stone staircases that wind in every direction. These weren't just shortcuts — they *were* the streets.

Sewell began in 1906 as a home for copper-mine workers at El Teniente. By 1960, about 16,000 people lived there, climbing stairs to reach school, the movies, and each other. But as roads improved, the town was quietly emptied. Today it stands frozen in time, protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## 2. A Kingdom Built on Iron — Sukur, Nigeria

In northeastern Nigeria, Sukur sits on a hilltop where, for centuries, people mined and traded iron. By the 17th century it had grown into a powerful iron-producing kingdom ruled by a leader called the Hidi, who held both political and spiritual power. Workers operated furnaces near their homes while farmers tended terraces carved into the hillsides.

Between 1912 and 1922, raids disrupted the kingdom and iron production faded. Yet the stone pathways, farming terraces, and traditional buildings are still in use today — a living connection to the past.

## 3. A Town That Turned Floodwater Into Power — Banská Štiavnica, Slovakia

In this Slovak mountain town, miners dug for gold and silver but hit a problem: groundwater kept flooding their mines. Their brilliant solution? Build artificial reservoirs called *tajchy* to catch rainwater and snowmelt, then use that stored water to power pumps that kept the mines dry.

By the 18th century, an entire network of channels and tunnels stretched through the mountains. The obstacle became the solution, and the clever engineering is now recognized by UNESCO (inscribed in 1993).

## 4. A Well With Two Spiral Staircases — Orvieto, Italy

When Pope Clement VII fled Rome during an attack in 1527, he took refuge in Orvieto, a city perched on volcanic rock. Worried about siege, he commissioned a well — but architect Antonio da Sangallo the Younger had an extraordinary idea: two spiral staircases, each with 248 steps, that never cross. One spiral went down for water; the other brought people and pack animals back up. No traffic jams, no squeezing past each other.

Construction took ten years. Even today, visitors can walk down and look up into the enormous 53-meter shaft, lit by 72 windows.

## 5. A City Carved Out of Stone — Matera, Italy

For thousands of years, the people of Matera lived in caves carved into limestone cliffs. Known as the *Sassi*, these weren't caves for sleeping rough — they were entire neighborhoods of homes, churches, and passageways carved directly into the rock. The settlement goes back to prehistoric times.

By the 20th century, conditions were overcrowded and unsanitary, so the government relocated residents in the 1950s. But the caves didn't stay empty for long. Restoration turned the *Sassi* into a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1993), and today they're trendy hotels and restaurants that draw visitors from around the world.

## 6. An Emperor’s Abandoned Capital — Fatehpur Sikri, India

Emperor Akbar built an entire capital city after a spiritual teacher predicted he would have a son. In 1569, Prince Salim was born, and Akbar built Fatehpur Sikri near the teacher's home. The city filled with palaces, courtyards, mosques, and grand gateways, all built of red sandstone.

Just a decade later, in 1584, Akbar moved the court to Lahore. Some say water shortages, some say military concerns. Either way, the "perfect city" was abandoned — but its stunning buildings survived, earning UNESCO recognition in 1986.

## 7. People Who Made Their Own Floating Land — Uros Islands, Peru

On Lake Titicaca, the Uros people didn't wait for land to build their homes — they made their own. For generations, they have built floating islands using *totora* reeds that grow in the lake. They layer the reeds over bundles of roots to create platforms that stay afloat. Because the reeds slowly rot, families add fresh layers every few weeks to keep their islands bobbing.

Living on water helped the Uros resist stronger mainland groups. Today, families still maintain the islands, and visitors can paddle out to learn how a whole community can literally float.

## 8. A Desert City With Secret Rooftop Streets — Ghadamès, Libya

In the Sahara oasis of Ghadamès (the "Pearl of the Desert"), houses were built so close together that the streets ran beneath the buildings. But while men and children used the shaded ground-level passages, women had their own network of connected rooftop terraces — hidden walkways to visit neighbors without going downstairs.

For centuries Ghadamès was a busy stop on trans-Saharan trade routes. Residents eventually moved to newer homes, leaving the old city protected as a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1986). The rooftop maze still stands, silent and secret.

## 9. Tombs Carved Like Underground Homes — Tierradentro, Colombia

What looks like an ordinary hillside in southwestern Colombia hides something remarkable: entire burial chambers carved 12 meters deep into volcanic rock. Between the 6th and 10th centuries, ancient communities built chambers with columns, walls, and paintings in red, black, and white — more like decorated homes than graves.

These tombs suggest the builders believed the dead were still part of daily life. Today, 162 of these underground chambers are protected in an archaeological park (UNESCO, 1995).

## 10. A City That Planned for No Rain — Dholavira, India

In the arid lands of Gujarat, the people of Dholavira (part of the Indus Valley Civilization) faced a problem: there was almost no rain. Their answer was brilliant. They carved giant reservoirs into the stone to catch every drop of seasonal water, then built channels to carry it through the city. Streets, homes, and a fortified wall all surrounded these water systems.

They did this as far back as 2650 BCE — thousands of years before modern plumbing. In 2021, UNESCO named Dholavira a World Heritage Site, recognizing a city that showed even survival can be an act of invention.

## Try This at Home

Look around your neighborhood: is there a building with a clever staircase, a watery trick, or a hidden path you never noticed? Take a photo and ask an adult to help you research its story. You might discover that real places near you have histories just as amazing as these.

## Fun Fact

Matera's cave homes were used as movie sets — they stood in for Jerusalem in *The Passion of the Christ* (2004) and for the Batcave in *Spider-Man: No Way Home* (2021). Real caves, fake bats!
