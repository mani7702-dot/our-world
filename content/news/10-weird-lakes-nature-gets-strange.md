# Why do some lakes look like they broke nature's rules?

Most lakes are simple: water collects in a dip, fish swim, people picnic by the shore.

But not these 10 lakes. Each one does something so strange that it looks like a mistake — or like nature deliberately pressed a "weird" button.

## 1. Lake Natron — the lake that can look red

Lake Natron in northern Tanzania is not red all the time, but its shallow water can turn bright orange or red. The water is also hot (warmer than a bath) and very alkaline, thanks to dissolved sodium carbonate — the same powder people use for washing clothes. Tiny algae and shrimp that live in the lake turn red when they are alive or dying, which makes the whole lake glow like a setting sun.

Despite the harsh water, millions of lesser flamingos visit to feed. The alkaline water protects their eggs from most predators. But the water really is dangerous for people: birds and animals that wander in can get stuck in the shallows, and the crusty edges are sharp.

Wikipedia confirms it sits in the East African Rift in Arusha Region. The flamingo connection checks out too — flamingos famously nest here.

## 2. Lake Hillier — the bubblegum-pink lake

Lake Hillier sits on a small island off the south coast of Western Australia. It is naturally **bright pink** all year round — like someone spilled a bottle of bubblegum dye into the water.

The pink colour comes from tiny algae and bacteria that live only in very salty water. The salt level is so high that swimmers float like a cork, just like in the Dead Sea.

## 3. The Boiling Lake — water that steams

On the island of Dominica in the Caribbean, a lake is filled with bubbling, steaming water. It is the world's **second-largest hot lake**. The water looks grey-blue and is covered in vapour because volcanic heat warms it from below.

Hiking to it is tough — the trail climbs right into the jungle and clouds often roll in. But seeing that steamy water is unforgettable.

## 4. Spotted Lake — the summer camp swimming pool that looks like a dalmatian

In British Columbia, Canada, a lake dries up every summer and leaves behind **hundreds of white and brown spots** on its surface. From above, it looks like someone splashed a dalmatian with mud.

Each "spot" is a pool filled with minerals like calcium and sodium sulfate. When the water evaporates, it leaves mineral circles — like rings in a tree that show the lake's history.

## 5. Lake Maracaibo — the lake with never-ending lightning

In Venezuela, Lake Maracaibo sits in a bowl of mountains. Every evening, the warm, wet air rises, hits the peaks, and comes back down — creating **towering storm clouds that flash lightning almost every night of the year**.

Locals say sailors once used the distant glow as a lighthouse. Scientists now measure it as the place on Earth with the most lightning — about 28 flashes per minute at peak season.

## 6. Lost Lake — Oregon's bathtub drain

Near Santiam Pass in Oregon, a small lake **disappears every summer**. When the rains stop and snowmelt slows, the water drains underground through holes in the volcanic rock.

It sits on top of old lava tubes — tunnels left behind when molten rock flowed away leaving hollow pipes. The lake fills up during winter, then drains away like water going down a bathtub drain.

## 7. The Hot Tub of Despair — a lake under the sea

Deep, deep underwater in the Gulf of Mexico — nearly 3,000 feet below the surface — lies a pool of water so salty and dense that it sits like a lake on the seafloor.

Researchers exploring in the submersible *E/V Nautilus* found it and nicknamed it the **"Hot Tub of Despair"**. Its water is more than four times saltier than normal seawater and warmer than expected for the deep ocean. Creatures that fall in can get stuck in the thick brine — it really is deadly. But special mussels and tube worms live right at its edges, fed by microbes that eat chemicals from the seafloor.

## 8. Lake Kivu — the lake holding back a gas bomb

On the border of Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Lake Kivu holds something invisible but enormous: **millions of tons of dissolved carbon dioxide and methane** trapped deep underwater.

Another pair of African lakes — Nyos and Monoun — did explode. In 1986, Lake Nyos suddenly released a cloud of CO₂ that rolled down the valley and suffocated 1,700 people and livestock in their sleep. Lake Kivu is hundreds of times bigger, and it is watched constantly. A 2026 study found the risk stays low for at least the next 500 years — but everyone still checks.

## 9. Abraham Lake — frozen bubbles in summer

In Alberta, Canada, Abraham Lake freezes over in winter. Under the clear ice, you can see thousands of **white bubbles** — like a galaxy of tiny stars frozen in midair.

The bubbles are methane gas. Bacteria at the lake bottom decompose dead plants and release methane. In summer it bubbles up and escapes quietly. In winter, each bubble freezes and stays trapped. By the time new bubbles form, they stack up like layers — a frozen record of the lake's own breathing.

## 10. Williston Lake — the floating forest

In British Columbia, Canada, a whole **floating island of trees** was sighted on Williston Lake. A boater filmed what looks like a mat of healthy trees, plants, and roots drifting across the reservoir like a tiny boat.

Using satellite photos, scientists confirmed it really exists — but only for short periods. When the water level is very high, it can lift a chunk of shoreline plant life and set it adrift. Nobody has pulled it apart to see exactly how it holds together, but it is real.

## Why do lakes get so weird?

The answer is usually simple: **geology, chemistry, and biology**. A lake on a volcano has heat. A lake in a dry basin collects salt. A lake in a deep, sealed basin traps gas. Sometimes life itself — algae, bacteria, plants — changes the colour, smell, or chemistry in ways that look like magic.

The strange part is that scientists understand most of it. They just had to discover the rules.

## Try this at home

- Make a "pink lake" in a jar: add water, a pinch of salt, a drop of food colouring, and a pinch of baking soda. Watch how colours mix.
- Freeze coloured ice cubes with a bubble in the middle: when you look at them, you see trapped gas — just like Abraham Lake's bubbles.
- Draw a map of your imaginary weird lake: does it glow? Does it float? Does it drain?
