# 10 wild facts about bears that prove nature's gentle giants are full of surprises

## 1. Some bears roll in poop to stay warm

Giant pandas in China's Qinling Mountains have a weird habit: they seek out fresh horse manure, sniff it, rub it on their faces, roll in it, and smear it all over their bodies.

It isn't random. Pandas almost always pick the freshest piles, and they roll most often when the temperature drops below 59°F (15°C). Scientists think two chemicals in the manure — beta-caryophyllene and caryophyllene oxide — block a cold-sensor protein in the skin called TRPM8. By dulling the feeling of cold, the dung works like a cozy blanket. As the manure ages, the chemicals fade, which is why pandas always pick the newest piles.

It may look messy, but it is brilliant biology.

**Try this at home:** On a chilly morning, rub a little peppermint oil on the back of your hand. The cool tingle you feel? That is your TRPM8 protein. Pandas found a way to turn it off — using poop.

## 2. Pandas aren't the only bears with surprising tricks

Brown bears (and grizzlies) can use tools. In Alaska, researcher Volker Deecke watched a brown bear pick up a barnacle-covered stone and deliberately rub it along its muzzle and neck — over and over. The rough barnacles acted like a natural scrub brush, helping the bear loosen its thick winter coat. The bear didn't just rub against a stationary rock; it picked up the stone, moved it, and applied it exactly where it itched.

It is one of the first recorded examples of a wild bear making and using a tool for grooming.

**Fun Fact:** Bears have an incredible sense of smell — about 100 times stronger than a bloodhound's. They can smell a carcass from 18 miles away.

## 3. Bears freeze their embryos until the timing is right

Brown bear cubs are born in January or February, even though mating happens in spring and summer. That is a pregnancy that lasts up to eight months — but the embryo doesn't start developing right away.

After fertilization, the tiny embryo becomes a hollow ball of cells called a blastocyst. It floats freely inside the uterus for months, paused in suspended animation. Only when the mother has built up enough fat reserves for winter denning does the embryo implant and begin growing. If she hasn't eaten enough, the embryo is simply reabsorbed.

**Try this at home:** Fill two balloons with the same amount of water. Freeze one, leave one at room temperature. The frozen one stays solid — but when it melts, it releases all its water at once. A bear's delayed implantation is like nature's version of waiting to "release" the next generation when conditions are just right.

## 4. One bear fought in a real war — and got promoted

In 1942, Polish soldiers traveling through Iran met a boy with an orphaned Syrian brown bear cub. They adopted it, named it **Wojtek**, and raised it alongside the 22nd Artillery Supply Company. Wojtek learned to salute, wrestle with soldiers, and even drink beer — just like the men.

When the unit shipped out to Italy, British navy regulations forbade mascots aboard transport ships. The clever workaround? Wojtek was **officially drafted into the Polish Army** as a private — complete with paybook, serial number, and rank. He rode in trucks, carried 100-pound crates of artillery shells, and earned a promotion to corporal. His unit's emblem — a bear carrying an artillery shell — became famous.

After the war, Wojtek lived out his days at Edinburgh Zoo, where he died in 1963 as a gentle celebrity who had seen more of the world than most people.

## 5. Yellowstone's bears were once entertainment

Starting in the early 1900s, Yellowstone National Park treated its bears like a show. Dumpsters behind hotels were left open, and hundreds of tourists would gather at night to watch black and grizzly bears rummage through garbage. It was advertised as a "Lunch Counter — For Bears Only."

Visitors even fed bears from their cars. The bears became bolder, demanding food, damaging property, and injuring people. By 1931, the park averaged over 48 bear-related injuries and 100 property-damage incidents every year.

Starting in the 1960s, the park changed the rules. They closed the dumps, built bear-proof food storage, and taught visitors to keep their distance. Today, bear encounters in Yellowstone drop to about one injury per year — a 98% improvement.

**Fun Fact:** A baby bear is called a **cub**, but a group of bears has no single special name — you just call it a **sloth** (yes, that's confusing — it's not the same as the tree-dwelling sloth animal).

## 6. Bears don't clog their own blood during hibernation

When humans lie in bed for days, blood can pool and form dangerous clots. But brown bears spend months motionless in their dens — sometimes losing 30% of their body weight — and wake up perfectly healthy.

Scientists discovered why: bears dramatically rewire their blood-clotting system during hibernation. A key protein called HSP47 (heat shock protein 47) drops to roughly **one-fiftieth** of its normal level. With less HSP47, platelets are far less likely to grab immune cells and form unnecessary clots.

The same protection shows up in pigs that stay still and in people with long-term paralysis. But healthy volunteers bedridden for short periods do not get the benefit — the body has to "decide" it will be immobile for months before flipping the safety switch.

**Try this at home:** When you sit still for a long car ride, wiggle your toes and stretch your legs every hour. That tiny movement keeps your blood flowing the way a bear's hibernation chemistry does naturally.

## 7. Winnie-the-Pooh was a real bear who rode a train

Before A.A. Milne turned his son's teddy into a storybook character, there was a real Canadian bear named **Winnie**.

In 1914, Lieutenant Harry Colebourn — a Canadian veterinary officer — bought an orphaned black bear cub from a hunter at a train stop in White River, Ontario. He named her Winnie (for his hometown of Winnipeg) and brought her along to England as a regimental mascot.

When his unit shipped out to France, Colebourn left Winnie at London Zoo. She became such a gentle, popular attraction that he donated her permanently. Among her daily visitors was a young boy named Christopher Robin Milne, who renamed his toy bear after her. The rest is literary history.

## 8. A tame bear sat in on Cambridge lectures (and demanded a fellowship)

English poet Lord Byron entered Trinity College, Cambridge in 1805 and quickly gained a reputation for wild behavior. College rules let students keep dogs — but explicitly forbade bears. Byron found the loophole and acquired a tame bear anyway.

"I have got a new friend, the finest in the world, a tame bear," he wrote to a friend in 1807. When someone asked what he planned to do with it, Byron replied that it "should sit for a fellowship."

The bear never got its degree, but it reinforced Byron's rebellious image and became the subject of drawings, poems, and college gossip — proving that even 200 years ago, bears had a way of stealing the spotlight.

## 9. Yellowstone once hosted "bear lunch counters"

Okay, we already covered this one — but it deserves its own spotlight. For decades, Yellowstone's open garbage dumps were the hottest wildlife show in America. Hundreds of visitors paid to watch grizzlies and black bears dig through trash. The bears grew accustomed to humans, lost their fear, and became dangerous. The program was shut down in the 1960s — a lesson every national park now teaches: **never feed wild bears**.

## 10. Zoo polar bears sometimes turn green

Polar bears look white because their transparent, hollow guard hairs scatter light. But in warmer zoos, something odd happens: their fur can take on a swampy green tint.

In 1978, three polar bears at the San Diego Zoo turned green during a hot summer. Researchers expected algae growing on the surface of the fur — but under the microscope, the outer surface was clean. Instead, the algae had colonized the **hollow interior** of the bears' wider guard hairs, like tiny gardens inside each strand.

Warm temperatures, moisture, and algae-filled pools create the right conditions. The green bears were otherwise healthy, and the discoloration was harmless — though zoos sometimes rinsed the coats with salt water to restore their snowy appearance.

**Fun Fact:** Polar bears can swim for days without resting. Their fur is covered in a natural oil that keeps water from soaking in, and their black skin soaks up heat from sunlight.

> **Quick recap:** Bears are way more than big, quiet animals that sleep all winter. They roll in dung like it is perfume, join armies, inspire children's authors, turn green in zoos, and carry cargo in wars — all while being gentle enough to nurse a teddy bear to sleep. If there is one thing bears prove, it is this: never underestimate the quiet giants of the wild.
