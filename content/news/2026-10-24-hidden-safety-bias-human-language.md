# Why do we always say 'safe' — scientists discover a hidden bias in human language?

## The great word hunt

For more than 70 years, scientists thought they understood how words work. They believed that the meaning of a word — whether it makes you feel happy, sad, tense, or calm — could be mapped on three emotional axes. This model, called **VAD** (Valence, Arousal, Dominance), was invented in the 1950s and has been used in psychology, linguistics, and artificial intelligence to figure out how people feel when they read or write.

But what if the real story was hiding in plain sight?

## The safety switch in our speech

A team at the **University of Vermont** decided to test this old model against something much bigger: **billions** of words from books, news articles, social media posts, and spoken language. When they ran the numbers, the classic VAD model covered about 72% of the patterns they found.

But the UVM researchers noticed something deeper. They found that words are actually organized around three fundamental ideas:

1. **Power** — is something weak or powerful?
2. **Danger** — is something safe or dangerous?
3. **Structure** — is something ordered or chaotic?

This new system, which they call **"ousiometrics"** (from the Greek word for "essence"), explained more than **90%** of how word meanings fit together.

The biggest surprise? Across every type of text they studied — from Jane Austen novels to Twitter posts — words about **safety** showed up far more often than words about danger. It was as if every language user carried a hidden switch that defaulted to "safe."

## Why "safe" wins

Psychologists already knew about the **Pollyanna principle** — the tendency for people to use more positive than negative words. Most scientists thought this simply meant humans prefer to feel happy.

The UVM team thinks something more basic is going on. They suggest the Pollyanna effect is actually a shadow of a deeper **safety bias**. Words that signal safety, stability, and predictability help our brains stay alert to threats while keeping us calm in ordinary moments. Over millions of years of evolution, paying attention to what is safe (and what is not) may have become so important that it shaped how we built language itself.

Peter Dodds, the study's senior author, put it simply: **"Expressions of safety are crucial to all language."**

## Try this at home

Test the safety bias yourself! Read a short story out loud — maybe a fable or a comic book. Every time you hear a word that sounds "safe" (like *home*, *warm*, *friend*), raise your hand. Every time you hear a "danger" word (like *dark*, *growl*, *trap*), lower it. Chances are, your hand will be in the air far more than it is down — that is the safety bias in action!

## Fun Fact

The researchers built a tool called the **"ousiometer"** to measure this hidden meaning. It can map how a whole story shifts across the power-danger-structure landscape — kind of like a mood ring for language. They even used it to trace the meaning arc of *Les Misérables*, watching the narrative move from "dangerous" to "orderly" and back again as the plot unfolds.

**for-grownups:** The study was led by Peter Dodds and Chris Danforth at the University of Vermont's Computational Story Lab and published in Science Advances (DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.adr4039). Their "ousiometrics" approach uses mathematical methods to measure word meaning across 20,000+ words analyzed in billions of real-world contexts. The traditional Valence-Arousal-Dominance (VAD) model from the 1950s described meaning through three emotional dimensions. The new framework replaces these with three more fundamental dimensions — power (weak/powerful), danger (safe/dangerous), and structure (ordered/chaotic). The researchers also built the "ousiometer," which can track how word meaning shifts across a story (e.g., mapping the emotional arc of Les Misérables). The apparent positivity of human language — known as the Pollyanna principle — is actually a projection of this deeper safety bias, which the team argues may have evolutionary roots in threat detection.
