# How did a test camera take one of the most stunning photos of space?

## A camera built just for testing

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile is home to one of the most ambitious astronomy projects ever planned. Its main camera — the LSST camera — is the largest digital camera ever built, packing 3.2 billion pixels to capture wide views of the night sky. Astronomers plan to photograph the entire southern sky every few nights for ten years, creating a time-lapse record of the cosmos called the *Legacy Survey of Space and Time*.

But before the big camera could take over, the observatory used a smaller engineering camera named **ComCam** (short for Commissioning Camera). ComCam was not meant for final science — its job was to test the telescope's systems, align the mirrors, and make sure everything worked. Think of it as the test drive before the real journey begins.

## A test run among the stars

ComCam was mounted on the Simonyi Survey Telescope in 2022 and spent about seven weeks observing the sky in late 2024. During that time, it gathered roughly 16,000 exposures — each one a test of the telescope's precision. Even though ComCam is much smaller than the LSST camera, it still produces sharp 144-megapixel images, with each photo covering an area of sky about twice the width of the full Moon.

One of those test images shows a glowing cloud of gas called the **Seagull Nebula** (scientific name **IC 2177),** located about 3,700 light-years from Earth in the constellation **Monoceros** (the Unicorn). The nebula's brightest part, called **Sharpless 2-292**, looks like the head of a seagull with its wings spread across the sky.

## What the seagull is made of

The Seagull Nebula is an **H II region** — a colorful cloud of gas that has been lit up by the intense ultraviolet radiation of hot, young stars. Inside the nebula, baby stars are still forming inside dense pockets of dust and gas. The bright pink glow comes from hydrogen gas that has been energized by these newborn stars, while the blue areas come from dust that reflects light from other young stars.

Thanks to ComCam's infrared and visible-light sensitivity, astronomers could see right through the dusty regions where new stars were born — something that older optical telescopes could not do. Hot, young stars emitted radiation that cleared away obscuring dust, lighting up the surrounding gas and giving us a front-row seat to the drama of star formation.

## A bright future for astronomy

Now that ComCam has finished its commissioning duties, the Rubin Observatory will soon begin its full survey with the giant LSST camera. Over the next decade, that camera will capture millions of galaxies, study dark energy, track near-Earth asteroids, and uncover mysteries we have not even imagined yet.

If a *test* camera can already show us the Seagull Nebula in such stunning detail, imagine what the main camera will reveal.

## Try this at home

You can see the Seagull Nebula too! It is in the constellation Monoceros, which rises high in the sky during autumn and winter in the Northern Hemisphere. With a small telescope or good binoculars, look for a fuzzy, glowing patch near the bright star **Rigel** (in Orion). In a dark-sky location, the nebula's hazy glow becomes visible. You can also explore it online using NASA's [WorldWide Telescope](https://worldwidetelescope.org/) or the [ESO Virtual Sky](https://www.eso.org/public/outreach/education/softwares/skysafari.html) — search for "IC 2177"!

## Fun Fact

The Seagull Nebula is so large that even from 3,700 light-years away, its angular size is nearly **three times the width of the full Moon** in our sky! If you could see the entire nebula at once, it would stretch across six full Moons side by side.
