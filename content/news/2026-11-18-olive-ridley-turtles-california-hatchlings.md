Sea turtles have been swimming in the Pacific for a very long time. But nobody had ever seen one lay its eggs on a beach in California. Not once, not in any record.

Then, on 16 September this year, something happened that had never happened before.

## 1 - A turtle walks out of the sea

Lifeguards at Seal Beach, in Orange County, watched a sea turtle crawl up the sand. She dug a hole, laid her eggs, covered them over, and slipped back into the water, leaving a trail of flipper marks behind her.

It was the **first sea turtle nest ever recorded on the West Coast of the United States**.

Three days later, about eight miles away, a second turtle did the same thing near Huntington Beach Pier. Between the two nests, there were **204 eggs** in the sand - about the size of ping-pong balls.

The turtle is an **olive ridley**. It is the smallest kind of sea turtle, and it usually nests on warm tropical beaches thousands of miles to the south. California is not where olive ridleys are supposed to be.

## 2 - A storm, a cooler, and a long wait

The eggs were sitting on an open beach, and a tropical storm was heading their way. So scientists from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, NOAA and the California Department of Fish and Wildlife made a careful decision.

On 25 September they gently dug up the eggs and drove them to the **Aquarium of the Pacific** in Long Beach, where staff could keep them safe and warm.

Then everyone waited. Sea turtle eggs usually take 45 to 60 days to hatch. For the Seal Beach nest, it took 58.

On Friday evening, 13 November, the first tiny turtle pushed its way out. It was less than two inches long and weighed about as much as a big strawberry.

## 3 - The walk down to the waves

Early on Saturday morning, 14 November, aquarium staff and biologists carried the hatchlings back to Seal Beach, close to where their mother had laid the eggs.

They set them down on the sand in the dark. Then they stepped back and let the babies do the rest.

One by one, all **54** hatchlings scooted down the beach and disappeared into the waves. The next morning, **21** more were released at Huntington Beach.

About 50 eggs are still waiting in their cooler in Long Beach, and may or may not hatch. A third nest, found further north in Ventura County on 1 November, did not make it - the sand there was too cold.

## 4 - Nobody is quite sure why

So why would a tropical turtle suddenly nest in California? Scientists are honest that they do not know yet.

One idea is the weather. This year has brought unusually warm ocean water, and warm water is what olive ridleys like. Another idea is that it is a sign that sea turtle conservation is finally paying off - after decades of protecting nesting beaches and changing fishing nets, olive ridley numbers have grown in many places. More turtles could mean some of them exploring new beaches.

It is also possible that this was simply one strange, one-off year. Nobody can say yet whether California will see turtle nests again next autumn. So scientists will keep watching the sand.

## Try this at home: find the brightest horizon

Baby sea turtles find the ocean by crawling toward the **brightest light** they can see. On a wild beach, that is the sea, because water reflects the sky. That is also why bright lights from buildings near a beach can confuse hatchlings and send them the wrong way.

You can try the same trick with your eyes:

1. At home, turn off all the lights at night and let your eyes get used to the dark for a minute.
2. Look around slowly. Which direction is brightest - a window, a doorway, a streetlight?
3. That glow is exactly what a hatchling follows. If the brightest thing is the ocean, it wins. If it is a car park, the turtle goes the wrong way.
