# How did Spain's brown bears come back from almost nothing?

Thirty years ago, the mountains of northern Spain held one of Europe's most endangered animals. Only about **140 Cantabrian brown bears** were left, split into two little groups that could no longer reach each other across a deep, road-cut gorge system.

Today that number is **370**, and the two groups have grown back together into one population.

## The number that went the wrong way first

The bears live in the Cantabrian Mountains, in Spain's far north. Scientists at the Doñana Biological Station looked back at decades of records and found the population shrinking by 25% between 1982 and 1992.

Then something changed. Two national parks — Somiedo and Redes — were set aside right where the last bears lived, adding protected ground exactly where it was needed. Killing a bear without permission became expensive, with fines reaching around $350,000. Rural villages emptied as people moved to cities, and the forest grew quieter.

Between 2003 and 2012, the bears' range grew by **70%**. The two groups met.

## Bears pick the quiet way round

The researchers also asked *where* bears chose to go. The answer was calm, unbothered country: places close to where other bears already lived, and far from people.

That is not luck. It is what a shy animal does when it has room. Their model of that behaviour turned out to be accurate — it correctly named 77% of the places the bears went on to occupy between 2013 and 2022.

Their world is now more than **17,000 square kilometres** of mountain, roughly the size of Kuwait.

## The catch in the good news

The bears are spreading faster than the paperwork. Spain's official recovery plans were written for a tiny, contained population and still only cover about half the area the bears actually use. Outside that zone there is less monitoring, fewer rangers, and more chance of a bear meeting a bee hive, an orchard, or a farmer.

Next door in Asturias, bears vanished completely. There is around 5,000 square kilometres of good forest there waiting, and almost nothing written down about how to look after bears when they arrive.

## What this shows

Big animals do come back, but only where three things line up: protected ground, a real penalty for killing them, and space to move. Take any of those away and the graph turns back down.

Spain got the first two right, and the bears did what bears do — walked outward. The next job is making sure the map they walk onto is ready for them.
