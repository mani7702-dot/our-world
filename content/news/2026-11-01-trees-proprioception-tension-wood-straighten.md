Stand up and close your eyes. Without looking, you still know that your arm is raised, or that your head is tilted. That sense is called **proprioception** — your body's built-in awareness of where its parts are. For a long time, scientists thought only animals had it. It turns out trees do too — and a new study shows exactly how they use it to stand up straight.

## The leaning tree problem

A young tree that gets knocked sideways faces a tricky problem. It can't just walk to a better spot. Instead, it grows its way back to upright.

Plants use several clues to steer their growth. They sense **gravity** (which way is down), they sense the direction of **light**, and — as researchers showed back in 2012 — they also sense the **shape of their own stems**. That last one is proprioception.

The trouble was that nobody knew how the tree turned that "I'm bent" feeling into actual movement. To find out, the team had to switch off the other clues.

## The spinning-platform experiment

The scientists placed young **poplar trees** on a platform that slowly rotated around its axis, inside a sphere that was lit equally from every direction. The spinning removed the steady pull of gravity as a direction cue, and the even light removed the "grow toward the window" signal. All that was left was the tree's sense of its own body.

At the start, the small trees were lying horizontally, and they had already curved upward toward the sky. Over the following weeks, something remarkable happened: the curved stems slowly **straightened out**.

## Two woods that pull like muscles

When the team studied the wood, they found the trick. The original curve was made by **tension wood** — a special wood that, instead of pushing, *pulls*. Grown along the **upper** side of a stem, it acts like a contracting muscle that hauls the tree upright.

Once the platform started spinning, that upper-side tension wood **stopped forming**. At the same time, brand-new tension wood began growing on the **opposite (lower) side**. The two opposing "muscles" pulled against each other, and the stem slowly eased back to straight.

"It's a genuine sensorimotor loop operating in the woody parts of trees," the researchers said — a sense-and-respond system built into the trunk itself.

## Why it matters

Trees face storms, landslides and steep slopes all the time, so being able to sense a tilt and fix it is a survival skill. Understanding the system could help foresters grow straighter, stronger timber, and help plant breeders develop cereal crops that are less likely to flop over before harvest — a problem called **lodging** that costs farmers a lot of grain.

## Fun Fact

Tension wood is why you can sometimes see a curving, muscle-like pattern at the base of trees on a hillside. The tree spent years pulling itself upright against the slope — and its wood recorded every tug.

## Try this at home

Put a small houseplant on its side in a warm, bright spot for a few days, then stand it back up. Watch over the next week or two: many plants will bend their growing tips back toward the light and upward. You're watching the same bend-and-correct behaviour the poplars used — just powered by light (a response called **phototropism**) instead of pure proprioception.
