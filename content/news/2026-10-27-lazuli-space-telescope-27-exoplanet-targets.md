# 5 Things You Should Know About the Lazuli Space Telescope

**Question:** What if a former Google CEO built a telescope that can see planets hiding in the blinding light of distant stars?

That's exactly what's happening with the **Lazuli Space Telescope** — the world's first privately funded space telescope, set to launch in 2029.

## 1. A 3-Meter Mirror, Bigger Than Hubble

Lazuli has a **3-meter (about 10-foot) mirror** — that's bigger than the Hubble Space Telescope's 2.4-meter mirror. This larger mirror means Lazuli can collect way more light, letting it see fainter objects and finer details in the cosmos.

## 2. It Hunts for Alien Worlds With a Coronagraph

One of Lazuli's three instruments is called the **ExtraSolar Coronagraph (ESC)**. A coronagraph is like a tiny eclipse inside the telescope — it blocks out a star's bright light so scientists can spot the faint planets orbiting around it. Remember from Wikipedia: a coronagraph is "designed to block out the direct light from a star... so that nearby objects can be seen." Lazuli's ESC will study **27 specific exoplanet targets** that have never been imaged this way before.

## 3. It Uses Reflected Starlight, Not Heat

Most telescopes that find distant planets rely on the planet's own heat glow. But Lazuli's coronagraph uses **reflected starlight** — the same light that bounces off a planet and makes it visible to our eyes at night. This is a brand-new way to study planets, and it works because planets don't get dimmer as they age the way heat-based methods do.

## 4. It Flies in a Special Lunar Orbit

Instead of staying near Earth like Hubble, Lazuli will orbit from a **3:1 lunar-resonant orbit** — meaning it loops around the Moon in a special pattern. This position helps it stay stable and respond quickly to surprise cosmic events. It can react to emergencies in **under four hours**, faster than any other telescope of its size.

## 5. Scientists Can Use It for Free

Even though Eric Schmidt (Google's former CEO) helped start the project, Lazuli is designed as a **general-purpose facility**. That means astronomers from around the world can apply for time to use it — just like applying for any other major telescope. The team wants it to be a global resource, not just a private one.

## Try This at Home

Next time you're outside on a dark night, look for the brightest object in the sky after the Sun sets (that's Venus). Imagine a telescope that can block that brilliant light and reveal invisible planets hiding right next to it. That's what Lazuli will do — but from space, where Earth's blurry atmosphere can't mess up the view!

Use a star-map app like SkySafari or Stellarium to find Jupiter or Saturn. Notice how much brighter they appear than the faint stars around them. Now imagine trying to spot a tiny speck of dust sitting right next to a searchlight beam — that's the challenge coronagraphs solve.

## Fun Fact

As of October 2026, **6,445 confirmed exoplanets** have been discovered (per Wikipedia's Exoplanet article). Lazuli's first 27 targets are all giant gas planets — the kind that are big and bright enough to study with reflected light. The dream is that one day, a space telescope like this could even spot signs of life, like oxygen or methane, in a distant world's atmosphere.

## for-grownups:

The Lazuli Space Telescope (arXiv:2601.02556) is a real 3-meter private observatory concept led by Eric Schmidt and Saul Perlmutter (Nobel laureate, 2011). Its ExtraSolar Coronagraph (ESC) will directly image mature exoplanets via reflected starlight — a technique that decouples observations from planetary age. The coronagraph design follows established principles (Wikipedia, "Coronagraph"): internal occulting masks block stellar light to reveal faint companions. The first target list of 27 giant exoplanets was proposed by Logan Pearce (University of Michigan) and Ewan Douglas (Steward Observatory, University of Arizona). Key technical specs confirmed via arXiv: 10⁻⁸ raw contrast, 10⁻⁹ post-processed contrast, 35′ × 12′ wide-field imager, R ≈ 100–500 integral field spectrograph, and <4-hour target-of-opportunity response time from a 3:1 lunar-resonant orbit.
