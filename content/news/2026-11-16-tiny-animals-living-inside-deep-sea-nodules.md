Imagine a stone on the seafloor, four kilometres down. No sunlight. Water pressing on it with the weight of a hundred elephants. It looks like the most lifeless thing on Earth.

Now imagine opening it up and finding **245 tiny animals** packed inside.

That is exactly what a team of scientists found when they took apart rocks from the bottom of the Pacific Ocean. Not one lucky rock — they opened **244 of them**, and the cracks inside were full of life.

## 1. What these rocks actually are

They are called **polymetallic nodules**. The name is a mouthful, but the idea is simple: they are dark, lumpy stones about the size of a fist, made mostly of iron and manganese, plus small amounts of metals like nickel and copper.

They grow in the **Clarion-Clipperton Zone**, a huge stretch of deep Pacific seafloor between Mexico and Hawaii, at depths of **4,000 to 6,000 metres**. Down there the bottom of the ocean is soft mud — which is why a hard stone is a big deal. The nodules grow incredibly slowly, adding just a few millimetres every *million* years. Some of the ones the scientists studied are millions of years old.

## 2. The surprise was inside, not on top

Scientists already knew animals live *on* nodules, the way barnacles live on a rock at the beach. This study asked a different question: what lives *inside* them?

The nodules are not solid. They are wrinkled and cracked, full of tiny pores and tunnels, like a sponge made of stone. The team collected nodules with box corers and diving robots, brought them to the lab, and carefully broke them open to look in the gaps.

Across all 244 nodules they counted **4,139 animals** — an average of about **17 per rock**. One especially crowded nodule held **245**.

All of them were smaller than a millimetre: worms called **nematodes**, tiny shrimp-like **copepods**, and single-celled shell-builders called **foraminifera**. Too small to see without a microscope, but very much alive.

> "The crevices in the polymetallic nodules function as small shelters and provide a home to animals that we do not encounter in the soft sediment of the seafloor," said Dr Coral Diaz-Recio Lorenzo, one of the lead authors.

## 3. A neighbourhood of their own

Here is the part that really caught the scientists' attention. They compared the animals inside the rocks with the animals in the mud right next to them. They were **not the same community**.

The creatures in the cracks are a group you basically do not find out in the open mud. And the bigger the nodule, the more animals it held — bigger rock, wider cracks, more tenants.

In other words, these stones are not just objects sitting on the seafloor. Each one is a tiny, ancient apartment building.

## 4. Why it matters

The metals inside nodules are used in batteries and electronics, and some companies want to collect them from the deep sea. The scientists point out that removing the nodules would remove the homes inside them too — homes that took millions of years to build.

Nobody is saying the ocean is doomed. The point is quieter and more interesting: we are still finding out who lives down there, and every time someone looks closely, the deep sea turns out to be busier than we thought.

## Try this at home

Be a micro-habitat detective. Find a rough stone, a piece of old brick, or a lump of coral on a beach or in a garden. Look at its surface with a magnifying glass, then imagine the tunnels and cracks going inward. How many creatures could fit inside something that size if each one were the size of a grain of sand? Draw your own "cutaway" picture of the stone with the animals inside — that is exactly what the scientists did in the lab, just with a microscope instead of crayons.

## Fun fact

A nodule grows about as fast as your fingernails would if you trimmed them once every few thousand years. The stone in this story started forming long before there were any humans on Earth.
