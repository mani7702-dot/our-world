In the middle of the Pacific Ocean, the sea has been quietly rising. Not because of waves - because the water itself has been getting warmer, and warm water takes up more room.

NASA scientists have been watching that rise from space, and what they see looks a lot like the start of one of the biggest El Niño events ever recorded.

## A warm bath the size of a continent

**El Niño** is a pattern that shows up every two to seven years. Normally, steady winds called the **trade winds** push warm surface water west, towards Asia and Australia.

That warm water piles up in the west, so the sea there sits a little higher than the sea in the east. Sometimes the trade winds weaken. When they do, warm water starts sliding back east across the ocean.

That weakens the winds even more, which pushes even more warm water east. It is a loop that feeds itself. The result is a huge tongue of unusually warm water stretching along the equator towards South America.

Warm water changes where rain falls, so weather far away - in California, in Africa, in Indonesia - can shift because of it.

## The trick: the sea is not flat

How do you measure something as big as the Pacific? You do it from space. Satellites can measure the height of the ocean surface to within a couple of centimetres.

The trick is that warm water expands. So where the sea sits higher than usual, there is usually extra heat stored below. NASA compared two maps: the Pacific on **13 October 1997**, and the Pacific on **12 October 2026**.

The 1997 map came from a satellite called **TOPEX/Poseidon**. The 2026 map came from **Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich**. They look almost the same.

In both, the sea is low in the west and high in the east, with warm water running all the way to the coast of South America.

## Why 1997 keeps coming up

1997-98 was one of the strongest El Niño events ever recorded. That year, the changes in rainfall helped fuel huge fires in Indonesia and the Amazon.

It also caused drought and failed crops in southern Africa, and brought heavy rain and flooding to Southern California.

"What's unique about altimetry is that we have a 35-year record from nearly identical satellites flying almost the same ground track, with each mission overlapping the next," said **Josh Willis**, a scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

"That lets us look back in time and compare this El Niño to what happened in 1997. And right now, this one is on track to be even bigger."

## What scientists still don't know

The 2026 El Niño arrived more suddenly than the one in 1997. And looking similar is not the same as behaving the same.

"Just because this El Niño looks like 1997 doesn't mean it will be exactly the same - it's just our best guess at the moment," Willis said.

So scientists will keep watching the ocean rise, one satellite pass at a time.

## Try this at home: warm water takes up more room

You can see the idea behind the satellite maps in your kitchen.

1. Fill a tall glass to the brim with cold water and stand it in a bowl.
2. Mark the level with a piece of tape.
3. Let it warm up to room temperature for an hour, then look again.

The level creeps up. Nothing was added - the water simply expanded as it warmed. That is exactly what makes the Pacific bulge during El Niño, and why a satellite can spot it from space.
