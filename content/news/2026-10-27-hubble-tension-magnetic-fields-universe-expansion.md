## 2 Ways to Measure How Fast the Universe Expands — and Why They Disagree

Imagine you're on a picnic and you blow up a balloon. The dots on the balloon move apart as it inflates — but if you had a ruler that kept changing length, your measurements would never agree!

Something similar is happening with our universe. Scientists have two different "rulers" for measuring how fast everything is flying apart, and they give **different answers**. This puzzle is called the **Hubble tension**, and it’s one of the biggest mysteries in astronomy today.

## 1 Way: Looking at the Baby Universe (Gives 67)

One method uses the **cosmic microwave background** — the faint afterglow left over from the Big Bang itself. The Planck Space Telescope has mapped tiny patterns in this ancient light, revealing how the universe looked when it was just 380,000 years old.

From those baby-universe patterns, scientists calculate: the universe is expanding at about **67 kilometers per second per megaparsec** (a megaparsec is roughly 3.26 million light-years). Think of it like this — if a galaxy is one megaparsec away, it's racing away from us at 67 km/s. If it's twice as far, it's moving at 140 km/s.

## 2 Way: Watching Nearby Exploding Stars (Gives 73)

The other method is more direct. Astronomers use objects called **Type Ia supernovae** — exploding stars that always burn with the same brightness. By measuring how dim these stellar explosions appear, scientists can calculate how far away they are. They then compare distance to how fast the galaxy is moving away (measured via redshift).

They also use **Cepheid variable stars** as cosmic yardsticks — these stars pulse in and out like heartbeat beacons, and their rhythm tells astronomers the distance.

This "nearby universe" method says the expansion rate is about **73 km/s/Mpc** — noticeably faster than the CMB method.

## Where Could the Difference Come From?

The gap between 67 and 73 may seem small, but it's **statistically huge** — too big to be a fluke. If both methods are correct, then something important is missing from our understanding of the cosmos.

One exciting new idea comes from **primordial magnetic fields** — incredibly weak magnetic fields left over from the first moments after the Big Bang. These fields may have subtly changed how the universe’s first light traveled, throwing off the Planck-based calculation. Studying this could open a window into physics at energies far beyond anything we can reach on Earth.

## Try This at Home

✨ Look up at the night sky on a dark night. Every star you see is moving away from you — not because it's flying through space, but because **space itself is stretching**, carrying everything along with it. The Andromeda galaxy is the only major exception: it's close enough that gravity wins, and it's actually heading toward us for a cosmic collision in about 4.5 billion years!

## Fun Fact

The Hubble constant was named after **Edwin Hubble**, who in 1929 discovered that the universe is expanding. But the idea actually came first from Belgian priest and astronomer **Georges Lemaître** in 1927, who derived the expansion from Einstein's equations. Hubble’s better data confirmed it two years later.

Primordial magnetic fields — proposed to also solve the cosmic magnetism puzzle — offer one potential bridge, as they could alter recombination-era physics that shifts the CMB-inferred expansion rate.
