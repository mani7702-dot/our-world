# Why is a 45-million-year-old crocodile skull missing its nose? It was found in the very rocks that built the Great Sphinx

## A fossil that matches the Sphinx in the strangest way

When you visit the Great Sphinx of Giza in Egypt, the first thing everyone notices is that its mighty nose is gone. Now, scientists have found a fossil skull that shares a very similar secret. The skull belongs to a type of crocodile that lived about 45 million years ago — and the tip of its snout is missing too.

The strange match does not mean the creature inspired the Sphinx (the monument lost its nose centuries ago, most likely during target practice or erosion). It is a coincidence so neat that the researchers decided to name the new species after the Egyptian god Heru (also spelled Horus), who was often linked to the sphinx.

## Carved from the same rock

The fossil skull was pulled from the **Mokattam Formation** — the very limestone that ancient Egyptians quarried to carve the Great Sphinx itself. That means the stone the builders removed was already 45 million years old when they began chiseling. Inside that stone were the bones of crocodiles that once swam in the warm, shallow seas that covered the region long before any humans walked there.

In the 1920s, workmen digging the Sphinx trench uncovered several crocodile fossils. At the time, experts thought they all belonged to a living genus called **Tomistoma** — the false gharial, which still lives in Indonesia and Malaysia. But something never quite fit. Tomistoma today lives far, far away from Egypt.

## A second look using CT scans

Now, a team led by Dr. Paul Burke from Sweden's Museum of Natural History compared the old fossils with modern scans. Using **CT scanning** — which can see inside bone without chopping it up — they found important differences in the skull shape, the teeth, and the bone structure.

Those differences meant the fossils were not Tomistoma after all. The scientists re-described them as a **brand-new species**: *Herugavialis cairensis*. "Heru" honors the Egyptian god; "gavialis" means it was a gharial-type crocodile; "cairensis" means it came from near Cairo.

Only two species of gharial exist today — one in India's Ganges and one in Southeast Asia. But the fossil record shows gharials were once found on nearly every continent, living in shallow seas that later dried up.

## Why the snout is gone

The skull is missing its snout tip, and that damage happened after the animal died — probably from the rock cracking over millions of years, or from the fossil being handled roughly during early fossil-collecting trips. The break is clean enough that researchers can see the original bone texture stopped there.

"It's a great reminder that fossils are not perfect casts of past life," said the team. "They are real bones that have been worn down by time."

## What it teaches us

Crocodiles have been around for over 200 million years — long before the dinosaurs, and long after they are gone. Each new fossil helps fill in a page of a story that most people forget: the parts of Earth's history that happened before humans even existed.

The limestone under the Sphinx was once a sea floor. The crocodiles that lived there could not have imagined that millions of years later, humans would carve their bones' resting place into a giant stone face — and give that face the same missing nose as the ancient reptile.

## Try this at home

- **Paper excavation:** Layer a few sheets of crumpled newspaper in a cardboard box, then pour Plaster of Paris or thick white glue over them. Once it hardens, carefully dig with a butter knife until you "discover" a hidden shape — just like a fossil.
- **Gharial teeth:** Look up a photo of a gharial skull. Count how many teeth you can see in its long, narrow snout. Gharials have more than 100 — but most of those teeth only show on the *bottom* jaw when the mouth is closed, a trick no other crocodile has.
- **Rock timeline:** Find a piece of layered rock or a stack of old newspapers. Each layer represents a moment in time. Imagine one layer is the sea floor 45 million years ago — what animals might have been swimming in it?

## Fun Fact

Modern caimans and crocodiles carry genes that let them regrow a tail or a patch of skin if they are born with a spinal injury. Some day, studying those genes in living crocodilians could even help doctors understand how to heal spinal damage in people — nature's own medical research department.
