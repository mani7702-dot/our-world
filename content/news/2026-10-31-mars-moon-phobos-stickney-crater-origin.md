## A giant scar on a tiny moon

Mars has two moons, and the bigger one — **Phobos** — is nothing like our own. It is small and lumpy, shaped a bit like a potato, and it is marked by a single, enormous scar: a crater so big that if you could stand on Phobos and look down at Stickney Crater, it would fill most of your view.

The crater is called **Stickney**, after the 19th-century astronomer who first spotted it. It measures about **9 kilometres (5.6 miles)** across — big enough that an impact strong enough to carve it should, by all rights, have shattered the moon entirely. So how did Phobos survive?

## A moon with a buried secret

That is the question Dr **Benjamin Haser** and colleagues are trying to answer. Their idea is that the Stickney impact was so powerful that it squeezed the rock beneath it, packing it tighter and making a hidden region denser than the rest of the moon. That denser pocket changes the way Phobos wobbles and pulls on nearby spacecraft — and those tiny gravitational ripples are like a fingerprint of what the inside of the moon looks like.

In their **2026 paper** (published in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*), Haser and co-author Thomas Andert ran computer models showing that such a compressed zone could explain what we already measure from orbit. If the dense band beneath Stickney is responsible for all the gravity oddities scientists see, it would make up about **7 per cent of Phobos' entire volume** — a big, hidden lump sitting under one giant hole.

## Why Phobos' birthday matters

But the Stickney crater is only part of the puzzle. The bigger mystery is: **where in space did Phobos come from in the first place?**

There are two main teams in this debate.

**Team Capture** says Phobos started life as an asteroid from the asteroid belt. After all, it is small and lumpy, a bit like a "rubble-pile" asteroid held together only by its own weak gravity. The trouble is that Phobos' orbit sits almost perfectly in Mars' equatorial plane — and captured moons usually have crooked, tilted orbits.

**Team Impact** says a Mars-sized object crashed into Mars billions of years ago, hurling debris into space. That debris eventually clumped together to form Phobos and its even smaller sibling, Deimos — the same way many scientists think Earth's Moon was born. Signs that support this include Phobos carrying phyllosilicates (clay-like minerals) that also appear on Mars' surface, and its low density suggesting a porous, sponge-like interior.

Pinning down the answer is tricky when Phobos is just 22 km across and orbits Mars every 7 hours. But the Japanese **MMX mission** (Martian Moons Exploration), due to launch in late 2026, plans to study Phobos close-up and bring samples home by **mid-2031**.

## Phobos is running out of time

Phobos is also a moon on borrowed time. It orbits so close to Mars that the planet's gravity is slowly pulling it inward at about **1.8 centimetres per year** — that is roughly the length of a fingernail every decade. As it creeps closer, Mars' tidal forces will eventually stretch Phobos beyond its breaking point. The moon will either **shatter into a ring** around Mars, or crash straight into the planet.

None of that will happen for millions of years. But it makes Phobos a record of the very deep past of the Solar System, still actively changing today — a frozen time capsule that is also a ticking clock.

## Try this at home

Take a lump of Play-Doh or plasticine. Roll it into a small, rough ball — that is Phobos. Now press your thumb firmly into one side to make a big dent — that is the Stickney impact. If your "dent" goes right through, the model moon is too fragile to survive, just like Phobos should have been. But if you make the "rock" soft and squishy (low density), your thumb sinks in and the moon holds its shape. That is why some scientists think Phobos might be a cosmic sponge.

**Track the moon:** On a clear night, look for Mars in the sky. Through binoculars, Mars looks like a bright red pinprick — and Phobos is far too faint to see without a telescope. But every few months, Phobos passes in front of (or "occults") a star that telescopes on Earth can spot. That invisible graze is how astronomers weigh the moon and test its shape, all from 50 million kilometres away.

## Fun Fact

Phobos rises in the **west** and sets in the **east** on Mars — the opposite of our Moon — because it orbits faster than Mars rotates. From the Martian surface, you could watch Phobos race across the sky twice in a single day, and it would look like a tiny star streaking past. It was also the first alien moon ever discovered: American astronomer Asaph Hall spotted it in 1877.
