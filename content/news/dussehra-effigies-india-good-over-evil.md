# Why Do Millions of People Light Fires on Hilltops Each October?

If you have ever driven past a quiet hill on a windy October evening and seen a giant paper giant burst into flame, spilling sparks like falling stars down the slope, you were watching Dussehra — one of the world's oldest and most joyful "good over evil" parties, and one that lights up the whole of India at once.

## The story behind the bonfire

Long, long ago, people say, there was a king with ten heads and a heart full of mean plans. A prince named Ram — kind but brave — fought him for nine days. On the tenth day, the prince won, and the king's pride came crashing down in a roar of fire and sparks.

That is the heart of Dussehra: the moment the good guy's light is brighter than the bad guy's shadow. Every year, families build giant models of the ten-headed king out of paper, straw, and colored cloth, stuff them with fireworks, and set them alight in empty fields or on hillsides, shouting, "Ram is king!" as sparks swirl up into the sky.

## A festival that moves around

Here is the neat part: Dussehra is not stuck to one date. It follows the moon, not the calendar on your phone. Each year it lands in late September or October, when the air turns crisp and the mango trees have dropped their leaves.

Where you celebrate, the party changes. In the north, the big moment is a mile-long procession with drummers and dancers carrying torches. In the east and south, the week leading up to Dussehra — called Navratri — is already a string of dance parties in colorful dresses. On the final night, the goddess Durga's victory over a shape-shifting buffalo is celebrated with drums and lamps, and the same fireworks that mark Ravana's defeat light the sky a little later.

## Tiny traditions, huge meaning

The big burning is only part of the fun. Homes get rangoli — pretty colored-powder patterns — at the doorway to welcome luck. Children dress up in costume (some as young Ravan with all ten heads, some as the victorious prince). Markets fill with sweets shaped like little soldiers and lotus flowers, and the smell of popcorn and jalebi — syrupy fried dough — swirls through the lanes.

But the real magic is the shared story. For one night, millions of people tell the same tale at the same time — that light, even a tiny flame held in the palm, can outshine a very, very big shadow.

## Fun Fact

The biggest Ravana effigy ever recorded stood over 120 feet tall (that is taller than a ten-story building!) and was burned in Mumbai in 2013. It took 1,500 volunteers six months to build, using 2,200 rolls of paper and more than 500 kg of fireworks.

### Try this at home

Make a mini effigy from a toilet-paper tube, construction paper, and a tea-light candle (ask a grown-up to light it, and never leave it alone). As the flame curls up, remember: every culture has a "good over evil" fire story, and yours is one of the oldest ones still burning bright.
