# What does the edge of a sparkling spiral galaxy look like from 37 million light-years away?

## A Side-On View of Cosmic Beauty

Imagine floating in space, 37 million light-years away, looking toward a swirling galaxy. From that viewpoint, the galaxy would appear as a thin, glowing streak across the dark sky — its flat disk seen exactly edge-on. This is the view that NASA's **Hubble Space Telescope** captured of **NGC 1827**, a spiral galaxy in the constellation **Columba**.

The image, taken with Hubble's Advanced Camera for Surveys, shows the galaxy's edge like a glittering ribbon. Bright **blue stars** dot the galaxy's disk, while wispy **brown dust lanes** weave through the starlight like brushstrokes on a cosmic canvas. One particularly brilliant star shines near the center, its light flaring into four spikes — an artifact created when light bends through Hubble's camera.

## The Shape of a Spiral

Not all spiral galaxies look the same. Astronomers sort them into groups based on their shape. **NGC 1827 is an "intermediate" spiral**, meaning it sits between two main types:

- **Barred spirals** have a long, straight bar of stars cutting through the center.
- **Unbarred spirals** have a rounder, more circular center with spiral arms winding outward.

NGC 1827's structure is somewhere in between, with a gently defined central region and spiral arms that wind outward from the disk. From Earth, we see it sideways — which is why the Hubble photo shows a thin edge rather than the familiar face-on pinwheel shape.

## A Galaxy Among Many

NGC 1827 is not alone in space. It belongs to a small family of galaxies called the **NGC 1808 group**, which includes 14 galaxies orbiting one another through the darkness. NGC 1827 was first spotted on November 28, 1837, by British astronomer **John Herschel**, who catalogued thousands of celestial objects from his observatory in South Africa.

Spiral galaxies like NGC 1827 are among the most common shapes in the universe — along with irregular galaxies, they make up about **60% of all galaxies** we can see. Our own **Milky Way** is also a spiral, though it is a barred spiral. Looking at NGC 1827 from 37 million light-years away gives us a glimpse of what our own galaxy might look like to an outside visitor.

## Try this at home

You can see NGC 1827 yourself! Use a small telescope or good binoculars on a clear, dark night. Look in the constellation Columba (the Dove), near the bright star **Phact** (Imai). The galaxy appears as a faint, elongated smudge. Or, explore it online using NASA's [WorldWide Telescope](https://worldwidetelescope.org/) — just search for "NGC 1827"!

## Fun Fact

NGC 1827 is so far away that when Hubble took this photo, the light had been traveling for **37 million years** — that means dinosaurs were just beginning to walk the Earth when this light first left the galaxy!
