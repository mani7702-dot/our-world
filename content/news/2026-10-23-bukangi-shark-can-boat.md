# Can a shark learn to swim back home? The tale of Bukangi the canal shark

## A lost shark becomes a city's hero

In the port city of **Busan, South Korea**, a very unusual resident has been making waves. Her name is **Bukangi** — a nickname given by locals — and she is a three-metre-long (about 10 feet) **dusky shark** who somehow found her way into a calm canal instead of the wide, open ocean.

Bukangi is not just any shark. She is a **female dusky shark**, a species that usually swims far out at sea. But since **September 18, 2026**, she has stayed in the artificial canal at **North Port Waterfront Park**. Scientists think she followed her fish prey into the canal as ocean temperatures warmed, then could not find her way back out.

## A month-long rescue starring the whole city

When people first saw Bukangi, they were surprised — and then they were rooting for her. She even became so popular that the city named her an **honorary ambassador of Busan**!

For weeks, rescuers tried to help. The **Korea Coast Guard** sent boats and patrol vessels. The **National Institute of Fisheries Science** studied how to bring her safely back to sea. They used big **nets** and special **barriers** to gently guide her, but Bukangi kept swimming back into the canal.

On **October 21**, in a **livestreamed operation** that lasted more than eight hours, rescue workers used a large net to carefully shepherd Bukangi toward the open harbour. Hundreds of people gathered on the park hillside, waving and cheering goodbye. It looked like a happy ending!

But the very next morning, Bukangi had **swum back** through a barrier and into the canal again. This time, she got stuck between two layers of netting. Rescuers rushed in, removed the outer net, and made the barriers stronger so she could not return.

## Why can't she just leave?

Even though Bukangi now swims farther out each day, she still **circles back** to the canal mouth. Marine experts say that is actually normal shark behaviour — she is checking a familiar place. They also remind us that sharks are wild animals, not pets, and the kindest thing is to let her find the ocean on her own time.

The warmer the sea gets, the more sharks like Bukangi may wander into places they do not belong. That is why scientists are watching — and why a whole city learned a big lesson about sharing Earth with amazing sea creatures.

## Try this at home

Next time you visit a harbour or estuary, bring binoculars and scan the water for movement. Look for a **dorsal fin** cutting through the waves — that could be a shark, seal, or dolphin saying hello! You can also draw a picture of Bukangi: a grey, torpedo-shaped body, a tall curved dorsal fin on top, and a smaller fin near her tail. Remember: if you ever see a wild animal in the wrong place, the best help is telling a grown-up ranger or lifeguard — never try to touch or feed it.

**Fun fact:** Dusky sharks can live up to **40 years** and grow over **4 metres** long. They are gentle giants of the ocean — nothing like the scary movie version!

**For grown-ups:** Bukangi (Carcharhinus obscurus) is listed as threatened by the IUCN Red List. Her entry into a Busan canal is linked to warming sea temperatures pushing prey species northward. The Korea Coast Guard and National Institute of Fisheries Science conducted three rescue attempts; the most recent, on Oct 21, involved an eight-hour net-guided operation livestreamed to the public. As of Oct 23, Bukangi remains near the canal mouth, swimming progressively further out. She was appointed an honorary ambassador of Busan.
