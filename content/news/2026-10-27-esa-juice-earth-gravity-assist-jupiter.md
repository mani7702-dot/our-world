# 1. How did ESA's Juice use Earth as a slingshot to reach Jupiter — and why is that so clever?

# The spacecraft that treats planets like stepping stones

Imagine throwing a tennis ball at a moving train — if the train is heavy and fast, the ball bounces back much faster than you threw it. That's the same trick ESA's **Juice** spacecraft just pulled off, but with Earth standing in for the train and space for the bouncing.

On **September 28, 2026**, Juice raced past Earth at just after 11:45 UTC, skimming high above the Indian Ocean. Instead of lighting its engines, it let Earth's gravity do the work — and walked away **3.5 km/s faster**, pointing it confidently toward Jupiter.

## 2. Why not just point and go?

A trip to Jupiter is a long one — about 600 million kilometers on average. Juice launched in April 2023 and won't arrive until **July 2031**. That's eight years of coasting through the darkness.

Firing rockets the whole way would need an impossible amount of fuel — thousands of kilograms that no rocket can carry. So spacecraft use **gravity assists** instead: they fly close to a planet, stealing a little of its orbital momentum to gain speed and change direction, just like a soccer ball bouncing off a curved wall.

Juice is doing this dance with Earth and Venus, picking up speed each time. The September 2026 flyby kicked Juice's path by **20 degrees** and gave it that 3.5 km/s boost — all while burning a tiny fraction of its fuel reserves. The saved fuel will pay off big time when Juice arrives at Jupiter and needs to orbit and study the icy moons.

## 3. Earth also got a close-up photo op

The flyby wasn't just about steering — it was also science. As Juice glided past, its cameras captured a stunning view of our blue planet hanging in space. The monitoring camera snapped Earth from 8,640 km up, showing swirling clouds and the bright glare of sunlit oceans.

Scientists also used the chance to check all ten of Juice's scientific instruments on a real target they know well. This was the third instrument-calibration opportunity during the mission (after a 2024 lunar-Earth assist and 2025 observations of Comet 3I/ATLAS). Each test gets Juice's cameras and sensors ready for the grand finale: studying **Ganymede, Callisto, and Europa** — Jupiter's three largest moons that hide saltwater oceans beneath their icy shells.

One special bonus: while flying through Earth's magnetic tail — the long stream of magnetic field stretching away from the Sun — Juice measured magnetic fields and charged particles far from the planet. This data helps scientists understand how Earth's magnetic shield works, and it could prepare them for what Juice will see around Jupiter's own powerful magnetosphere.

Juice still has one more Earth flyby coming: **January 2029**, which will put it on the final approach path to Jupiter in 2031.

## 4. What will Juice find around Jupiter?

When Juice arrives at the gas giant, it will make **35 close flybys** of Ganymede, Callisto, and Europa — looking for signs that these moons could host life. Under miles of ice, each moon is thought to hold a vast saltwater ocean. Juice will map their surfaces, probe beneath the ice with radar, and sample their mysterious plumes.

**Fun Fact:** Juice is the first spacecraft ever to orbit a moon other than Earth's own Moon — it will eventually go into orbit around Ganymede, the only moon known to have its own magnetic field and a subsurface ocean.

**Try this at home:** You can build a simple "gravity assist" simulator using a marble, a basketball, and a towel. Roll the marble toward the basketball on the towel — the marble will curve around the ball and shoot off in a new direction, just like a spacecraft using a planet's gravity. Try rolling at different angles to see how the exit path changes!
