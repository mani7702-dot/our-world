# How does a dog sniff out an invisible sea invader called 'sea vomit'?

## A slimy orange blanket from the sea floor

Imagine a creature that looks like orange slime spreading across rocks and seaweed. It can cover entire seabeds, pushing out the plants and animals that live there. This is **sea vomit** — not something you sneeze, but a real sea creature called the **carpet sea squirt** (*Didemnum vexillum*).

It sounds gross, and it is. But it is also why a clever Labrador named **Sgiath** is becoming a sea-faring hero.

## Why is 'sea vomit' so harmful?

The carpet sea squirt is a **colonial tunicate** — a tiny sea animal that lives in groups. One colony can hold thousands of individuals, all squeezed together under a slimy orange sheet. The sea squirt is **not native to the UK**. It started life in Japan, then hitchhiked on boat hulls and oyster shipments to England and Wales in 2008.

Once it arrives, it grows fast:
- It can smother **native plants and animals**, starving them of space and food
- It **clogs aquaculture pens** and boat parts — costing millions of pounds a year to clean up
- In some Dutch waters, it covers **95% of the seabed**

In short, it is a quiet invader that changes whole underwater worlds — and humans have no easy way to spot it early.

## Enter Sgiath: the sea-squirt-hunting dog

Sgiath (pronounced **Skee-ah**) is a **black Labrador** who lives in the Scottish Highlands with her owner, marine ecologist **Dr Texa Sim**.

When she was a puppy, Sgiath was training to be a **mountain rescue dog**. She was so good at finding hidden things that Dr Sim wondered: *Could Sgiath use her nose underwater?*

**She absolutely could.**

At low tide, Sgiath trots along beaches and rocks. When she catches the scent of a sea-squirt colony, she gives what handlers call a **"passive indication"** — she stops, stares, and points. No barking, no bouncing. Just a quiet nose-led show.

Dr Sim says Sgiath is "always right" when she flags a colony. The dog has found **hundreds** of sea-squirt patches hiding among seaweed and on piers, docks, and oyster cages.

## Why does this matter?

Sea vomit is **tricky to spot**. It looks just like orange slime mixed in with seaweed — easy to miss. Even expert divers can walk right past a colony.

But dogs have a **superpower**: they can smell things humans cannot. And when a dog finds sea squirt early, scientists can **remove it before it spreads**.

That is why **Sgiath** was just named **Animal of the Year** at the International Fund for Animal Welfare's (IFAW) 26th annual Animal Action Awards on **Thursday, 15 October 2026**.

Dr Martyn Kurr, a marine biologist at Bangor University, says: *"These wonderful animals can smell changes in water that signal a marine invasion which otherwise might have gone unnoticed."*

So next time you visit the beach, remember: the real guardians might not be lifeguards or warning signs. They might be a Labrador with a very good nose, walking the tideline, looking for slime.

### Try this at home
You can train a dog to find almost anything with the right treats and patience — but only an adult should help. At home, try hiding a small treat under one of three cups while a dog watches. Swap the cups around, then say "Find it!" The dog will use its nose to pick the right cup every time.
