A compass works anywhere on Earth. Stand in a desert, a forest or a boat in the middle of the ocean, and the needle still swings to the north. That is because Earth's magnetic field is tidy: it behaves like one enormous bar magnet sitting almost straight up through the planet.

Now travel billions of kilometres out to Uranus and Neptune. Their magnetic fields are anything but tidy.

## What Voyager 2 found

Only one spacecraft has ever been close to these two worlds. NASA's **Voyager 2** flew past Uranus in January 1986 and past Neptune in August 1989, and what it measured nobody expected.

Earth's magnetic field is tilted just about 11 degrees away from the axis the planet spins around. Uranus's field is tilted **59 degrees**. Neptune's is tilted **47 degrees**.

They are lopsided too. On Earth, the field is generated deep in the middle of the planet. On the ice giants, the part of the planet doing the work sits well away from the centre.

And the neat north-south part of the field is tiny there. It makes up only around **12 per cent** of the magnetic energy at their surfaces. The rest is a jumble.

For nearly forty years, nobody could explain it.

## The new idea: it depends on how well the inside carries electricity

A team led by **Chi Yan** and **Krista Soderlund** at the University of Texas at Austin, working with researchers at UCLA, Imperial College London and Northumbria University, built 3D computer models of what happens deep inside the two planets. Their study was published on 3 November 2026 in the journal *Communications Physics*.

Here is the key idea. Deep inside Uranus and Neptune, the pressure and temperature are enormous - high enough to break water molecules apart into charged pieces called **ions**. Charged particles can carry electric current, and electric currents create magnetic fields.

The deeper you go, the more of the water is broken apart, so the deep layers carry electricity better than the shallow ones.

When the team built that detail into the model, the simulated planets grew magnetic fields that look like the real thing: tilted, lopsided and messy. When they left it out, the model produced a neat bar-magnet field like Earth's - which is not what Voyager 2 saw.

## The fields might not sit still

Then came the surprise. In the models, these fields do not stay put. They wander.

In one simulation that resembled Uranus, the tilt of the field swung by about **84 degrees** over 40 simulated years. Runs that resembled Neptune shifted by roughly **26 to 56 degrees** over the same span.

If that is happening for real, then the fields measured back in the 1980s may not match the fields today.

There is one small hint that something has already changed. Neptune's southern aurora - a glow made when charged particles from the Sun crash into the atmosphere - appears to have moved by around **30 degrees** in latitude between 1989 and 2023.

Be careful here, though. **This is a computer model, not a measurement.** Nobody has been back to check.

## How we could find out

The team says the way to test the idea is to put three things together: Voyager 2's old measurements, fresh aurora pictures from the James Webb Space Telescope, and the new simulations. Combined, they could estimate what the magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune look like today.

Planetary scientists have been asking for a new mission to Uranus for years - it would take about a decade just to fly there - but nothing has been approved yet. So for now the ice giants keep their secret, and scientists have a much better idea of where to look for it.

## Try this at home

You need a bar magnet and a compass (the kind hikers carry).

1. Put the magnet flat on a table. Walk the compass slowly around it in a circle and watch which way the needle points at each spot.
2. Now tilt the magnet so it leans over, and walk the same circle again. The needle swings to completely different directions.
3. That is the difference between Earth's field and Uranus's field - the same magnet, tipped over.

Ask a grown-up to help you draw both patterns on paper. The tidy one is Earth. The wobbly one is Uranus.

## Fun fact

Uranus itself is tipped over by about **97 degrees**, so it rolls around the Sun on its side like a rolling barrel. Its magnetic field is tipped over *again* on top of that - which makes "north" a very confusing idea on Uranus.
