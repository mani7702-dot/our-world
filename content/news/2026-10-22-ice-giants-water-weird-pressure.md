# 1. What happens to water when the pressure is 2 million times Earth's?

## The weird water hiding inside distant worlds

When you think of water, picture a sip from your bottle, or ice cubes in a freezer. But in the cold depths of space, water plays by different rules.

Neptune, the eighth planet from our Sun, is one of the Solar System's two "ice giants" — along with Uranus. Despite their name, they are not made of ordinary ice. Their insides are squeezed by pressures so extreme that water ice transforms into shapes impossible back home.

## Ice that forgets its shape

On Earth, ice cubes are a simple hexagonal crystal called "ice Ih." But under pressure, water ice changes — it can become ice VII or ice X, where each oxygen sits between two hydrogens. Scientists assumed the tighter you squeeze, the more orderly it becomes.

New experiments say otherwise.

Researchers at the University of Nevada used a diamond anvil cell — two diamonds pressing together — to squeeze water ice to over **2 million times Earth's atmospheric pressure** (222 gigapascals, the pressure found deep inside Uranus and Neptune). Instead of getting more orderly, the ice grew *less* symmetrical.

## Why it matters for strange worlds

This discovery helps explain a puzzle scientists have studied for decades: how do ice giants work inside?

NASA says Neptune is "dark, cold, and whipped by supersonic winds." Both planets have interiors where pressures reach 700 gigapascals — far beyond what the lab tested. Every new clue builds better models.

## Try this at home

Fill a plastic water bottle halfway, seal it tight, and squeeze. The water barely compresses — ordinary water on Earth is already in its densest form. Now imagine that squeeze, but millions and millions of times harder.

## Fun Fact

There are so many kinds of ice — over 15 confirmed phases on Earth alone — that scientists number them instead of naming them. Ice Ih is what you know from your freezer. Ice VII has been found in meteorite impacts. Ice XI only forms near absolute zero. Ice X, the most exotic, requires pressures so extreme that atoms share electrons in a perfectly symmetric dance — until the new research showed that dance can stumble.
