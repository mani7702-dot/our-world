# Why did 756 seabird pairs come back to a beach they had abandoned?

Imagine a beach where hundreds of birds used to lay their eggs. Then imagine it going quiet — not one nest, not one chick, for a whole summer.

That happened at Pagham Harbour in West Sussex, England. And this year the beach is loud again: 756 pairs of sandwich terns nested there and raised 570 chicks.

## What went wrong in 2022

Sandwich terns are seabirds the size of a big gull with a black cap and a thin, clever beak. They do something brave and risky: they lay their eggs right on the ground, in the open, in a crowd of other terns.

In 2022 a bird sickness called avian flu swept through that crowd. Sandwich terns, black-headed gulls and Mediterranean gulls were hit hard, and the whole colony gave up on Pagham Harbour. Zero pairs. For a bird that flies thousands of kilometres from West Africa to breed in Britain, losing one summer costs a lot.

## The fix was surprisingly ordinary

Nobody invented a medicine. Instead, a team from the RSPB and the Solent Seascape Project made the beach a place where a nervous ground-nesting bird could relax.

- They put up fencing that foxes and other hunters could not get past.
- They watched for rats, which eat eggs.
- They cut back plants so there was bare, open ground for scraping a nest.
- Rangers and volunteers walked the reserve, gently steering visitors — and dogs — away from nests, and made separate dog-exercise areas.
- They sorted those jobs out across a huge stretch of coast, from Brighton and Southampton out towards the Isle of Wight.

## It was not only the terns

Little terns, ringed plovers, black-headed gulls and oystercatchers all got the same helping hand, and their numbers rose as well. When you fix the ground and calm the traffic, more than one species benefits.

Elsewhere in England the same pattern is showing. The National Trust counted 1,164 pairs of sandwich terns back at Blakeney Point in Norfolk after two years with none at all — again after years of fencing, nest protection and seasonal dog rules.

## Try this at home: be a nest detective

Next time you are near a beach, look for a rope fence or a sign saying birds are nesting. Sit down a good distance away and watch quietly with binoculars or just your eyes.

Notice how the eggs are not in trees — they are on pebbles and sand, coloured to blend in. That is the whole trick of these birds: hide in plain sight, and hope nobody steps on you. Now you know why one wandering dog can empty a beach.
