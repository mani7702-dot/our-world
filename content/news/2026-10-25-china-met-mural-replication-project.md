## How Did a 700-Year-Old Chinese Mural End Up at the Met — and Why Did a Chinese Art Institute Spend 3 Years Copying It?

Imagine walking into a museum and seeing a painting so big it fills an entire wall — 50 feet long, covered in swirling colors and peaceful Buddha figures. Now imagine someone spent *three whole years* copying it, brushstroke by brushstroke, crack by crack. That is exactly what a team of Chinese artists and conservationists recently did with a very special painting at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

This is the story of a mural that traveled from a quiet temple in central China to one of the world's most famous museums — and back again, in the form of a perfect copy.

### From Guangsheng Temple to the Met

The mural, painted around 700 years ago during China's Yuan Dynasty (a time when the Mongols ruled China), shows the Medicine Buddha surrounded by his two helper spirits, Avalokitesvara and Cintamanicakra. The Buddha of Medicine is a very important figure in Mahayana Buddhism, believed to heal both the body and the spirit with his calming presence.

The mural originally hung in the Guangsheng Lower Monastery (also called Guangsheng Si) in Zhaocheng County, in central China. But how did it end up in New York? It was not stolen or taken by force — far from it. The poor monks who cared for the temple had to chop the mural into pieces and sell them. Why? The roof of their monastery was falling in, and they needed money to fix it. As Buddhist teaching reminds us, everything changes, and even precious art can become a tool to help others in need.

The mural traveled through several hands across the West before it was donated to the Metropolitan Museum of Art by Dr. Arthur M. Sackler in 1965. The Met's own records confirm the mural's journey from the Guangsheng Temple, through various collectors, to its permanent home in New York.

### The Great Copying Project

When Zhang Jian, director of the "Meticulous Painting Institute" at the Chinese National Academy of Arts, saw the mural at the Met, he was amazed by its "awe-inspiring energy." But when he learned that it had come from Guangsheng Temple and that the monastery now had only an empty wall where it once hung, he had an idea: what if his team could copy the entire mural by hand?

For three years, Zhang and his team of artists and conservationists visited the Met again and again. They studied every crack, every faded spot, and every small mistake made by conservators over the years. The team discovered that the Met's own staff had accidentally tilted the Buddha's head 2 centimeters off-center — a mistake so small you had to look closely, but so noticeable once you saw it!

To make the copy as real as possible, the team used the same ancient techniques as the original artists. They mixed clay with rice straw to make the painting surface, just like the monks did 700 years ago. They studied which mineral pigments the medieval painters would have had available, and how those colors had changed over centuries. Lighting, cameras, and printing all change how colors look, so they kept adjusting their paints until the copy matched the original perfectly.

### A Meeting of Cultures

What makes this project so special is how much it brought two cultures together. The Met actively helped Zhang's team — letting them use big lifts and bright lights in the museum's galleries for days at a time. Visitors understood when the equipment blocked their view, and the museum staff worked closely with the Chinese artists.

In October 2024, the Met and the China National Arts Fund (a government-backed preservation group) made a special agreement: the Met would share ultra-high-resolution photos and give the Chinese team full access to study the original mural up close.

This kind of teamwork is rare — a major American museum working side by side with Chinese researchers to understand and preserve art from their shared human heritage.

### The Copy That Gave the Original a New Life

When the copy was finished, it went on display at the China National Arts and Crafts Museum in Beijing in September 2025. Zhang said the project lets the ancient mural "re-enter contemporary cultural life" while still keeping the wisdom of the past alive.

The project also inspired a new international meeting: in October 2025, the Met and Chinese researchers co-hosted a big academic conference in Beijing about Chinese murals — the first time the New York museum had joined forces with a national research group in China for a major gathering.

The mural's journey from a temple roof in rural China to a copy in Beijing, with the original safe in New York, shows how art can cross oceans and centuries — and how kindness, curiosity, and teamwork help the best of human culture shine through.

### Try this at home

Want to try being an art detective? Visit your local museum or look up famous paintings online. Pick one artwork and study it carefully for 5 minutes. Notice the colors, the shapes, the small details you never saw before. You can also try mixing clay, sand, and flour to make your own "ancient" paint — just like the monks did with clay and rice straw!

### Fun Fact

The medicine Buddha mural is painted on **clay tiles mixed with rice straw** — an ancient Chinese technique. The rice gives the clay extra strength, kind of like how gluten helps bread dough hold together. The same recipe was used 700 years ago when the monks first painted the mural!
