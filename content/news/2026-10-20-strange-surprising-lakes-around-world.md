# 10 Surprising Lakes Around the World That Defy What You'd Expect

Most lakes sit still and calm, right? Fish swim, boats glide, and that's that. But what if we told you some lakes *breathe*, *migrate*, or even *disappear?

Here are five real lakes that sound like they belong in a fairy tale — but are very, very real.

## 1. Abraham Lake's Frozen Gas Bubbles

In Alberta, Canada, Abraham Lake is famous for its "frozen breath." When winter freezes the surface, millions of bubbles get trapped inside the ice like tiny glass beads.

The bubbles are pockets of **methane** — a gas made by bacteria eating dead plants at the lake bottom. During summer, the bubbles float up and pop into the air. But in winter, each bubble freezes where it stops, creating a layered tower of gas.

Visitors sometimes light the bubbles for spectacular flames — but the real danger isn't explosions; it's thin ice and sudden weather changes.

## 2. Palau's Jellyfish That Follow the Sun

On the island of Eil Malk in Palau, there's a lake full of **jellyfish** — about five million of them. The golden jellyfish (*Mastigias papua etpisoni*) are found nowhere else on Earth.

Every morning, the jellyfish swim east toward the sunrise. By midday, they turn around and swim west. It's called a daily migration — they follow the sun to get energy from tiny algae living inside their skin.

The jellyfish have very mild stinging cells, so visitors can gently swim among them. But the population is fragile: during the 2015–2016 El Niño drought, almost all the adults vanished. Tiny polyps survived and brought the jellyfish back a few years later.

## 3. The Drifting Forest of Williston Lake

In August 2026, a British Columbia man was boating on Williston Lake when he spotted something impossible: a **floating island of trees**.

The mass was about 100 km (62 miles) long and contained mature, healthy-looking trees — as if someone had cut out a whole patch of forest and set it adrift. Scientists checked satellite photos and confirmed: the floating forest was real.

The most likely explanation? Unusually high water levels lifted a mat of soil, roots, and woody debris from a sheltered bay. It's like nature's own boat — and nobody knows exactly what holds it together.

## 4. Lake Natron's Colorful Surprise

Lake Natron in northern Tanzania looks like it belongs on another planet. Its water can turn bright red or orange, and the temperature often climbs above 40°C (104°F).

The shocking colors come from salt-loving microorganisms — not from the lake turning living things to stone.

Famous photographs show birds that look petrified on the shore. But photographer Nick Brandt carefully posed already-dead, salt-crusted birds — they didn't turn to stone when they touched the water.

Despite the harsh conditions, Lake Natron is one of the world's most important breeding grounds for **lesser flamingos**. Salt islands in the lake give the birds safe nesting spots, protected from predators by the caustic water and mud around them.

## 5. Lake Kivu: The Lake That Powers a Country

Lake Kivu sits on the border of Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo. Deep below its surface, the lake holds enormous amounts of **methane gas**.

Unlike most lakes, Kivu's water doesn't mix from top to bottom. Salty groundwater creates dense layers that trap gas from underground volcanoes and decomposing plants. The pressure keeps the gas dissolved — until it doesn't.

Scientists worry about a "limnic eruption" — a sudden release of gas that could be deadly. But a 2026 study found a wide safety margin: the risk of a natural eruption should remain very low for the next **five centuries**.

Better yet: the methane is being safely tapped to generate electricity for nearby homes. What could have been a deadly lake is now a power plant.

## Try This at Home

**Make your own bubble lake:** Fill a clear container with water, add a pinch of baking soda, then drop in a vitamin C tablet. Watch the bubbles rise and freeze in place (using ice cubes) — just like Abraham Lake!

**Make a jellyfish migration:** Draw a jellyfish on a paper plate. Every few minutes, move it across your "lake" (a blue sheet) — following an imaginary sun across the sky.
