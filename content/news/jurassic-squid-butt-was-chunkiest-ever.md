Not all fossils are bones. Some are _butt-prints in stone_.

Paleontologists in Wyoming have found something that looks like a stubby cigar — but it is actually the chunkiest squid relative that ever lived. The creature, named **Wyoteuthis linsterorum**, swam the oceans about 160 million years ago, during the Jurassic Period, when the land that is now Wyoming was covered by a warm, shallow sea.

## 1. A fossil that stuck out

Most belemnites — the group Wyoteuthis belongs to — leave behind slender, bullet-shaped shells about as thick as a pencil. But these two fossils were thick as a thumb and just as wide as they were long. That is what tipped off paleontologist Bill Wahl, who has spent years combing Wyoming's rocks. He said the specimens "stuck out like a sore thumb" next to the countless ordinary belemnites around them, and he knew he was looking at something new.

## 2. The chunky gun

Wyoteuthis has an internal skeleton called a **rostrum**. It is the hard, pointed part that usually fossilizes, and scientists think it helped the animal swim upright — like a built-in counterweight. Where a normal belemnite rostrum is roughly 5–10 cm long and only 1–2 cm wide, Wyoteuthis grows to about 10 cm long and up to 6 cm wide. That makes it the thickest belemnite ever recorded by a long shot. With its soft body and arms included, the whole animal probably reached about 60 cm — roughly the length of a ruler and a half from head to tail.

## 3. Built by CT scans

The fossils were packed so tightly with hardened sediment that the team could not study them with the naked eye. Instead, they turned to **CT scanning** — the same kind of X-ray doctors use, but far more detailed. Sliding through the rock on a computer, the scientists saw the creature's hidden shape clearly enough to name it, describe it, and compare it to relatives from 200 years of prior research — all without chipping a single chip from the real fossil.

The discovery also rewrote a small piece of history. Wyoteuthis belongs to a family of belemnites that scientists had thought disappeared millions of years earlier, leaving a gap in the fossil record. Finding a creature from that "missing" branch means the timeline needs a little lengthening.

## Try this at home

Next time you walk on the beach or dig in a sandbox, look for tiny, perfectly smooth stones that feel oddly heavy. Belemnites are sometimes called "fossil bullets" — and if you find one, you are holding 160 million years of ocean in your palm.

## Fun Fact

Belemnites are so common in some rocks that kids are often the ones who find them first. The "squid butt" nickname is just field slang — no one calls them that in the science journals, but it sticks because it is exactly what the rostrum looks like when you hold it.
