Every amphibian you have ever met needs fresh water. Frogs, toads, salamanders, newts - their skin is thin and leaky on purpose, so they can breathe and drink through it. Put them in the sea and the salt comes straight in.

So here is the puzzle: scientists have now described an amphibian that appears to have lived **its entire life in the ocean**, 237 million years ago. Not visiting. Not paddling. Living there.

They named it ***Gerrothorax maritimus***. The second word means "of the sea".

## Two skeletons, far from any shore

The fossils come from Guizhou Province in southern China, from rock that formed on the floor of an ancient ocean during the Late Triassic period. Back then, that spot was open water - at least 30 kilometres from the nearest island, and by some measures hundreds of kilometres from any big landmass.

Two nearly complete skeletons were found there. Local farmers collected them back in 2004, and they are now kept at the Geological Museum of Guizhou in the city of Guiyang. An international team from the University of California, Davis, Peking University and the Guizhou museum studied them, and published the results in the journal *Nature Ecology & Evolution*.

Because the skeletons were so complete, the team could rebuild the animal. It was about **74 centimetres long** - nearly three feet - with a flat, disc-shaped body that was almost completely covered in **rigid bony plates**, like a living shield.

## Why this should be impossible

Out of more than **8,000 species of amphibian alive today, not one** lives fully in the sea. Fewer than 2 in 100 can even tolerate salt for a while. Mammals, reptiles and birds all have ocean-going members - whales, sea turtles, penguins - but amphibians have none.

The reason is that leaky skin. Water and salts pass through it in both directions, and amphibians use it to breathe and to soak up water. In seawater, that same trick works against them: they lose water and take in too much salt.

## The armour was the loophole

Here is the neat part. The team think the bony plates were not just for defence - they were the reason the animal could survive.

Armour covers skin. If most of your body is sealed in bone, you are no longer breathing and drinking through your skin, so salt cannot pour in that way. Instead, *Gerrothorax* would have relied on its **gills** and its **gut**, and those can be adjusted to cope with salt in a way that skin cannot.

The skull shape and the flat body suggest it was a **bottom-dweller that sucked up prey** - lying still on the seafloor, then opening its mouth fast to gulp in whatever swam too close. The same rock layers hold fossils of ichthyosaurs, big fish-shaped marine reptiles, so armour would have been useful for that too.

Two more clues back up the sea-dwelling idea. The skeletons were found complete and undisturbed, not smashed up and washed in from somewhere else. And when the researchers measured **strontium isotopes** in the bones - a chemical signature that records the water an animal grew up in - the values matched the seawater of that ancient ocean.

## What we still do not know

This was a **dead end**. *Gerrothorax maritimus* has no living descendants, and no other amphibian is known to have managed the same trick. So this is not the start of a long line of sea amphibians - it is one strange branch that tried the ocean and stopped there.

The team also say more specimens are needed before they can say much about how it fed, how fast it grew, or why this particular group - which already carried heavy armour - was the one that made it into salt water.

## Try this at home

- **Test your own skin.** Amphibian skin is permeable; yours is not. That is why you can swim in the sea and a frog cannot live there. Feel how different a wet frog's skin is from your own arm.
- **Make a salt-water test.** Ask a grown-up to help you put a thin slice of potato in fresh water and another in very salty water for an hour. Compare them. Water moves across thin membranes toward the saltier side - exactly the problem amphibians face.
- **Count the armour.** Draw *Gerrothorax* from above, then draw a modern frog from above. Which one would be harder to bite?
- **Think like a fossil hunter.** The skeletons were dug up by local farmers, not scientists. What would you do if you found bones in a rock?

**For grown-ups:** *Gerrothorax maritimus* is described as a fully marine stem amphibian (a temnospondyl, the same broad group as *Gerrothorax pulcherrimus* from the Late Triassic of Europe) on the basis of two nearly complete skeletons from Guizhou, China, dated to about 237 Ma. The marine interpretation rests on three independent lines of evidence: articulated, near-complete skeletons in open-marine facies at least 30 km from the nearest palaeo-island; strontium isotope values consistent with contemporaneous seawater; and functional anatomy - a flattened, dorsoventrally compressed body almost entirely enclosed in dermal armour, retained gills, and a skull consistent with benthic suction feeding. The authors' argument is that extensive armour would have blocked the cutaneous water, gas and salt exchange that constrains modern amphibians, shifting osmoregulation and respiration onto the gills and gut, which tolerate salinity far better - effectively pre-adapting an armoured lineage for marine life. Minimum body length is 73.7 cm. The lineage leaves no living descendants, so this is a single derived experiment rather than an adaptive radiation. As always with a two-specimen sample, ecology (diet, growth rate, population structure) remains inference rather than observation.
