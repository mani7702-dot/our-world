# What do 2,884 exploding stars tell us about how the Universe grows?

*Report filed at 04:13 IST from the Our World news desk.*

## The universe's brightest beacons

Picture the night sky scattered with glittering stars. Now imagine that some of those stars end their lives not with a whimper, but with a flash so bright it can outshine an entire galaxy of a hundred billion suns. These are called **Type Ia supernovae** — exploding white dwarf stars that briefly shine with a predictable, almost perfectly known brightness.

Because they all reach essentially the same peak brilliance, astronomers call them "standard candles." By comparing how bright a supernova really is with how dim it appears from Earth, scientists can calculate how far away it is — and therefore how fast the Universe is stretching at that distance. Over decades, teams have collected thousands of these cosmic mile-markers, each one a data point in our biggest cosmic-distance ladder.

## Stitching together 30 years of star data

An international team led by the University of Queensland decided to do something bold: they took **2,884** of these exploding stars and merged them into one giant, carefully calibrated collection. The data came from many different telescopes and surveys, gathered over more than thirty years. Before the stars could be compared, the scientists had to correct for tiny differences — cosmic dust that dims starlight, the masses of the galaxies that host each explosion, and even the subtle bending of light by gravity.

The result, called the **Unite** dataset, is the most complete picture yet of how the Universe's expansion has changed over time. When the team compared their measurements with other cosmic clues — such as the leftover glow from the Big Bang and maps of how galaxies are spread across space — something surprising emerged.

## Dark energy might not be so constant

For more than 20 years, scientists have believed that dark energy — the mysterious "push" driving the Universe's expansion faster — is a fixed, unchanging force. But the new data hints at something different. Like two independent witnesses telling slightly different but consistent versions of the same story, the Supernova dataset and a separate measurement called DESI both suggest that **dark energy may be weakening as the Universe ages**.

If dark energy changes over time, it means our cozy, well-tested model of the cosmos needs a small but important revision. It also means we are getting closer to solving one of physics' greatest puzzles: how to bring together Einstein's gravity with the quantum world. Sometimes the biggest discoveries come from simply collecting more data — one star, one explosion, one number at a time.

## Try this at home

On a clear night, look for the **Big Dipper** (Ursa Major). The two stars at the end of its bowl are called the **Pointers**. Follow them in a gentle arc across the sky, and you'll sweep past **Corona Borealis** (the Northern Crown) to the bright blue-white star **Vega** — one of the closest named stars to our solar system. Every photon that reaches your eyes from Vega began its journey over 25 years ago, traveling through a Universe that is still growing, still changing, and still full of surprises.

## Fun Fact

Type Ia supernovae are so reliably bright that they were used to discover that the Universe's expansion is *accelerating* — a finding so unexpected it earned the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics. The same kind of exploding star is now helping us refine that discovery, showing us that dark energy might be more complex than we thought.
