There is a crystal that behaves like two things at once. Electricity flows through it like water through a pipe. And yet, hidden inside it, are millions of microscopic spots that each act like a tiny magnet — one end plus, one end minus.

Scientists at Auburn University in Alabama just found a way to make those tiny spots all point the same way. Their tool? A flash of laser light, lasting less than a trillionth of a second.

## Tiny islands with a plus end and a minus end

The crystal is a modified form of a material called strontium titanate. Normally it is a clear, glassy-looking ceramic. Add a few other atoms into the mix, and something odd happens: the crystal can carry electric current, while also holding little patches where the electric charge is lopsided.

Physicists call these patches "polar nanoregions." Nanoregion means they are nanometres wide — tens of thousands of them could sit side by side across the width of a human hair.

Each patch has a direction, like a compass needle. At room temperature and above, the needles point every which way, so the crystal as a whole has no overall direction.

## The puzzle: do the islands exist first, or only after they line up?

For decades, physicists argued about how this kind of material settles into order as it cools. One idea said the atoms shift slightly, like a row of people leaning together. The other said the little islands are already there, pointing randomly, and simply swing round to face the same way when it gets cold enough.

The Auburn team settled it by watching. They built a very sensitive optical set-up — graduate student Mohamed Kandil spent nearly a year getting it to detect the faint signals — and tracked the islands' directions while heating and cooling the crystal again and again.

The islands were there the whole time. On cooling, they turned to face preferred directions. So the second idea was right: the order comes from islands swinging into line.

## Making order with light

Then came the surprising part. At warm temperatures, where the islands would normally stay messy, the researchers fired extremely short laser pulses at the crystal. The pulses pushed the islands into an aligned pattern — and a second optical measurement could read the pattern back.

It did not last. The order melted away in about one picosecond, a trillionth of a second. In that sliver of time, light itself travels only about a third of a millimetre.

Short-lived or not, the trick matters. It shows that light can write and read a pattern of electric order inside a material, without wires or metal contacts touching it.

## Why scientists care

Storing and moving information with light instead of wires is one of the big goals in electronics, because light pulses can be far faster than the signals that crawl through circuits.

To be clear about the size of this result: nobody is building a light-powered computer from it yet. It is one careful experiment on one family of crystals, published in *Physical Review Letters*, where editors picked it as a suggestion worth reading. But it answers a 60-year-old question about how these materials organise themselves — and it shows a new way to steer them.

**Try this at home:** drop a handful of small paper clips on a table. They land pointing every which way, like the islands in a warm crystal. Now bring a magnet close. The clips swing round and line up. You have just watched disorder turn into order — the same idea, at a size you can see.
