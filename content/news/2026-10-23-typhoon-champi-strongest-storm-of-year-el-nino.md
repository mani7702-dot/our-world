# How did Typhoon Champi become 2026's mightiest storm — and why is El Niño feeding it?

## 1. A storm that grew to be the strongest on Earth

In a year packed with powerful storms, one stood taller than all the rest: **Typhoon Champi**. Starting life as a small tropical disturbance near the islands of Micronesia on October 11, 2026, Champi gathered energy as it crossed the warm Pacific Ocean. By October 17, forecasters had upgraded it to a **Category 5 super typhoon** — the highest rating on the scale. That makes it the **strongest storm on Earth so far in 2026**, with wind gusts measured at an incredible **185 mph (298 km/h)**.

A Category 5 storm is like a giant engine that never stops running. Champi's core was so powerful that the air pressure in the center dropped to just **900 millibars** — about the same pressure inside a car tire after you've let some air out. The lower the pressure, the stronger the storm.

## 2. What made it spin so fast?

Typhoons are born over warm ocean water. The Pacific was running several degrees above normal this year, thanks to **El Niño** — a natural climate pattern that dumps extra heat into the sea every few years. That warm water acts like fuel. As warm, moist air rose from the ocean surface, it cooled and condensed, releasing heat that powers the storm's engine and spins the winds inward.

As Champi pulled in more warm air, its winds grew faster and faster — a bit like blowing across the top of a soda bottle makes the liquid inside spin faster. The Coriolis effect (Earth's spin) gave it that classic spiral shape, and the warm Pacific waters kept it churning as a Category 5 for several days. The record-breaking El Niño of 2023-2024 left extra warmth in the Pacific, which is partly why this storm became so fierce.

## Fun Fact

Typhoons in the western Pacific are called "typhoons," while the same kind of storm is a "hurricane" in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific. They are all the **same type of storm** — just named differently depending on which ocean it forms over! The name "Champi" comes from the international list for Pacific storms, and in the Philippines it had the local name "Rosal" (meaning "rose").

## Try this at home

Fill a bowl with warm water and add a drop of food coloring. Watch how the colored water spirals up — that is warm, moist air rising, just like a real typhoon! Now gently spin the bowl. The color coils into a tight center — that is your storm's calm **eye**. The faster you spin, the tighter the spiral, just like how warmer ocean water makes real storms spin faster. Ask a grown-up to help, and never leave hot water unattended!
