# Do spiders dream? What their sleep tells us about the evolution of rest

## A tiny hunter with surprising habits

Imagine a spider hanging upside down, perfectly still, eyes barely moving. To our human eyes, it looks like it's just resting. But what if we told you that spider might actually be going through something very similar to the sleep stages that humans experience?

Scientists at the University of Bonn in Germany decided to find out — not just whether spiders *can* sleep, but whether their sleep looks anything like ours. To study this, they chose a very special spider: *Portia fimbriata*, the Australian jumping spider.

## Meet Portia: the spider that hunts other spiders

*Portia fimbriata* is tiny — about the size of a one-euro coin. But despite its small size, it's one of the cleverest spiders on Earth. Unlike most spiders that wait in webs for dinner to fly by, Portia actively hunts *other spiders*. It sneaks up on them, sometimes even mimicking the vibrations of a struggling insect to lure prey into thinking dinner has arrived.

This clever hunting behavior made Portia the perfect test subject for studying spider sleep. If a spider can be this smart while awake, maybe its sleep is interesting too.

## Two stages of spider sleep

For decades, scientists knew that spiders can stay still for long periods — but they weren't sure if that meant the spiders were actually sleeping. True sleep isn't just inactivity; it's a regulated biological state with specific features.

The Bonn team watched Portia spiders over many days and nights, recording them in small plastic dishes (about the size of a coaster) using infrared cameras so they wouldn't disturb the spiders during their nighttime rest.

They found something amazing: the spiders go through **two clearly different sleep stages**:

1. **Deep rest** — the spider is still and relaxed, like a human in deep sleep.
2. **REM-like stage** — the spider's eyes move rapidly (just like human REM sleep) and its muscles twitch, similar to the twitches and jerks people make during dreams.

But here's the big question: does the REM-like stage mean spiders are *dreaming*?

## What the experiments revealed

The scientists didn't just watch the spiders and guess. They ran real experiments:

| Test | What it showed |
|---|---|
| **Sound puffs** | When spiders were gently puffed with air during their rest, they reacted much more slowly than when awake — proving they were genuinely asleep. |
| **Sleep deprivation** | After being kept active, the spiders compensated by sleeping longer the next time — just like humans "catch up" on sleep. |

These are the same hallmarks scientists look for when proving true sleep in any animal: reduced response to stimuli, and rebound after deprivation. The spiders passed both tests.

However, the researchers were careful to note: spotting eye movements and twitches doesn't *prove* spiders dream. Dreams require brain activity that we can't yet measure in spiders. What the team did prove is that even tiny-brained creatures can have complex, regulated sleep.

## What spider sleep tells us about evolution

Most of what we know about sleep comes from studying mammals and birds — animals with big, centralized brains. Finding REM-like sleep in spiders suggests that some aspects of sleep evolved **much earlier** in evolutionary history, or that they developed independently in creatures with very different nervous systems.

Either way, it tells us something exciting: sleep — and the different stages within it — may be one of the most ancient and widespread biological phenomena in the animal kingdom, from the tiniest spider to the biggest whale.

## Try this at home

Sit quietly in your room or backyard for five minutes without moving or making noise. Then, try blowing gently on a houseplant's leaves (like the spiders were tested with air puffs). Notice how the plant doesn't react. Now, think about how different spider sensing is from ours — they feel vibrations, not sound the way you do. That's why scientists used gentle air puffs instead of loud noises to test the spiders' alertness during sleep.

## Fun Fact

Spiders don't have eyelids — they can't close their eyes. But they can move their eyes inside their head (some can move them in all directions!). Some people think this ability to move their eyes while resting may be related to why we see similar eye movements in human REM sleep.
