# What Are 27 Alien Worlds Teaching Us About a Private Space Telescope?

## A telescope built for alien worlds

Sometime around **2029**, a new telescope will launch into space — but unlike NASA's famous Hubble or James Webb, this one belongs to a private team. It is called the **Lazuli Space Telescope**, and it has a very special job: to take pictures of distant worlds called **exoplanets** by catching the tiny amount of starlight they bounce back into space.

Most telescopes today spot exoplanets by watching for the dimming of their star when a planet passes in front of it — like a mini-eclipse. But Lazuli's instrument, the **ExtraSolar Coronagraph (ESC)**, will try something trickier: it will block the blinding glare of the star itself and photograph the planet's faint reflected glow directly.

## Why 27 worlds?

A new research paper has named the **27 best targets** for Lazuli's first missions. Every one of them is a **gas giant** — a Jupiter-like planet — orbiting a star that is well-mapped and well-understood. Picking known planets is important because Lazuli's camera will need very long, careful observations (think tens to hundreds of hours), and astronomers want to maximize their chances of success.

The key advantage is **reflected starlight**: older planets have cooled down and glowing dimly from their own heat, making them hard to see in infrared. But by watching the visible light they reflect, Lazuli can study planets of **any age** — opening a window into worlds that are billions of years old.

## How it compares to other telescopes

Lazuli's 3-meter mirror is **larger than Hubble's** 2.4-meter eye, giving it sharper vision. Its ESC will work at visible wavelengths (350–2000 nanometers), complementing NASA's **Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope** and the **Extremely Large Telescope (ELT)** on the ground, which observe at slightly different wavelengths. The researchers say the best results will come from **combining observations** across all these facilities.

Some of the 27 targets are famous planets like **Beta Pictoris b** — one of the first exoplanets ever directly imaged — while others are newer discoveries waiting to be studied in detail.

## Try this at home

Stand in a dark room holding a tiny flashlight (your planet) next to a bright lamp (your star). Shine the lamp on a wall — the lamp dominates. Now cover the lamp with your hand, leaving a tiny gap. If you look at just that gap through a cardboard tube, you might just make out the small flashlight's glow. That gap is like a coronagraph, and your flashlight is the exoplanet shining by reflected light.

## Fun Fact

The name "Lazuli" comes from **lapis lazuli** — the deep blue semi-precious stone that was once worth more than gold. The telescope was created by the **Eric and Wendy Schmidt Observatory System**, named after Google's former CEO and his wife, making it one of the first major space telescopes funded entirely by private philanthropy rather than a government space agency.

**for-grownups:** The Lazuli Space Telescope (3-meter aperture) is developed by Schmidt Sciences under the Eric and Wendy Schmidt Observatory System. Its ExtraSolar Coronagraph (ESC) will perform reflected-light imaging of exoplanets at visible wavelengths (∼0.5–2 μm), reaching contrasts of 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁹. The first target catalog of 27 giant exoplanets, selected by Pearce & Douglas (arXiv:2610.31286, submitted Oct 2026), prioritizes well-characterized orbits to maximize campaign efficiency given long exposure times. The telescope design, instruments, and science capabilities are detailed in Roy et al. (arXiv:2601.02556). Targets include Beta Pictoris b and others that overlap with the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope's coronagraph and ELT's METIS thermal imager for synergistic multi-wavelength characterization. Expected launch: ~2029.
