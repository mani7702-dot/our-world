# Did You Know? Ghana Has a New Rule About Preaching on Buses?

In the bustling city of Accra, Ghana's capital, mornings begin with a special kind of sound: drums, singing, and the voice of a pastor sharing a message of hope on long-distance buses. But a new law might soon change that!

### 🎵 Morning Songs on the Move

Ghana is a country where **71% of people are Christian** (that's about as religious as a country can get!). On big buses that travel between cities, you'll often find pastors — like **Boateng Bismark James**, a 61-year-old preacher from Gospel Light Church — standing in the aisle or stairwell, reading from the Bible and singing songs of praise.

Why do they do it? Boateng explained: *"Not everyone can go to church on Sunday. Some passengers need our message of salvation to repent from their sins. We even heal the sick on some buses!"*

Some passengers love it. Others... not so much!

### ⚖️ The New Rule: No Preaching While Moving

In **May 2026**, Ghana's parliament passed a new rule: anyone caught preaching on a **moving** public transport vehicle could face up to **8 months in jail**, a fine of up to $52, or both! The government says it's to keep passengers safe — loud preaching might distract drivers and create safety issues.

Critics agree: *"Some of them spit saliva on you unconsciously... they are a nuisance in terms of noise,"* said one passenger who preferred to stay anonymous.

### 💬 But Pastors Say It's Their Job

For many pastors, preaching on buses isn't just about faith — it's about **making a living**. Boateng said half the money he uses to send his children to school comes from the small donations passengers give during these bus sermons.

*"We are very worried,"* Boateng said. *"The government should allow us to preach on the buses and alight when it is exiting Accra."*

Other passengers think the preaching is actually helpful: *"The message of the pastor can give the person renewed hope,"* said one traveler named Bernice Nartey.

### So What Happens Now?

Ghana's constitution **guarantees freedom of religion** — but it also needs to keep roads safe. The new law says preaching is still allowed... just not while the bus is rolling. Pastors can still share their message at bus stops or when vehicles are parked.

It's a delicate balance between **religious freedom** and **public safety** — a challenge many countries face as they grow and change.

### Try This at Home

Next time you ride a bus or train, try this: close your eyes and listen to all the different sounds around you. Can you hear people talking, laughing, music from phones? In some countries, you might even hear a bus preacher singing or praying! Now imagine a law that said you couldn't do that anymore. How would you feel?

### For Grown-ups

Ghana, officially the Republic of Ghana, is a country in West Africa with a population of over 35 million. According to the 2021 Ghana census, **71.3% of Ghanaians are Christian**, 19.9% are Muslim, and the rest follow traditional faiths. Ghana is a secular state with a constitution that guarantees freedom of religion and worship. The new public transport evangelism regulation, passed by parliament in May 2026, prohibits preaching and "hawking" on moving public service vehicles, with penalties of up to 8 months imprisonment and a fine of up to 500 Ghanaian cedis ($52). The law aims to prevent driver distraction and improve passenger safety and comfort. Supporters of religious freedom argue that preaching on public transport is a vital outreach method, especially for pastors who depend on voluntary donations to support their families. Critics say it disrupts passengers and creates hygiene and noise concerns. The regulation allows preaching when vehicles are stationary but not while in motion. Wikipedia confirms Ghana ranks among the world's most religious countries and that the constitution protects both freedom of worship and public order.
