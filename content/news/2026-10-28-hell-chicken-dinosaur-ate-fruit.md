## A dinosaur the size of a turkey, with no teeth at all

Imagine a dinosaur about as big as the turkey on a holiday table. About 12 kilograms. Feathers, most likely. A beak instead of a mouth full of fangs.

Now imagine it with absolutely no teeth. Not one.

That animal was real. It lived in North America around 68 to 66 million years ago, in the same place and at the same time as Tyrannosaurus rex and Triceratops. Which is exactly why its fossils were found in a rock layer called the Hell Creek Formation. The name of the rock came first. The nickname came second.

Say hello to Infernogallus jamesi. In Latin, infernus means hell and gallus means rooster. Scientists have been calling it the hell chicken.

## The skull that finally showed up

Palaeontologists have known about this family of dinosaurs, the caenagnathids, for a long time, mostly from bits of leg and hip and jaw. What they never had was a decent skull.

That is not bad luck. It is the shape of the bone. A skull is thin-boned and full of holes, and 66 million years of being squashed by rock is not kind to thin-boned, holey things. So for decades nobody could say much about what these animals ate, because the answer to this question usually is written on the skull.

Then a team led by Gregory Funston at Stony Brook University got hold of a specimen with a nearly complete skull, still articulated, meaning the bones had stayed in their proper places instead of scattering. It had been somewhat crushed, so the team did what modern palaeontology does: they put it through a CT scanner, the same kind of machine a hospital uses to look inside a person, and rebuilt it in three dimensions on a computer.

What came out was strange.

## A mouth that chewed sideways

Almost every predatory dinosaur bit straight down. Open, close, crush. Up and down. That is the T. rex approach, and it is a good one if your dinner is a bone.

The hell chicken was built differently. In its skull, the bones of the palate, the roof of the mouth, arch downward into the mouth itself. That small change of shape moves a set of jaw muscles, so instead of pulling the jaw up and down they pull it backward and forward. The lower jaw slid.

On top of that, the mouth was lined with ridges and grooves that fit against each other, like the two halves of a mortar and pestle. Push soft food between them, slide, and the food turns to pulp.

If that sounds familiar, it is because you have watched a parrot do it. Or a person. Your own back teeth grind sideways, not just straight down.

The team found a living animal that matches almost exactly: Pesquet's parrot, a fruit specialist from New Guinea. It mashes the soft flesh of fruit, works it loose, and lets the hard seed drop.

## So what was on the menu?

Fruit. Specifically, the paper suggests, fruit with a stone in the middle, the kind botanists call a drupe. Cherries and peaches are drupes: soft flesh around one hard stone.

Three clues point that way, and none of them is a fossilised lunch.

- The sideways chewing is only useful on something soft that still needs work. Tough stems do not reward mashing. Fruit pulp does.
- The bite was weak. There were no stomach stones in the body cavity either, the little swallowed rocks that many plant-eating dinosaurs used as internal grinding mills. No stones, weak bite, mashing jaws: soft food, chewed carefully in the mouth.
- The timing. Flowering plants were spreading fast in the Cretaceous, and their fruits were getting bigger and fleshier. Here was a small dinosaur, living among them, with a mouth shaped for exactly that.

## The part where scientists disagree

Here is where a careful reader should slow down. The word in the paper is hypothesise, not prove.

In the study's supplementary analysis, the hell chicken scores about 40.6 percent as a soft-plant eater and about 40.3 percent as an omnivore. Those two numbers are practically the same number. A different researcher, speaking to CBC Radio about the find, called the fruit case circumstantial, and said the paper makes a strong argument that this dinosaur ate fruit without fully ruling out other soft plant food: shoots, buds, tender stems.

The only thing that would settle it is direct evidence. A specimen that died right after eating, was buried fast, and fossilised with its stomach contents still inside. That has happened for a handful of dinosaurs. It has not happened for this one.

There is a second open question too, and a bigger one. If these dinosaurs were eating fruit, were they spitting out the seeds, or swallowing them and carrying them somewhere new? Carrying seeds around is one of the ways animals help forests spread. The authors raise it. They do not answer it.

## What it means for a turkey-sized dinosaur

For most of the age of dinosaurs, the big plant-eaters were built like industrial shredders. Teeth for slicing, jaws for shearing, huge bellies for fermenting the results.

The hell chicken went the other direction. It shrank. It lost its teeth entirely. It developed a beak, a sliding jaw, and a grinding palate fine-tuned for one of the softest foods a plant makes.

That is not a weird footnote. It is a small dinosaur finding its own way to eat, at a moment when the plants around it were making food that had not been there before.

## Try this at home

You can feel the difference between the two chewing machines with a bread crust and a chewy gummy sweet.

1. Take a piece of **bread crust** and a **gummy sweet** of similar size. Ask a grown-up before you start.
2. Try to break the crust with a **straight-down bite** of your front teeth only. Notice how much force it takes, and how it cracks instead of mushing.
3. Now put the gummy sweet between your back teeth and **slide your jaw sideways** a few times. Notice how fast it flattens, and how little force it took.

Front teeth cut. Back teeth mash. The hell chicken had a jaw built for the mashing job, which tells you its food was soft enough to mash, and soft enough to be worth mashing.

Then try the harder question. An animal has a weak bite, no stomach stones, and a mouth built to pulp soft things. What could it eat besides fruit? Write down three answers. You are now doing exactly what the palaeontologists are still doing.
