# How did an ancient sea creature navigate using a magnetic GPS 97 million years ago?

## A compass hidden in stone

Imagine having a tiny built-in compass inside your body that lets you find your way across thousands of kilometers — even underwater, in the dark. Scientists have now found what may be the **oldest evidence** of such a system, hidden inside fossils from a time when giant dinosaurs roamed the Earth.

The clue comes from **microscopic magnetic fossils** preserved in ancient seafloor mud from the **North Atlantic Ocean**. These tiny structures — shaped like spearheads, spindles, and bullets, each smaller than a single bacterium — are about **97 million years old**, dating back to the time of the Cretaceous period. What makes them remarkable is that they look like they were made by a living creature on purpose.

## The world's oldest animal GPS

Researchers from the **University of Cambridge** and **Helmholtz Zentrum Berlin** used powerful 3D imaging to examine these magnetic fossils. They discovered something extraordinary: the structures were shaped and arranged in a way that was perfectly optimized for detecting the Earth's magnetic field.

"Whatever creature made these magnetofossils, we now know it was most likely capable of accurate navigation," said Professor Rich Harrison from Cambridge's Department of Earth Sciences, who co-led the study.

The fossils are roughly **10 to 20 times larger** than the magnetic particles that modern bacteria use to orient themselves. That's a huge size difference — and computer simulations showed that these bigger structures could detect both **which direction** the magnetic field pointed and **how strong** it was. Those two pieces of information together are like having both a compass and a map — a true built-in GPS.

## Why this matters

Today, animals like **sea turtles, migratory birds, and even some fish** still use Earth's magnetic field to navigate across entire oceans. But scientists don't fully understand how this sense — called **magnetoreception** — evolved. These 97-million-year-old fossils may represent an early chapter in that story.

Some modern bacteria use chains of tiny magnetite crystals to line themselves up with the planet's magnetic field. But the ancient creature that made these fossils took things much further — creating a sophisticated magnetic sensor that could act like a living map.

Understanding how this system first evolved could help scientists learn more about the remarkable migration abilities of animals today, from sea turtles crossing entire oceans to birds flying across continents twice a year.

## Try this at home

Find a magnet (a fridge magnet works!) and a pile of steel wool fibers on a smooth table. Let the magnet slowly approach the wool — the tiny iron strands will line up with the magnetic field lines and create visible patterns. That's a simple version of what the magnetic fossils inside that ancient creature may have done, but in 3D and inside its body!

## Fun Fact

Magnetite — the magnetic mineral in these fossils — is the **most magnetic mineral on Earth** and is also found in lodestone, the stone that gives its name to "magnetic." A single magnetite crystal is so perfectly formed that it was one of the first minerals to be studied using **X-ray crystallography** — a technique that helped scientists discover the structure of DNA!
