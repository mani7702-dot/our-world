# How did an octopus cheat death — and star in Blue Planet III?

## A hunt that almost caught its star

Deep down where sunlight turns silver, a predator crept forward on silent fins. Its target was an octopus that had wandered away from the rockpile it calls home — a little too far, into open water where the hunter's silhouette looked enormous. For one heart-stopping moment the octopus was trapped: the predator snapped, jaws wide, water exploding from the strike. Then — a blur — and the octopus was gone, leaving only a puff of ink where it had been.

That split-second escape was caught on camera by the team behind **Blue Planet III**, the BBC's newest celebration of ocean life, and it is the kind of moment that makes you rethink everything you think a "simple" sea creature can do.

## The octopus superpower no engineer could copy

What let that octopus survive is the same kit that lets them open jars, solve mazes and impersonate flounder: a body with **no bones and no skeleton at all**. While a shark or seal is built like a machine with hinges and joints, an octopus is built like a very smart blob of muscle and skin — and that means it can squeeze through any gap bigger than the small, hard beak tucked in the middle of its head.

Add eight arms, each lined with suckers that can grip rock or feel a passing current, plus a brain so distributed that a severed arm can still smell its way to food, and you have an animal built for slipping out of trouble. When camouflage fails, they fire a cloud of ink and jet backward in a rush — a getaway in reverse.

## Why it matters (and why Attenborough still leans in close)

These escapes are not rare tricks, but they are rare to *see*. Octopuses live hidden, and most of their drama unfolds in three dimensions among rocks and kelp where cameras rarely fit. Catching one cheat death on film takes patience and a lens that does not disturb the scene — exactly the kind of work that has made the BBC Natural History Unit's Blue Planet series legendary since the year 2000.

Sir David Attenborough, now in his second century, returns to narrate Blue Planet III. The new series premieres Sunday 1st November at 8pm on BBC One and iPlayer, taking viewers from icy polar seas to the coral tropics and out into the open ocean where encounters like the octopus escape still wait in the dark.

## Fun Fact

An octopus has **more neurons than a mouse**, and roughly **two-thirds of them live in its arms**. That is why an arm can keep tasting and moving even after it has been dropped — the arm is thinking for itself.

## Try this at home

**Build an octopus escape tube.** Take a large cardboard box, cut a small hole in one side (just big enough for a child's hand), and drape a dark cloth over the top so the box is dim inside. Hide a small toy or snack at the bottom. The challenge is to retrieve the prize using only the hole — no reaching around, no lifting the cloth. It is surprisingly hard, and it is a tiny taste of how an octopus must navigate a world of tight spaces while staying out of sight.
