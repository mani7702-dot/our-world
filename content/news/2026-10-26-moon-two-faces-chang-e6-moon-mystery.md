## 1. Why does the Moon play favorites?

![The Moon's near side (dark) vs far side (bright) — two faces of the same world. Credit: Wikimedia Commons](../assets/images/2026-10-26-moon-two-faces-chang-e6-moon-mystery.jpg)

When you look up at the night sky, the Moon shows you only **one face** — the one that always turns toward Earth. But that face looks very different from the side that perpetually stares into space.

The **near side** (the one we see) is dotted with dark, smooth patches that look like spilled ink on a paper plate. The **far side** — the "dark side" that never faces us — is almost entirely bright and cratered, like a battered golf ball.

For decades, scientists have wondered: *why does our closest celestial neighbor look like two different worlds?* Now, fresh clues from a mission that landed on the hidden side of the Moon may finally explain the answer.

## 2. A rock haul from the forbidden side

Most Moon rocks on Earth were collected by NASA's Apollo missions — but every single one came from the **near side**. That changed in 2024, when China's **Chang'e-6** spacecraft became the first mission ever to bring back samples from the Moon's **far side**, landing in the vast South Pole-Aitken Basin.

A team led by Professor Hejiu Hui of Nanjing University studied those precious far-side rocks and compared them to samples from humanity's earlier visits. They examined three kinds of lunar rock — **anorthosites**, **Mg-suite rocks**, and **mare basalts** — to piece together how the Moon's two faces grew so different.

## 3. Earth's invisible hand

Here is what they found: deep beneath the surface, the Moon's **mantle** is surprisingly similar on both sides — but the **crust** tells a very different story.

The far side's crust contains more of a magnesium-rich mineral and less of the potassium-and-rare-earth elements that pile up on the near side. That mismatch fits a bold idea: once the Moon became **tidally locked** (always showing us the same face), Earth's gravity started to play a role.

When the Moon was young and still mostly molten, Earth's gravitational pull heated the near side more than the far side. That temperature difference set off a giant slow-motion churn — **hot magma flowed from the near side toward the far side**, hauling magnesium-rich material along with it. Meanwhile, the last, leftover molten bits rich in potassium and rare elements drifted back toward the near side.

Over time, this invisible conveyor belt built up a **thick, bright crust on the far side** and left the **near side darker** with its smooth plains. Think of it like a cosmic game of "hotter gets lighter" — the side closer to Earth stayed a bit hotter and lighter, while the far side cooled into something heavier and brighter.

## Try this at home

Fill a large baking dish with two colors of gelatin (one dark, one light). Once it starts to set, tilt the dish slightly and blow gently across the surface with a straw. Watch how the "warm" top layer (the air from your breath) flows and swirls, while the "cool" bottom stays calmer. That's a tiny taste of the kind of slow churn that shaped the Moon's two faces.

## Fun Fact

The Moon is still **shrinking**! As it slowly loses heat from the inside, the whole planet gets a little bit smaller — about a hair's width per year — which is why astronauts have spotted thousands of tiny cliffs called "lobate scarps" across the Moon's face.
