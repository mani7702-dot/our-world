# How the Universe's Biggest Supercomputer Is Revealing What Happened After the Big Bang

## The foggy universe that needed brightening

About **13.8 billion years ago**, our universe was a fog. It was not empty — it was filled with a thin soup of **neutral hydrogen atoms** that scattered light in every direction, making space opaque. This era is called the **Dark Ages**, lasting from just after the Big Bang until somewhere between **150 million and 1 billion years** later.

Then, something changed. The **first stars and galaxies** formed, showering space with intense ultraviolet light. That radiation **ionized** — split apart — the neutral hydrogen atoms. When most of that hydrogen was ionized, the universe went from foggy to clear. Scientists call this pivotal moment the **Epoch of Reionization**, and it is often described as the universe's last great phase transition.

## The new digital time machine

But here is the puzzle: the details of how it happened are still mysterious. Did **hydrogen** and **helium** get ionized all at once? Or in patches, over time? And which sources — young stars, exploding stars, or feeding black holes — did the most work?

A new study offers a fresh tool to answer these questions. Astronomers created **Lumina**, one of the **largest and most detailed** cosmological simulations ever made, named after the Latin word for "light." It runs on more than **15,000 graphics processing units** on the Frontier supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory — one of the world's fastest.

## What Lumina reveals

Lumina simulates a cube of space about **1.5 billion light-years** on each side, large enough to mirror roughly what our best telescopes can see. That volume is about **150 times bigger** than the previous record-holder, letting scientists study rare objects like bright quasars and measure true cosmic statistics.

The simulation follows two linked processes:

1. **Hydrogen reionization** — driven mainly by ultraviolet light from **young stars** in early galaxies.
2. **Helium reionization** — which came later, powered by the ferocious radiation of **quasars and supermassive black holes** during an era astronomers call "Cosmic Noon" when star birth and black-hole feasting peaked.

Lead author **Oliver Zier** of the Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian explained the goal: "With JWST, the early universe has become an exciting new frontier. To understand what we are seeing, we need to translate our theories into detailed predictions."

## Why bigger is better

Cosmologists have been building digital universes for decades, but hydrogen-only models could not tell the full story. Lumina is one of the first to model **both** hydrogen and helium reionization at once.

Helium makes up about **25%** of all matter in the universe, yet its reionization is harder to simulate because it demands extra computing power — it must evolve nearly an extra billion years after hydrogen reionization ends. Lumina handles this by tracking how galaxies and active black holes glow across a cosmic volume 10 times larger than previous efforts combined.

The first results match real observations: helium reionization begins at redshift z ≈ 6 and reaches its midpoint around z ≈ 4, leaving less than 1% of helium neutral by today. This agreement gives scientists confidence that Lumina can now help interpret what the James Webb and Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescopes are actually seeing.

## Try this at home

Draw a large rectangle on black paper — that is your early universe. Dab yellow dots at the bottom (young stars) and sprinkle glittering silver dots across it (quasars and black holes). Shine a flashlight from one corner: the yellow light spreads slowly, ionizing a path. Now tilt a mirror to angle the beam — that is helium reionization, arriving later and sweeping differently. Together they transform a dark canvas into one where light can travel freely.

## Fun Fact

The Epoch of Reionization is sometimes called the **universe's "cosmic noon"** counterpart — not the noon, but its dramatic book-end. After it ended, space became transparent for the first time since the Big Bang, letting light travel freely across the cosmos just as it does today.
