# 1. Why Is the Space Station Getting a Power Upgrade?

Imagine your phone's battery slowly losing its ability to hold a charge — it works fine in the morning, but by evening you're scrambling for a charger. The International Space Station (ISS) has the same problem, but on a grander scale. Its original solar wings, which have been orbiting Earth for over two decades, are naturally wearing out from constant sunlight, radiation, and the extreme temperature swings of space.

That's why NASA and its international partners are sending astronauts spacewalking outside the station to install new solar arrays. These aren't just any solar panels — they're called **iROSAs** (ISS Roll-Out Solar Arrays), lightweight and flexible power generators designed to clip right onto the old arrays and give the station a fresh burst of energy.

# 2. Canada's Col. Kutryk Takes His First Spacewalk

On Thursday, October 29, 2026, Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk will step outside the ISS for the first time. A colonel in the Royal Canadian Air Force, Kutryk will be joined by NASA astronaut Anil Menon, a colonel in the U.S. Space Force who has already completed three spacewalks.

The pair will venture out at 8 a.m. EDT and spend about **6 hours and 45 minutes** working in the vacuum of space. Their mission: to deploy the **seventh and eighth** iROSA solar arrays. Canada's robotic arm — **Canadarm2** (officially the Mobile Servicing System) — will play a crucial supporting role, carefully positioning the astronauts and the new solar arrays for installation.

This spacewalk is special not just for Kutryk, but for Canada as a whole. It marks the **first spacewalk by a Canadian astronaut in over seven years**, showcasing the continued partnership between NASA and the Canadian Space Agency on the ISS.

# 3. From 160,000 Watts to 215,000 — A 30% Boost

Once all eight iROSAs are installed (the first one went up in 2021), the ISS will see its power supply rise from about **160 kilowatts** to roughly **215 kilowatts** — a boost of nearly **30%**. That extra power means more energy for experiments, life support, and daily operations for the crew of seven astronauts and cosmonauts who call the station home.

The two spacewalks are planned as a pair, with the second expected no sooner than November 5. Both will be guided from inside the station by veteran capcom Jenni Gibbons — another Canadian astronaut who previously supported NASA's Artemis II mission around the moon.

**Try this at home:** You can demonstrate how solar panels work with a small solar-powered calculator or garden light. Place it in bright sunlight and watch it spring to life instantly. Then slowly move it into shadow — notice how the power fades? In space, the sun never sets (from the ISS's perspective it orbits every 90 minutes!), so solar arrays there are constantly bathed in light. The challenge is managing that energy, not storing it for nighttime.

**Fun Fact:** The iROSA panels were developed by Redwire Space and use a unique "roll-out" design — they're folded up like a tiny scroll and unfurled once in space, making them much lighter than traditional rigid solar panels.

**for-grownups:** During EVA-76, CSA astronaut Joshua Kutryk and NASA astronaut Anil Menon will conduct the ISS's 76th spacewalk since assembly began. They will install the 7th and 8th ISS Roll-Out Solar Arrays (iROSAs), lightweight flexible solar panels developed by Redwire that increase ISS power generation from 160 kW to ~215 kW (~30% boost). Canadarm2 (Mobile Servicing System) provides robotic support, with Luke Delaney and Jessica Watkins operating the arm from inside the station. The capcom is CSA astronaut Jenni Gibbons. The spacewalk begins at 8:00 a.m. EDT (1200 GMT) and lasts approximately 6 hours 45 minutes. The ISS orbits Earth at ~250 miles altitude, completing one orbit every ~90 minutes. Each iROSA uses flexible solar cells on a composite blanket that rolls out from a compact cylinder, reducing mass compared to traditional rigid-panel arrays.
