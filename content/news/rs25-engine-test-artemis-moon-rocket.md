# Why did NASA just fire a rocket engine for 500 seconds?

## A test as long as a real launch

Picture a rocket engine firing at full power — but instead of lifting off and flying to space, it stays rooted to a test stand, burning fuel for as long as a real launch lasts. That is exactly what **NASA** did on **October 21, 2026**.

Down at the **Stennis Space Center** in Mississippi, engineers fired the **RS-25 engine** — the same kind of engine that powers NASA's big new rocket — for a full **eight and a half minutes**. That is **500 seconds**: the exact amount of time the real rocket needs to push through the atmosphere and into orbit. If the test had been a real launch, passengers would already be weightless by now.

## Inside the RS-25 — the engine that lifts the big rocket

The RS-25 is no ordinary engine. It sucks in two of the coldest liquids in the solar system — **liquid hydrogen** (about minus 253 degrees Celsius) and **liquid oxygen** — and burns them together at incredible pressure. Four of these engines sit beneath NASA's **Space Launch System**, or **SLS** rocket, and together they produce a combined **2 million pounds of thrust**. That is enough to lift a **747 plus an entire house** off the ground.

During the test, the engine was pushed to **111% power** — the same level the SLS needs to escape Earth's gravity and send the **Orion spacecraft** sailing toward the Moon.

## What the test was actually for

This particular engine, numbered **10001**, will never fly to space. It is a **developmental test engine** — a practice model built to gather data. The point was to:

- Prove that new **flight components** can survive real launch conditions
- Test a **new thermal protection system** that shields the engine's nozzle from the searing heat of launch (the nozzle sees temperatures up to **3,300 degrees Celsius**)
- Help engineers fine-tune the next generation of RS-25 engines, called the **RS-25E**

## How the engine throttles — like a car's gas pedal

Unlike the old Space Shuttle engines, the modern RS-25 can be **throttled**, just like a car's accelerator. During launch, it can run as low as **67% power** or as high as **109%** (and up to 111% in testing) — adjusted in one-percent increments. That lets mission controllers fine-tune the rocket's climb, easing the stress on both the vehicle and the people inside.

## From the Shuttle to the Moon

The RS-25 is actually a veteran. It first flew on NASA's **Space Shuttle** program from **1981 to 2011**, carrying satellites, space stations, and even the Hubble Space Telescope into orbit. Now it is being rebuilt for a new job: landing **astronauts back on the Moon** as part of the **Artemis** program.

Four RS-25 engines plus two solid rocket boosters fire underneath the SLS — the most powerful rocket ever built — lifting the Orion capsule, its crew, and cargo toward our closest celestial neighbor.

### Try this at home

You can do a mini version of an engine throttle test with a hair dryer and a cardboard tube. Point the dryer straight down into the tube and hold a ping-pong ball above the opening — the airflow pushes it up and suspends it in midair. Tilt the tube and the ball stays floating (thanks to the Coandă effect — the air follows the curve). Now try blowing softer or harder: the ball rises higher or lower. That is exactly how NASA throttles the RS-25 — by adjusting the "breath" pushing the rocket upward.

**Fun fact:** The RS-25 engines on the Space Shuttle flew back to Earth, landed on a runway, and got reused. The RS-25 engines on the new SLS rocket fly only once — they fall off and burn up in the atmosphere, because the SLS is designed for one-way trips to the Moon and beyond.
