# How did octopuses become so smart? Their DNA folded into a brand-new shape

## A brain built from folding

Octopuses can solve puzzles, open jars, and change their skin color in the blink of an eye. They are the geniuses of the ocean — and they are also aliens, in a way. An octopus's nervous system evolved completely separately from ours, yet both lineages arrived at something remarkably clever.

But how? After all, an octopus's brain is built from totally different parts than a human's. The answer, scientists now think, may not be in the genes themselves — but in how those genes are **folded**.

## The genome's 3D dance

DNA is like a shoelace that never stops knotting and unknotting. In every cell, the long genetic strand folds and crumples so it fits inside the nucleus. Those folds matter: when two distant genes get physically tucked next to each other, they can switch each other on or off. For years, scientists assumed evolution worked mainly by changing DNA *letters*. But a new study says that was only half the story.

Researchers at the University of Vienna compared the 3D architecture of genomes in octopuses, squid, and cuttlefish — the coleoid cephalopods. They found that hundreds of millions of years ago, a massive reshuffling brought far-apart genes suddenly together, like rearranging a city's subway map so new neighborhoods connect. These new connections appear to have rewired how the nervous system develops, helping birth the unusually complex brains that let octopuses do everything from solve mazes to mimic flounder.

**The big idea:** intelligence can evolve when DNA literally folds into a new shape.

## Why octopus smarts are so special

Most animals inherited their brain-building toolkit from a common ancestor. But octopuses, squid, and cuttlefish evolved their sophisticated nervous systems entirely on their own — a second, independent origin of complex brains anywhere in the animal kingdom. Their neurons work differently too: two-thirds of an octopus's nerves live in its arms, so each limb can taste, touch, and think a little on its own.

## Try this at home

Cut a long strip of paper — that's a strand of DNA. Now fold it in a Z-shape and tape two distant spots together. Those two spots can now "talk" to each other, just like genes that only connect when the genome folds. Try a different fold and see how the connections change. You've just simulated how evolution can rewrite a body's instructions without changing a single letter!

## Fun Fact

An octopus has three hearts and blue blood — but the real marvel is its brain-to-body ratio. Some octopuses are smarter than dogs, yet their last common ancestor with mammals lived over 500 million years ago. That means intelligence evolved at least twice on our planet.
