# A 1,000-year-old coin hoard in Norway hid one "national treasure" for a thousand years

## A treasure hunt on a farm

In April 2026, two metal detectorists were sweeping a field near the village of Rena in eastern Norway. Their machines beeped — and beeped, and beeped. Under the grass they had found a giant stash of silver coins, the biggest Viking Age coin hoard ever discovered in the country.

At first archaeologists counted about 3,000 coins. As the careful work continued, the number kept climbing. Today the Mørstad Hoard (say it "MER-stad") holds about 5,500 silver coins — and researchers think there may be even more still to count.

## One coin stood out from all the rest

Sorting thousands of identical coins is slow, careful work. But one coin made archaeologist Anne Skogsfjord stop and stare. When she read the strange letters written around its edge — "ONLAF R+ NORMANORV" — she realized what she was holding: a coin stamped by Olaf, King of the Norwegians.

Olaf II Haraldsson was a Viking king who ruled Norway from 1015 to 1028. After his death he became St. Olaf, the country's most famous saint — so important that even today he is Norway's patron saint.

Here is how rare this coin is: only four examples of its exact type had ever been found before, and just one of those in Norway (back in 1924). In total, fewer than 20 coins ever minted by Olaf have survived — most of them found in other countries.

"I realized that we had found a national treasure," Anne Skogsfjord said.

## A coin a king may have held

The coin is beautiful. On one side it shows Olaf's face wearing a conical battle helmet. On the other side is a double cross and words that mean "My leader, my lord."

Coin experts say this design was Olaf's clever way of spreading a big idea. At the time, Olaf was pushing hard to bring Christianity to Norway — and he used his coins like advertising. "This is branding," said one expert. "We can almost imagine Olaf himself holding this coin in his hand."

The coin was probably made around 1024 to 1028, then buried with the rest of the hoard in the late 1040s. It has small knife cuts on both sides — a sign that people used it to buy things, checking the silver as they paid.

## Where you can see it

The Mørstad Hoard goes on public display at Oslo's Historical Museum on November 20, 2026 — the first time any part of the cache will be shown to the world.

## Try this at home

Can you spot the hidden treasure in your own space? Try a "silver hunt": gather 20–30 coins or bottle caps, hide one special one among them (a shiny coin, a special token), and have a family member find it. The real secret of archaeology is patience — the Mørstad coin sat underground for a thousand years before anyone found it.
