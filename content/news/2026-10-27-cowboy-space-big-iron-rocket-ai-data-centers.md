# What if a rocket's top half turned into the satellite it was carrying?

## A rocket and a satellite, all in one

Most rockets work like a firework: they blast off with a roar, shed their empty fuel tanks as they climb, and their final stage either crashes into the ocean or falls back down to be caught on a drone ship. But what if the top half of the rocket — the part that usually just flies away and is lost forever — could **stay in space and turn into the very satellite it was carrying**?

That is exactly what a California startup called **Cowboy Space** is trying to do. On October 27, 2026, the company unveiled its new rocket called **Big Iron**. The rocket is a two-stage design: the first stage comes back to Earth to be reused, and the upper stage does something no other rocket's upper stage does — it stays in orbit and morphs into a satellite.

## How Big Iron works

Big Iron is a shiny, 282-foot-tall (86-meter) stainless-steel rocket, about as tall as a 20-story building lying on its side. It is powered by Cowboy Space's own **Clydesdale engines** — nine of them fire in the first stage, and one fires in the upper stage, a layout similar to SpaceX's Falcon 9. But the key difference is what happens when the first stage separates and falls away: the upper stage keeps going, tips its solar panels open — and becomes a **data-center satellite**.

Each Big Iron satellite weighs about 33,000 pounds (15,000 kg) on Earth and, once in orbit, stretches out to about 433 feet (132 meters) long thanks to its solar arrays. That is longer than four school buses parked end to end!

## Why build 20,000 satellites?

Cowboy Space plans to launch a constellation of about **20,000** of these data-center satellites into **low Earth orbit** (LEO) — the region of space within about 2,000 kilometers (1,200 miles) of Earth where the International Space Station and most satellites live. The company calls this project **Stampede**, because they want to build it up one rocket at a time, launching one satellite per rocket until the constellation is complete. They hope to make their first launch in December 2028.

The idea is that these satellites will carry **artificial intelligence** computers — what the company calls "AI data centers" — into space. Up there, the computers can use solar power without needing to share resources with people on Earth. The payload fairing (the nose cone that protects the satellite during launch) even transforms into a radiator to dump away the heat that thousands of powerful computer chips would generate.

## What can they do right now?

Cowboy Space is not starting from zero. As of October 2026, the company is already operating one small satellite called **Reason-1**, which launched on a SpaceX rideshare mission in early October. Reason-1 is testing a technology called **power beaming** — using a high-power laser to send energy from space down to a receiver on the ground. If that works, it could be the first step toward powering those huge data-center satellites without needing physical power cables from Earth.

The company, founded in 2024 (it was originally called Aetherflux), is still building and testing its Clydesdale engines. But the vision is big: rockets that do not just deliver satellites, they **become** the satellites, and a space-based AI network bigger than anything ever built.

## Try this at home

Next time you see a rocket launch on TV, count how many stages the rocket has. Most rockets jettison (drop off) their first stage, then their second stage, and sometimes a third. Ask yourself: what happens to each piece after it is dropped? With Big Iron's design, the upper stage does not get dropped — it stays in space and becomes the payload. Can you draw a picture of a rocket whose top half transforms into something else?

## Fun Fact

Big Iron's first stage is planned to be reusable — meaning after the first stage drops off, it will find its way back to land (or a landing platform), just like SpaceX's Falcon 9 boosters do. This is part of a newer trend in rocketry where companies try to fly the same hardware again and again, which makes launching things to space less expensive and more frequent.
