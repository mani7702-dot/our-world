# Could Giant Black Holes Be the Universe's Missing Dark Matter?

## What is dark matter, and why do we need it?

Imagine spinning a pizza dough in the air. The faster you spin it, the more the edges f ly outward. **Galaxies spin the same way** — stars on the outer edges whirl around much faster than expected based on the gravity of all the visible stars and gas alone. Without something extra pulling inward, those galaxies should fly apart.

That "something extra" is called **dark matter**. It is invisible, does not glow, and passes right through regular matter and even through whole planets — yet it has gravity. Scientists think dark matter makes up about **five-sixths of all matter** in the universe. But what *is* it?

## Could black holes be hiding the answer?

For decades, astronomers have wondered: could dark matter be made of **black holes** — those ultra-dense leftover cores of dead stars where gravity is so strong that not even light can escape?

There are two flavors of black holes that people have considered:

1. **Small ones** — black holes as heavy as individual stars (or even smaller), called *primordial black holes*, which might have formed right after the Big Bang.

2. **Stupendously large ones** — black holes so massive they would outweigh entire galaxies. Some astronomers imagined these giants could be scattered throughout space like cosmic breadcrumbs, each one heavy enough to count as a chunk of dark matter.

## Checking the cosmic background for clues

The new study, led by theoretical physicists Christopher Gerlach, Yann Gouttenoire, and Nicholas Leister at the Mainz Institute for Theoretical Physics in Germany, looked for evidence of these stupendous giants by examining the **cosmic microwave background** — the faint afterglow light left over from the Big Bang, filling the entire sky.

If dark matter were made of a handful of giant black holes, the universe would look **grainy** on the largest scales — like a photo made of big, coarse pixels instead of smooth detail. But the cosmic microwave background is smooth and even, **not grainy**. That tells us these stupendously large black holes — ones heavier than 100 billion times the mass of our Sun — **cannot be a major part of dark matter**.

The largest black hole we know of weighs about 36 billion suns. The giants in this study would weigh 1 million to 10 billion times that — heavier than whole galaxies put together — and they have simply never been seen.

## What about tiny black holes?

The researchers do **not** rule out all black holes as dark matter candidates. They are now turning their attention to **tiny black holes** the size of asteroids — possibly primordial black holes formed in the first moments after the Big Bang. These mini-black holes would be too small to make the cosmic background look grainy, so they remain a promising mystery to investigate.

## Try this at home

**Make a galaxy spinner!** Fill a small square of clear plastic wrap over a cup, sprinkle a few drops of glitter and a drop of dish soap mixed with water on top, then swirl the cup gently. The glitter represents stars and the soapy water represents invisible dark matter — both together keep everything from flying away as you spin. Try adding more "dark matter" (more soap water) and see how it changes the spin!

## Fun Fact

The cosmic microwave background was discovered by accident in 1964 by two Bell Labs scientists, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, who kept hearing a mysterious "noise" like static coming from their sensitive radio antenna. They later won the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery!

**for-grown-ups:** The study will be published on October 26, 2026 in Physical Review Letters, a peer-reviewed journal established in 1958 by the American Physical Society. The authors analyzed whether the cosmic microwave background (CMB) — the relic radiation from ~380,000 years after the Big Bang — would exhibit a "grainy" structure if dark matter were composed of stupendously large black holes (>100 billion solar masses). The CMB is observed to be smooth at large angular scales, ruling out this scenario. Dark matter is thought to constitute ~85% of all matter in the universe (Wikipedia: Dark matter; Cosmic microwave background). The study builds on the gravitational lensing and CMB anisotropy constraints that are standard tools in modern cosmology.
