# Where do those S-shaped kinks in the solar wind come from?

## The Sun's invisible river has mysterious twists

Imagine a river flowing past you — but instead of water, it's made of **billions of tiny charged particles** rushing away from the Sun at incredible speeds. This is the **solar wind**, and it fills the entire Solar System, flowing past Earth, Mars, and every world in our cosmic neighborhood.

Sometimes, the solar wind's invisible magnetic field gets tangled into strange **S-shaped kinks** called **switchbacks**. It's like a garden hose that suddenly bends back on itself — but in space, where there's no hose and no hand to bend it. Scientists have known these switchbacks exist for years, especially from data collected by NASA's Parker Solar Probe, which has been diving closer to the Sun than any spacecraft before. But the big question remained: **where do these kinks come from?**

## Tracing the kink back to the Sun

In March 2022 — the same month the James Webb Space Telescope captured its first images — the European Space Agency's **Solar Orbiter** happened to be cruising through space about halfway between Earth and the Sun. Without warning, the spacecraft's instruments suddenly detected a **really large** switchback.

"Solar Orbiter flew through a very large switchback," says Dr. Jesse Coburn from the French National Center for Scientific Research (CNRS). "Because of this, we were able to sample rarely observed particles there that have tell-tale fingerprints of their origin."

The trick was looking at the **types of atoms** inside the kink. Solar Orbiter's Solar Wind Analyser (SWA) instrument measured the charge states of oxygen and carbon ions. These atoms looked different depending on what temperature they'd experienced — like a **fingerprint** that tells you where you've been.

The ions carried a clear signature: they had been heated to **millions of degrees** inside the Sun's corona — the super-hot outer atmosphere that crowns our star. The only place this happens is in **closed magnetic loops** that arch up from the solar surface, where the Sun's magnetic field traps and superheats gas before it shoots back out into space.

## Unlocking the Sun's magnetic puzzle

The Sun's magnetic field is like nature's ultimate shapeshifter. Some field lines stretch out freely into space — these are "open" lines that guide the solar wind away. Others loop back down to the Sun — these "closed" loops are where the mysterious heating happens.

The team's discovery points to a process called **interchange reconnection**: when an open field line and a closed loop meet, they can "reconnect" — essentially swapping partners. This launches a chunk of superheated plasma into space, creating the S-shaped kink we call a switchback.

"There are two main competing theories for how a switchback forms," explains Dr. Coburn. "The specific mix of particles detected by Solar Orbiter is the **smoking gun** for interchange reconnection."

But the story doesn't end there. Another theory says waves and turbulence in the solar wind itself can create switchbacks — but the data shows this happens **later**, as the kink travels billions of miles from the Sun.

"Excitingly, we also see signs of waves and turbulence," says co-author Dr. Stephanie Yardley. "Once the switchback has left the Sun, waves and turbulence take over and govern how it moves through space."

This means both theories are partly right — they just operate at **different stages** in a switchback's lifetime. First, reconnection creates the kink at the Sun. Then, waves and turbulence reshape it as it travels outward.

## What this tells us about space weather

Why does this matter? Those switchbacks aren't just cosmic curiosities — they're part of what creates **space weather**, the storms of charged particles that can knock out satellites, power grids, and communication systems on Earth.

By understanding how the solar wind gets its kinks, scientists can better predict when and where the next big space weather event will hit. And that knowledge protects our technology here at home — from the GPS in your phone to the power lines that keep your lights on.

## Try this at home

Fill a bucket with water and shine a flashlight through it. Now add a drop of milk — the water turns cloudy because the milk particles scatter the light. That's like how the Sun's corona scatters and heats particles. Now stir the water in a circle — the swirling motion is like the magnetic field lines twisting and reconnecting. The solar wind is made of this swirling "cosmic soup" streaming past us every second!

## Fun Fact

A single teaspoon of the solar wind would contain so many charged particles that, if you could collect them all, they would weigh only a few grams. But because they're moving at speeds of **300–800 kilometers per second** (that's 670,000–1.8 million miles per hour), they pack enough energy to fill all of Earth's skies with auroras when they hit our magnetic field.
