# How a 178-year-old train delay unmasked a solar storm typo

## The mystery of a slow train

In the 1840s, long before cars and phones ruled the roads, a new invention was racing across Europe: the **electric telegraph**. Messages that used to take days by horse could now fly between cities in minutes, carried by invisible pulses of electricity along wires.

Most evenings, the telegraph worked perfectly. But sometimes — especially on strange nights when the sky rippled with colour — the wires would spark, flicker, or fall silent altogether. People called it "the northern lights playing tricks."

One such night, in the English town of Exeter, a train was **delayed by 16 minutes**. The railway company blamed the telegraph: solar activity had scrambled the signaling system, and the schedule fell apart like dominos.

A scientist wrote it all down in 1871, giving the date as **October 18, 1841**. For more than 150 years, that date stood as the **earliest known moment space weather disrupted human technology**.

It was a great story — but something didn't add up.

## The typo that fooled everyone

Here is the problem: the train line named in the 1871 report, the **Exeter-to-Starcross railway**, did **not exist yet in 1841**. It only opened **in 1846** — five years *after* the storm was supposed to have happened.

That gap was the clue scientists needed. If a railway hadn't been built, a train couldn't have been delayed on it. The date must have been a **typo**.

So a team led by astronomer **Brett Carter** at RMIT University started digging. They compared old railway timetables, hunted through newspaper archives from 1848, checked records of **auroras** (northern lights) seen that night, and read **magnetic-field measurements** that scientists had kept in notebooks.

Every clue pointed to the same night: **October 18, 1848**.

That makes the Exeter incident **7 years older** than originally thought — and possibly the earliest documented case of a solar storm interfering with human tech, even older than the famous **Carrington Event** of 1859.

## Why space storms still matter today

Solar storms are explosions on the Sun: a flare + a cloud of charged particles called a **coronal mass ejection**. When that cloud reaches Earth a few days later, it slams into our magnetic field and makes the whole sky glow.

But the glow is only the pretty part.

The invisible punch can:

- Spark the **aurora borealis** so bright it wakes gold miners who think it is morning (this really happened during the Carrington Event)
- Send **power surges** through power lines, knocking out electricity
- Scramble **GPS** signals that planes and boats rely on
- Fry satellites that phones and weather forecasts depend on

The 1848 Exeter train was one of the first times people noticed. Now, with phones, GPS, and power grids woven into daily life, a big solar storm today could cause far more than a late train.

## The lesson from a 178-year-old typo

A single wrong digit in an old journal moved history backward and forward by five years. But finding the truth also showed something beautiful: **the same Sun that once delayed one train now governs the whole modern world** — and studying old notebooks is still the best way to prepare for the next big space weather day.

## Try this at home

Make a "magnetic storm" with a spoon and a cup. Drop a strong magnet near the bottom of a plastic cup, then shake a spoonful of steel pins or paper clips above it. When you tilt the cup, the pins wiggle and jump just like electrons in a wire feeling a magnetic push. That is a tiny version of how the Sun's invisible forces can jiggle the electricity flowing through long wires — just like the 1848 telegraph did.

## Fun Fact

The Carrington Event of 1859 is named after British astronomer **Richard Carrington**, who watched a solar flare through his telescope and then raced to compare notes — only to find telegraph operators worldwide were already reporting sparks and fires from their equipment. He had witnessed the Sun hitting Earth in real time, for the first time in human history.
