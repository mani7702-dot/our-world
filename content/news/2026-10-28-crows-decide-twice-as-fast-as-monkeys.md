## Two crows, one very fast question

Imagine a screen covered in hundreds of tiny white dots. Most of them drift to the left. A few wander randomly in every other direction. Your job: say which way the crowd is going, and say it fast.

That is a classic test scientists use to study how brains make decisions. Monkeys have done it for decades. People have done it. Now two carrion crows named Iggy and Lemmy have done it — and they were startlingly quick.

The birds pecked the correct side of the screen in about **235 milliseconds** on average. That is roughly a quarter of a second, and it is about **twice as fast** as monkeys and humans manage on the same task.

## Faster, but not sloppier

Speed usually costs accuracy. Not here.

When the dot pattern was easy to read, the crows were right nearly every time. When the dots were a confusing blur, the birds still beat random guessing — they were not simply pecking and hoping.

The researchers, Philipp Schmidbauer and Andreas Nieder at the University of Tübingen in Germany, published the results in the journal *Science*. To find out how the birds managed it, they listened in on individual brain cells while the crows worked.

## The decision shows up before the answer does

Inside a crow's brain there is a region called the **nidopallium caudolaterale**. It does a job similar to the prefrontal cortex in your brain — the part that helps you weigh options and choose.

Just **70 milliseconds** after the dots appeared, cells in that region had already started to split into "left" and "right" camps. The signal built up steadily, like a rising tide, until it crossed a threshold — and the crow pecked.

That is the same pattern seen in monkeys and people. Which is the surprising part. Bird brains are built completely differently: they have no layered cortex at all, just dense clusters of neurons packed into a lumpy structure. Different architecture, same trick.

## Why might a bird brain be quicker?

Nobody knows for sure yet, and the scientists say so plainly. Nieder has a few ideas worth testing:

- **Shorter wires.** A small brain means shorter connections between cells, and signals have less distance to travel.
- **More crowded neurons.** Corvid brains pack two to three times as many neurons into the same volume of tissue as primate brains.
- **A warmer engine.** Birds run hotter than mammals, and warmer chemistry tends to run faster.

There may also be a simple reason for the urgency: a bird steering through branches at speed, or aiming its beak at a moving insect, cannot afford to think slowly.

## Try this at home

Test your own decision speed — with a friend and a stopwatch, no lab needed.

1. Have a friend hold up a page with a big arrow drawn on it, hidden behind a book.
2. On "go", they flip the book away. You shout "left" or "right" as fast as you can.
3. Try ten rounds and have your friend time each one. Then swap.
4. Now make it harder: draw the arrow smaller, or add lots of distracting scribbles around it. Does your speed drop? Does your accuracy?

That trade-off between speed and accuracy is exactly what the crow researchers were measuring — just with dots instead of arrows.

## What it means

For a long time, big brains with layered cortexes were assumed to be the only way to get complex thinking done. Crows keep proving that wrong. They use tools, recognise individual human faces, and plan for tomorrow.

This study adds one more line to the list: they can decide, correctly, in about the time it takes you to blink twice. And they do it with a brain the size of a walnut.

**One honest caveat:** two crows is a very small study. The result is striking, but it needs to be repeated with more birds before anyone calls it a general rule for all crows.
