# How did scientists prove an ancient asteroid slammed into the North Sea 45 million years ago?

## A crater hidden under 700 metres of seabed

About 80 miles off the coast of Yorkshire, England, the seafloor of the North Sea hides a secret. Buried **700 metres** beneath the waves is a nearly perfect circular scar — 3 kilometres wide, surrounded by a ring of fractured rock 20 kilometres across. It is called the **Silverpit Crater**, and for more than two decades scientists argued about how it was made.

Some said it was the fingerprint of a **giant asteroid** that slammed into the ancient sea. Others said it was just underground **salt** slowly squeezing its way out of the ground, pushing the layers above it up and down like a blanket on a bed. In 2009, after a public debate at the Geological Society of London, most scientists voted for the salt explanation.

## The tiny crystals that changed everything

But now, a new study has found the real answer — and it comes down to minerals so small you need a microscope to see them.

Scientists led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson from Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh combined three lines of evidence:

1. **Seismic imaging** — they sent sound waves through the rock layers (like a medical ultrasound, but for geology) and built a 3D map of the crater's hidden shape.
2. **Rock samples** — they examined drill cuttings pulled up by oil wells right next to the crater.
3. **Computer simulations** — they modelled what would happen if a space rock hit the seafloor, then compared it to the real shape on the seabed.

The smoking gun was microscopic: the drill samples contained **shocked quartz** and **shocked feldspar crystals**. These minerals have their atomic structure permanently squashed in a way that can only happen under **immense pressure** — the kind of pressure created when a 160-metre-wide asteroid hits the ocean at **20,000 km/h**.

Ordinary geological processes simply cannot produce this signature. The crystals remember the impact, even after 45 million years of sleeping under the sea.

## What the asteroid left behind

Using their computer models, the scientists reconstructed the moment of impact:

- An asteroid about **160 metres** wide (roughly the length of a football field)
- It struck the seabed at a **shallow angle** from the west
- Within minutes, it blasted a **1.5-kilometre-high curtain** of rock and seawater into the air — that is **five Empire State Buildings** stacked on top of each other
- The water then collapsed back down, generating a **tsunami over 100 metres** high (taller than a 30-storey building)

The impact was 750 times more powerful than the largest nuclear bomb ever built.

## Why buried craters matter

Earth is covered in more impact scars than you might think — about **190 confirmed craters** — but almost none are easy to see. Rain, wind, ice and the slow creep of tectonic plates gradually erase them over millions of years. That is why finding a **well-preserved** crater beneath the ocean floor is so valuable.

The Silverpit Crater gives scientists a rare chance to study how a **space rock hits water**, how the surrounding rock fractures, and how tsunamis form from cosmic impacts. It also helps us understand what could happen if a similar asteroid struck today.

## Fun Fact

Only about **33 impact craters** have ever been found **beneath the ocean** — it is far harder to explore the seafloor than solid ground. The Silverpit Crater is one of the best-preserved underwater examples, giving us a window into a moment when a space rock larger than the Leaning Tower of Pisa hit our planet 45 million years ago.

**for-grownups:** The Silverpit impact structure lies 700 m below the North Sea floor, ~80 miles off Yorkshire. Discovered in 2002 by petroleum geoscientists Simon Stewart and Philip Allen, its origin was debated for years — a 2009 Geological Society debate favoured salt-withdrawal, but a 2025–2026 study (Nicholson et al., Heriot-Watt University + JAMSTEC, published in *Nature Communications*) found shocked quartz and feldspar crystals in drill cuttings. These minerals exhibit planar deformation features that form only under >5 GPa shock pressure — diagnostic of hypervelocity impact. Numerical modelling (Collins, Imperial College London) confirms a ~160 m asteroid struck ~43–46 million years ago at a shallow angle, generating a 100+ m tsunami. Fewer than 33 submarine impact craters are currently known.
