# How did a rock travel 90 kilometers into Earth?

Imagine dropping a pebble in a pond — it sinks straight down. Now picture that on a giant scale: a slab of Earth's crust slowly sliding beneath another and vanishing into the hot darkness below. That is exactly what happened to one rock from Papua New Guinea, and it has something surprising to teach us about heat inside our planet.

## A stone's long, deep journey

Earth's surface is broken into enormous tectonic plates that shuffle around like giant puzzle pieces. When two plates crash together, one usually bends and sinks down into the mantle in a process called **subduction**. An international team of scientists, led by researchers from the University of Göttingen in Germany, held a rock that had completed this journey.

By studying tiny mineral crystals trapped inside the rock, they worked out that it had been carried **at least 90 kilometers (about 56 miles)** beneath the surface roughly **four million years ago**. Along the way, the rock was squeezed and heated until it turned into a dense, red-and-green rock called **eclogite**.

## Tiny time capsules of pressure

Here is the clever part. Deep underground, the rock slowly collected microscopic crystals of a mineral called **coesite**. Coesite can only form under the crushing pressure found dozens of kilometers down — so finding it locked inside the rock is like a passport stamp saying "I was here." The crystals got trapped as the rock changed shape, recording the extreme conditions it experienced.

At its deepest point, about **800°C (1,472°F)** prevailed — hot enough to toast marshmallows millions of times over, yet cool enough that the crystal timers kept working.

## A surprise about Earth's hidden heat

The scientists expected steady warming as the rock sank deeper. But they found something unexpected. At about 45 kilometers (28 miles) down, the rock had already heated up to roughly **700°C (1,292°F)** — only about 100°C cooler than the bottom of its journey, even though it still had another 45 kilometers to go.

That means it got unusually hot at shallower depths, then entered a cooler zone farther down. It is a bit like walking into a warm room, then stepping through a door into a cooler one — and it tells us that heat spreads inside subduction zones may be more complicated than our computer models thought.

## Try this at home

Make your own "subducting slab" with a layer cake. Stack a thick slice of pound cake on a thinner slice of sponge cake and press down gently. The soft bottom layer gets squeezed sideways while the top bends — a tiny model of one tectonic plate diving beneath another. (Ask a grown-up to help!)

## Fun Fact

The deepest hole humans have ever dug — the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia — only reached about **12 kilometers (7.5 miles)**. That is less than a seventh of the way to where this little rock traveled. It went deeper than we ever have, carried there by the slow, mighty motion of Earth's plates.
