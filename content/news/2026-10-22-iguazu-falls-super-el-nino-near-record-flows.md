Most waterfalls are loud. But standing near **Iguazú Falls** in October 2026, a park ranger told NPR, the roar was so deep it "penetrated your body." That is because the water wasn't just falling — it was *surging*.

## How much water are we talking about?

The Iguazú River usually sends about **1 to 1.2 million liters** (that is roughly 265,000 to 320,000 gallons) of water per second over the cliffs. In October 2026, officials measured close to **12 million liters per second** — nearly **ten times** the normal rush.

To picture that, imagine every person on Earth taking a single sip of water at the exact same moment, all flowing past you in one second. That is how much extra water was crashing down.

## Why so much water right now?

Scientists link the deluge to this year's **Super El Niño** — a natural warming of the Pacific Ocean that shifts weather patterns around the globe. When the Pacific gets warmer, it changes the path of rain clouds. Over South America, that means far more rain falling in the Amazon basin and the River Plate watershed, which all flows into the Iguazú River.

"It just keeps raining," said the park director, when asked why walkways had to be closed. The metal catwalks that normally hover just above the falls were completely submerged.

## How the falls work

Iguazú isn't one big wall of water. It is a **complex of hundreds of smaller waterfalls** spread across more than a mile. The river splits as it drops over layers of ancient basalt rock, carving a staircase into the cliffside over thousands of years.

About **90 %** of the river's flow ends up in one dramatic chasm called the **Devil's Throat** — a narrow canyon that is 80 metres deep and 70 metres wide. Standing on the viewing platforms, with the mist rising around you, you can feel the ground tremble.

The good news? The falls are protected inside **Iguazú National Park** in Argentina and **Iguaçu National Park** in Brazil — both UNESCO World Heritage sites since the 1980s — so the natural show will keep going for future visitors to witness.

## Fun Fact

Iguazú Falls is actually **wider** than Victoria Falls on the Zambia-Zimbabwe border and nearly **twice as tall**. Local legend says the waterfalls were created when a spurned god sliced open the river in a fit of rage — but scientists know it was slow, patient erosion over millions of years.

**Try this at home:** Fill a tray with sand or soil, pile it up to make a small hill, and pour water slowly from a jug at the top. Watch how the water finds different paths down, carving channels just like a real river! You can see the same branching pattern that makes Iguazú's dozens of cascades.

**For the grown-ups:** The extraordinary October 2026 flow (~12 million L/s, ~425,000 cfs) at Iguazú corresponds to the peak of the 2025–2026 El Niño event, whose enhanced convection over the tropical South Atlantic increases moisture advection into the River Plate basin. Iguazú Falls straddles the Argentina-Brazil border on the Iguazú River, dropping ~80 m over a 2.7 km basalt sill into the Garganta del Diablo gorge; the river erodes headward at ~1.4–2.1 cm/year. The site has been a UNESCO World Heritage property since 1984 (Argentina) and 1986 (Brazil), protecting both the geological feature and its subtropical rainforest ecosystem.
