# What happens when zoo lions eat like wild cats — whole prey, feasts, and fasts?

## A feast that brings out the wild side

Lions in the wild don't rush to a food bowl at dinnertime. A pride of lions might go days between meals, then gorge on a single large kill — tearing, pulling, and manipulating every bit of meat before resting for days. But in zoos, lions are often served neatly portioned meals on a schedule. What happens when keepers switch to a more "wild style" approach?

Researchers from **Adelaide University** and **Monarto Safari Park** in South Australia wanted to find out. They studied three adult male **African lions**, introducing whole animal carcasses into their diet — about 200 kg of meat spread across the group, left available for up to three days, then removed for a resting and fasting period of about nine days.

## Three times longer at the dinner table

Using eight infrared cameras running around the clock, the team captured one of the most detailed 24-hour behavioral records of lions in human care. The difference was striking: when carcasses were provided, the lions spent **nearly three times longer** feeding than usual. They tore at the meat, pulled at bones, and manipulated their food in ways that mirror what wild lions do after a successful hunt.

"After a wild hunt, lions eat as much as they can, then rest while their bodies digest," explained lead researcher Lesia Hryhorenko. "We wanted to see if feeding routines that copy nature would bring out more natural behaviors in zoo lions."

## Rest, then rise — just like the wild

Once the feasting was done, the lions settled into long hours of rest and digestion. But as the days of fasting continued, their activity slowly crept back up — a rhythm that closely matched the feast-and-fast cycle wild lions follow.

The study also revealed something surprising about pacing — that repetitive walking some captive lions do. The researchers found pacing did **not** rise because the lions were hungry. Instead, it was tied to everyday routines like the arrival of keepers or being moved between enclosure areas. And importantly, introducing whole carcasses caused no increase in aggression or social tension among the lions.

## Why it matters

The results show that how we feed zoo animals — not just what we feed them — shapes their daily lives. Giving lions the chance to tear apart whole prey gives them more than just a meal. It gives them the chance to be lions.

## Fun Fact

A single African lion can eat up to 15% of its body weight in one meal — roughly the equivalent of a human eating 22 pounds of food at once!
