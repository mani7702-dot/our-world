Every year, when the autumn air turns cool and the marigold stalls pop up at every street corner, India gets ready for one of its brightest festivals. Dussehra — which means "tenth day" in Hindi — is the day when good throws a party and bad is sent packing in a blaze of fireworks.

## The story behind the effigy

Long, long ago, so the story goes, a king named Rama rescued his wife Sita after she was taken by the mighty demon king Ravana. After a great battle, Rama's arrow finally defeated Ravana — and the whole kingdom cheered. To remember that victory, people build towering effigencies of Ravana, his brother Kumbhakarna, and his son Meghanada, stuff them with fireworks, and set them off in a burst of sparks and cheers.

But Ravana isn't the only villain in the story. In the eastern and southern parts of India, Dussehra is also the day goddess Durga fought and won against the scary buffalo demon Mahishasura. So while the north burns Ravana, the east dances for Durga. One festival, many celebrations.

## How India celebrates

For nine nights before Dussehra, families welcome goddess Durga into their homes with flowers, sweets, and prayers. This part is called Navratri, and it's a time of fasting, feasting, and folk dances that go on until dawn. On the final tenth day, the effigies come out.

Markets fill with clay lamps, colourful clothes, and — of course — the giant paper monsters waiting for their dramatic end. Children run between the stalls, their faces painted like heroes and gods, while street performers stage the ancient battle with music and dance. It all happens on the same night, across thousands of towns and cities, all at once.

## Why Dussehra leads to Diwali

Here's a fun secret: Dussehra and Diwali are neighbours on the calendar. Just twenty days after Dussehra ends, India lights up for Diwali, the festival of lights. Some people say Dussehra clears away the bad energy so Diwali can bring in the good. Others say Rama came home on Diwali after his battle — so the two festivals are like the two acts of one big story.

**Try this at home:** build a mini effigy out of cardboard and newspaper — just Ravana's head with a few scary horns. Fill it with sparklers (adult help only!) and watch it go whoosh. It's a tiny taste of the real thing.
