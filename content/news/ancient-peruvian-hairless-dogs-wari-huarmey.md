# A 1,200-year-old mystery in Peru was solved by a dog's missing teeth

Five hundred years before the Inca Empire ruled South America, another civilization controlled the Andes. It was called the **Wari Empire**. And at one of its great cities, Castillo de Huarmey in northern Peru, archaeologists have just found something remarkable: the bones of ancient **hairless dogs**, including naturally mummified remains more than 1,200 years old.

It is the first physical proof that Peruvian hairless dogs lived at a Wari site. Before this, these dogs were known mostly from pottery — little clay figurines the ancient artists made. Now we have their real bones.

## How did the dogs survive 1,200 years?

The secret is **dryness**. Castillo de Huarmey sits in one of the driest places on Earth, the Peruvian coastal desert. When something is buried there, it doesn't rot the way it would in a wet forest. Skin, hair, ears, and even teeth can survive for over a thousand years.

The archaeologists found three sets of dog remains that stood out:

- A naturally mummified dog skull, its hairless skin and ears still attached, painted red with a mineral called **cinnabar** — a pigment used in ancient Peruvian burials.
- A second, larger dog skull.
- An almost-complete mummified male dog, buried in a shallow pit, missing only the lower front legs.

Radiocarbon dating showed the complete male dog was at least 1,200 years old — the oldest specimen ever recovered from the site.

## The tooth that gave it all away

But the real detective work happened **inside the bones**. The team studied the dogs' teeth and the chemistry locked inside them. And that's how they solved the riddle.

Here's the cool part: the scientists noticed the dogs were missing some teeth — not because they fell out. Some teeth had **never grown in at all**. First premolars and some back molars simply never appeared.

What could cause that? The **same gene** that makes a dog hairless is linked to a **reduced number of teeth**. So the missing teeth were the clue: these were **Peruvian hairless dogs**, the ancient ancestors of the breed we know today (the "Peruvian Inca Orchid").

The chemistry told another story too. The dogs' bones showed they ate a diet full of **maize** (corn), a major Andean food — sometimes a lot like what Wari **children** ate. One puppy, just 6–8 weeks old, was even buried alongside an elite artist. It seems these animals held a special place in the community.

## What does this tell us?

1,200 years ago, people and dogs had the same kind of complicated bond they have today. Some dogs were buried carefully, others were treated like waste. And a gene that seems like a quirk — "no hair" — also hides a second, secret effect: "fewer teeth." One tiny set of instructions in the dog's DNA caused both.

**For grown-ups:** The hairless-dog mutation (the *FOXI3* gene, linked to hairlessness and tooth development in the breed) causes first premolars and some molars to fail to erupt. The researchers used zooarchaeology and stable-isotope analysis of the dogs' teeth (puppy diet) and bones (last year of life) to reconstruct what the animals ate and where they lived.
