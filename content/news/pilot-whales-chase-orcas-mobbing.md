# Why do pilot whales chase killer whales?

In the deep blue of the North Atlantic, something odd happens. A pod of long-finned pilot whales — gentle, social creatures that swim in tight family groups — spots a much scarier neighbor: a pod of killer whales, the ocean's true apex predators. Instead of fleeing, the pilot whales do something surprising. They approach. They chatter loudly. Sometimes, they chase back.

## The giant vs. the conqueror

Killer whales, or orcas, hunt seals, sharks, and even other whales. Their targets know to keep their distance. Pilot whales, by contrast, spend their lives in stable matrilineal pods, gossiping with clicks and whistles, raising their young in communal crèches. A pilot whale's idea of danger is usually a fishing net — not a 30-foot black-and-white predator with a taste for blubber.

Yet off the coast of Iceland, where the two species overlap, scientists have watched pilot whales repeatedly pursue orcas. In 24 recorded encounters between 2007 and 2020, orcas backed down in 68% of cases, and the stand-offs often escalated into high-speed chases, with both species leaping clear of the water at up to 25 kilometers per hour.

## Fighting with sound

Mobbing is a behavior well known in land birds — think of chickadees dive-bombing a hawk — but for a whale to mob a predator ten times its hunting prowess takes guts. Pilot whales don't attack. They harass. They circle. They broadcast the situation to their pod.

A 2026 experiment showed how smart the strategy is. Researchers played recordings of pilot-whale calls to tagged orcas. The orcas — normally supremely confident — reacted exactly like prey: they tightened their formations, altered their own vocal behavior, turned away, and accelerated. The pilot whales had triggered the predators' own mobbing defense just by calling.

Earlier studies add another twist: when hunting, long-finned pilot whales off Australia have been observed mimicking orca calls to scare off competitors at a shared meal. Same predator, same trick — used both as a shield and a weapon.

## Fun Fact

Pilot whales are so social they never sleep alone. Pods take turns resting, with some individuals staying alert while others drift into short, surface-side naps. It's teamwork that keeps them alive in a world full of teeth.

## Try this at home

Next time a bigger kid picks on someone smaller, think of the pilot whales. Sometimes the gentlest response — staying in a group, making noise, and not backing down — is enough to send the bully away. Try it: stand a little taller, speak up, and watch how the situation changes.
