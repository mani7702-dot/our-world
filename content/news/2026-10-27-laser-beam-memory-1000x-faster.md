# Can a tiny laser beam make computer memory 1,000 times faster?

When a computer saves a photo or a game, it's really writing a secret code: a long line of 0s and 1s. Inside a hard drive, those digits are stored as tiny magnet spots on a spinning disc. A "north" spot means one, a "south" spot means the other.

To change a 1 to a 0, computers use a magnet to flip the spot's direction. But magnets are slow and clunky for this job. Engineers at the University of California San Diego just found a much faster way: flip the spots with a tiny, super-focused **laser beam**.

## Writing with light instead of magnets

The trick is called **optical switching** — using light to change magnetism. Light can arrive in impossibly short bursts and can be squeezed into a really small spot, so it could write data far faster than any physical magnet.

The UC San Diego team built a special ultrafast laser and reshaped its beam, making it dramatically smaller than any used before. When that super-fine beam hits the magnetic material, the first quick pulse heats a tiny dot and flips its magnetic direction. More pulses follow, and the little reversed dot slowly grows until it holds a stable new 0 or 1.

## Why it could be 1,000 times faster

The team estimates this light-powered method could work **more than 1,000 times faster** than the way hard drives flip magnets today. Because the beam can be focused so small, future memory chips could also pack far more data into the same tiny space.

And here's a neat surprise: the engineers made it work without even caring which way the light was "spinning" — earlier attempts depended on a special kind of light, which made them fragile. By shaping the beam just right, they got the magnetic switch to happen in a much thicker material (nine layers of platinum and cobalt, where the old record was only three). That removes a big obstacle to building real devices.

## The catch

So can we all get faster computers tomorrow? Not yet. The setup needs a specialist ultrafast laser that's too big and too expensive to squeeze inside a phone or laptop today. The engineers call this a promising path to explore, not a finished product — a first exciting step toward a future where light, not magnets, writes our data.

**Try this at home:** Watch a fridge magnet push a paperclip. Now imagine replacing your hand with an invisible beam of light that does that flip a million times a second. That's the superpower these engineers are chasing.

**For grown-ups:** All-optical switching (AOS) of ferromagnetic domains usually demands material-specific polarization coupling or exchange-bias engineering. Ndao's group showed that by engineering the optical beam itself — tailoring the polarization distribution and tight focusing (spot below ~1.36 um) — they achieved deterministic, helicity-independent magnetization reversal in stand-alone [Pt/Co] multilayers up to N=9, controlling local heating and optical torques without external bias. The mechanism decouples switching from intrinsic material properties, and the projected >10^3x speedup over field-driven switching motivates integration with on-chip photonics. The technology is not yet device-ready; the ultrafast Ti:sapphire source cannot be co-integrated on a chip. Published as *Optical beam shaping induced reconfigurable magnetic domains*, Nature Communications 17(1), 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-77572-3.
