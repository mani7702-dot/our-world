# Is that really Venus, or is it the Sun? This weekend Venus slips behind our star

## Venus goes hiding

If you step outside this weekend and look toward the horizon just before sunrise, you might wonder: where did Venus go?

The brilliant "evening star" — the third-brightest object in our sky after the Sun and Moon — has been dazzling stargazers for weeks. But starting **Saturday, October 24th**, Venus will slip behind the Sun from Earth's viewpoint. Astronomers call this moment **inferior conjunction** — when Venus moves directly between Earth and the Sun, lost in our star's brilliant glare.

Don't worry: Venus isn't gone forever. It's just taking a quick bow behind the Sun as part of its regular 224-day orbit around our star.

## Why can't we see Venus during this dance?

From Earth, Venus appears to travel along a imaginary line in the sky called the **ecliptic** — the same path the Sun appears to follow through the year. When Venus reaches inferior conjunction, it sits so close to the Sun in our sky that the Sun's glare completely washes it out.

It's like trying to spot a firefly next to a searchlight — even though the firefly is right there, the bright light makes it invisible. That's exactly what happens to Venus: it's physically there, but the Sun's brilliance hides it from our eyes.

## Venus's 8-year cosmic dance

Venus doesn't just drift randomly — it follows a beautiful, repeating pattern. Because Venus orbits the Sun every 224 days while Earth takes 365 days, the two planets line up in the same configuration roughly every **8 years** (or 13 Venus orbits ≈ 8 Earth orbits). This reliable rhythm is why ancient sky-watchers could predict Venus's appearances with remarkable accuracy.

During this cycle, Venus goes through phases just like our Moon — sometimes a thin crescent, sometimes a brilliant "full" evening star. The famous astronomer Galileo first saw these phases in 1610 through his telescope, and they were among the first clues that not everything orbits the Earth.

## Fun Fact: Earth's nearest neighbor

Venus is often called Earth's "**sister planet**" because it is almost the same size — about **12,103 km wide**, just 638 km smaller than Earth. If you could stand on Venus's surface (and you really shouldn't — it's 464°C, hot enough to melt lead!), you'd weigh almost as much as you do at home. But Venus's thick, toxic atmosphere traps heat so effectively that it became the hottest world in our Solar System — even hotter than Mercury, which is much closer to the Sun!

## Try this at home

Make a simple planet orbit model with a flashlight (the Sun), a large marble (Earth), and a small bead or tiny marble (Venus). Shine the flashlight on a dark wall and walk the "Earth" marble around it. Hold the "Venus" bead at a smaller distance and watch how, from Earth's view, Venus appears to swing around the Sun. You can model Venus's inferior conjunction right on your living room floor — just remember to turn the lights down and let the flashlight shine bright!
