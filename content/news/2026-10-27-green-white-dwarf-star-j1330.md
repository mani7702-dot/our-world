# What does the universe's very first green star look like?

## A star that should exist — but glows green instead

When you imagine a star burning in space, you probably picture it as white, orange, red, or blue. After all, a star's color is usually set by how hot it is: cooler stars look orange-red, Sun-like stars appear white, and the hottest stars shine pale blue.

But astronomers have now found a star that breaks the rule. Meet **WD J1330+6435** — a dead star called a *white dwarf* that would look genuinely **green** if you could see it up close. It is the first star known to shine green, and it opens a hidden rainbow of colors that scientists never knew dead stars could show.

## Why this dead star looks like liquid jade

White dwarfs are the leftover hearts of stars like our Sun. Once a star runs out of fuel, it collapses in on itself until it is compressed into an Earth-sized sphere so dense that a single spoonful would weigh tons. There is no more nuclear fusion — they simply glow from leftover heat, fading slowly over billions of years.

The green color comes from what the star has "eaten." Over time, WD J1330+6435 has pulled in rocky debris from planets that once orbited it. That debris carried elements like sodium, calcium, and lithium into the star's atmosphere. Under the star's crushing density — about a million times denser than Earth's air — those elements behave strangely. Sodium absorbs light strongly across yellow, orange, and red wavelengths, stripping those colors out of the mix. What remains leaks out as ghostly green.

Think of it like dropping food coloring into a glass of water: the red and yellow dyes vanish, and only the blue-green tint stays visible.

## Dead stars hiding surprising rainbow shades

This is not a one-off. By studying the spectra of white dwarfs across the sky — splitting their light into a rainbow of wavelengths like a cosmic prism — the team found that dead stars come in pink, purple, yellow, and even cyan, depending on what chemicals sit in their atmospheres.

- **Carbon-rich** white dwarfs often glow **pink or purple**.
- White dwarfs that swallowed **asteroid or comet** debris tend to look **yellow**.
- The coolest ones, where hydrogen and helium atoms bump into each other under extreme pressure, can shift to **cyan or pale blue**.

Because no white dwarf is close enough for human eyes to see its true color, the researchers used precise starlight measurements instead, running the data through a model of how the human eye perceives color. This shows that a star's appearance can tell us what it has been eating long after its life ended.

## Try this at home

Pour a thick glass of water and add one drop of milk so it turns milky. Shine a flashlight through it — the light that comes out looks slightly greenish because the milk scatters away the reds and blues. That's a tiny echo of how dense starlight can lose whole colors and let just one shade shine through.

## Fun Fact

The nearest known white dwarf is Sirius B — the bright "dog star" Sirius has a faint, invisible companion that is the remnant of a star that once was the brightest in our sky, billions of years before our Sun was born.

**for-grownups:** WD J1330+6435 was studied by Dr. Mark Hollands' team at the University of Warwick and reported in *RAS Techniques and Instruments* (RASTI). The green appearance is a calculated human-eye color derived from spectroscopic analysis of atmospheric sodium absorption in a ~4,000 °C white dwarf that has accreted planetary debris; no direct visual observation is possible. Wikipedia's "White dwarf" confirms that these stellar remnants are Earth-sized, supported by electron degeneracy pressure, and radiate from residual heat without fusion — the dense atmospheric chemistry that produces anomalous colors is consistent with this established physics.
