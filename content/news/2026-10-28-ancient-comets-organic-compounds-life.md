# 1. Did Space Snowballs Deliver the Building Blocks of Life?

For a long time, scientists liked to imagine a beautiful story: huge dirty snowballs called **comets** drifted through the early Solar System, crashing into the young Earth and dumping the raw chemical ingredients — amino acids, sugars, and other organic molecules — that would one day spark life itself. It was a romantic idea, like nature's own delivery service.

The story had strong evidence. Every time a space probe has flown close to a comet, it has found a treasure chest of carbon- and nitrogen-bearing molecules. The European Space Agency's **Rosetta mission** landed on comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko in 2014 and detected **glycine** (an amino acid) and **phosphorus** — exactly the kinds of things life needs. Comets seemed perfect delivery trucks for the chemistry of life.

But a big "but" comes next, and it is the key to today's discovery.

# 2. The Crash That Destroys the Cargo

Comets travel at **tremendous speed** — often tens of kilometers per second. When something hits Earth at that speed, the energy is staggering. To picture it, imagine dropping a marble from a skyscraper; now imagine it hitting a brick wall at bullet speed. That is the gentle end of a comet impact.

New computer modelling by Dr. Richard Anslow from the University of Cambridge shows that these **hypervelocity impacts generate such intense heat and pressure** that the fragile organic molecules — especially **hydrogen cyanide (HCN)**, a key prebiotic chemical — are almost entirely **destroyed before they can reach the ground**. It is not enough for comets to *carry* the right chemicals; they must *deliver* them intact, and the violence of the crash tends to vaporize the cargo.

Even worse, the period of heavy comet bombardment ended quickly — within the first **500 million years** of Earth's history. That leaves only a handful of rare, perfectly-angled, slow-moving impacts that could have preserved anything at all.

# 3. Why Mars Might Have Had Better Luck

Dr. Anslow's team discovered something surprising while studying Earth: **Mars might have been a better target.** Mars is smaller than Earth and has a much thinner atmosphere, so comets slamming into Mars do not heat up as violently. The researchers' models show that comets survive to the surface **five times more often** on Mars than on Earth.

This does not mean life definitely sprang up on the Red Planet — the study's authors are careful to say that cometary delivery to Mars is still **highly unlikely**, and other ingredients for life would still need to come from somewhere else. But if cometary delivery ever worked anywhere, Mars would have been the favoured address.

The researchers say their findings will help future astronomers decide which distant worlds to scan for life, especially when next-generation telescopes like the **Habitable Worlds Observatory** come online in the coming years.

### Fun Fact
The word "comet" comes from the Ancient Greek *kome*t*,* meaning "hair of the head" — because ancient stargazers thought a comet looked like a tuft of hair flowing from the Sun across the sky.

**for-grownups:** A new study led by Dr. Richard Anslow (University of Cambridge, Institute of Astronomy) challenges the century-old hypothesis that comets seeded Earth with the prebiotic molecules needed for life. The study focuses on hydrogen cyanide (HCN) — a key prebiotic feedstock — and uses Monte Carlo simulations of Earth's cometary bombardment, comet-atmosphere interaction models, and dynamical models of comet origin/evolution. Results: (1) cometary bombardment declined rapidly after the first 500 Myr; (2) hypervelocity impacts destroy >99% of fragile cometary organics (HCN especially) before surface delivery; (3) even surviving impacts rarely achieve prebiotically relevant concentrations; (4) cometary delivery is 5x more effective on Mars due to lower impact velocities and a thinner atmosphere. The finding that "Earth's cometary bombardment was unable to provide the concentrated reservoir of bioessential feedstock molecules required for synthetic prebiotic chemistry" does not rule out abiogenesis — other sources (asteroids, endogenous synthesis) remain viable. Published Oct 28, 2026 in *Scientific Reports* (Nature). Cross-checked against the University of Cambridge press release (Oct 28, 2026) and Wikipedia "Comet" / "Hydrogen cyanide."
