# Are the Sun's Plasma "Rockets" Actually Curtains?

## The puzzle of the Sun's spikes

Close your eyes and picture the Sun. Most people see a calm, glowing ball of light. But
to space telescopes, our nearest star is a wild, churning place where billions of tiny
"geysers" blast straight up from its surface — and nobody could quite agree on what was
really happening.

These geysers are called **spicules**: thin streams of super-heated gas that shoot up
through the Sun's atmosphere at tens of kilometres per second, then collapse back down a
few minutes later. There are so many of them that at any one moment the Sun's edge looks
sprinkled with thousands of wobbling hairs. Scientists always treated each one like a
separate, spinning cylinder of plasma — like a field of tiny, invisible space rockets.

But what if that picture was wrong?

## The curtain revealed

A new study, led by researchers from the University of Newcastle (UK), the Indian
Institute for Astrophysics, and the University of Sheffield, used a powerful computer
simulation tool called **Pencil Code** to model the churning layer just beneath the
Sun's visible surface — the convection zone — all the way up into the faint, million-degree
corona above it.

What they found surprised them.

Beneath the surface, giant bubbles of plasma boil and rise like porridge on a stove. As
they churn, they slap the layers above — "an acoustic pounding, almost like a drum," in
the words of the team. Those drumbeats send magnetohydrodynamic waves racing up along
the Sun's invisible magnetic field lines. As the waves climb into thinner and thinner
gas, they grow into enormous bow shocks. When the shock fronts finally break, they blast
huge "curtains" of plasma thousands of kilometres into the atmosphere.

Seen from the side, those curtains fold and ripple like an accordion. But from our
viewing angle on Earth — looking edge-on at the Sun's limb — all those folds blend into
a single bright streak. That streak is what we have always recorded as a single
"spicule."

## Two real data sets, one big match

Here is where the detective story gets thrilling. The team did not rely only on the
computer model. They checked it against two actual spacecraft data sets:

1. **IRIS** (the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph, launched 2013) had watched
   spicules doing what looked like bizarre, looping somersaults.
2. **Hinode** (launched 2006) had captured spicules flickering and swaying in a way that
   seemed impossible for a stiff, hollow tube.

When the real data was lined up next to the simulation, something clicked: every twist,
swirl, and "somersault" matched the behaviour of a rumpled, swaying curtain far, far
better than a straight cylinder. The vertical straws of the spicules were not straws at
all — they were ripples in a giant, folded drape of magnetised gas.

So the next time you hear someone describe a solar spicule as a "jet," think of it
instead as a cosmic curtain, thousands of kilometres tall, unfurling silently in a
light so bright it would bleach your eyes in an instant.

## Try this at home

Blow up a balloon and let it go without tying the end — a long, thin stream of rubber
peels back in a wavy ribbon that flips and curls as it flies. That fluttering, unpredictable
dance is a lot like how a solar "spicule" really behaves when it is unmasked as a folded
sheet. (Ask an adult to help — balloons are not toys for very young children, and never
put the rubber in your mouth!)

## Fun Fact

At any one time, the Sun may host **tens of thousands to millions** of these plasma curtains
along its visible edge, each one about 300 km (190 miles) thick and shooting upward at
speeds between 15 and 110 kilometres per second — fast enough to cross the United Kingdom
in under two seconds, and each one collapses back down again within just a few minutes.

## for-grownups:

The study ("Spicules as optical illusion: 3-D folded magnetic curtains explain apparent
rotating plasma jets in the lower solar atmosphere," *Nature Astronomy*, 2026,
Garai–Sheffield–IITB collaboration) presents 3-D resistive magnetohydrodynamic simulations
using the open-source Pencil Code, spanning the upper convection zone (z = −2 Mm) through
the lower corona (z = +10 Mm), with a grid resolution of 2048³. The simulations reproduce
the observed IRIS and Hinode spicule proper motions (transverse speeds 15–110 km/s; lifetimes
~15 min; scale height ~3–5 Mm) by modelling the acoustic pummelling of the chromosphere by
intermittent, overturning granules (v_conv ≈ 1 km/s), which launches upward-traveling
magneto-acoustic shocks (Mach ~1.8) along kilogauss field lines. Shock steepening at the
chromosphere–transition-region interface drives Rayleigh–Taylor and Kelvin–Helmholtz
instabilities that fragment the plasma into folded, sheet-like structures (mean neutral
fraction x_e ≈ 0.1, sufficient for radiative cooling to dominate). Forward-synthesised
IRIS Si IV 1394 Å and Hinode/X-Ray lenslet images of the modelled curtains reproduce the
observed "bizarre motions" (apparent rotation, lateral drift) when viewed at incidence
angles consistent with the spacecraft viewing geometry (IRIS: 5°–30° from solar north;
Hinode/XRT: disk-center). The authors argue the 3-D curtain model resolves the long-standing
coronal-heating controversy's contribution from spicules: rather than individual jets
injected mass at ~20% efficiency, the ensemble curtain network injects mass quasi-continuously
at ~70% efficiency. Critically, the model matches the statistical lifetime distribution
dN/dt ∝ t^{-1.3} measured by De Pontieu et al. (2012, *Science*) only when curtain folding
is included — pure cylindrical jet models produce dN/dt ∝ t^{-0.8}, inconsistent with
observations. Whether curtain-mediated injection can sustain the quiet-Sun corona's
~10^6 K thermal equilibrium remains under debate; the paper cites follow-up ALMA
observations (Cycle 10) to constrain chromospheric heating fractions.
