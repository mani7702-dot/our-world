# How do tiny snakes roll four times faster than they slither?

## The snake that turns into a rolling ball

Most snakes move by sliding side to side, a slow and steady motion called *slithering*. But some very small snakes in Southeast Asia have learned a much faster trick: they **roll**. Instead of wiggling along the ground, they tuck and twist their bodies so they can zip forward at up to **four times** their normal speed — like a tiny living top that never stops pointing the right way.

The rolling champions are a type of **dwarf reedsnake** (*Pseudorabdion saravacense*). These are slender, worm-like snakes that live on the forest floor among leaf litter. They are so small you could almost fit one in your palm, yet they can out-roll almost anything in the snake world.

## Why rolling is harder than it looks

Rolling might look easy, but it is actually one of the trickiest ways an animal can move. A bicycle wheel spins around a fixed axle, but an animal has no axle — its head, eyes, and brain are all part of the same body that is spinning. If a snake rolled by tumbling every part at once, it would be dizzy, blind, and unable to steer. That is why so few animals roll on purpose, and why the dwarf reedsnake's secret is so clever.

## The clever “stroke” that keeps the head steady

Scientists at Toho University filmed the snakes moving from many angles and watched something remarkable. The snakes do **not** spin their whole body together. Instead, each body segment works in a carefully timed pattern: the back half rotates rapidly, driving the snake forward, while the front half sweeps downward in a smooth arc beside the head. The researchers call that downward sweep a **“stroke”** — like a single oar pull through water.

With each stroke, the side touching the ground switches from left to right, and the stroke flips direction too. Repeating this pattern over and over creates a rolling wave that pushes the snake ahead. And through it all — even at full speed — the **head stays level and points forward**, so the snake never needs to stop and re-orient itself.

## Speed that beats a sprint

When they roll, the dwarf reedsnakes reach forward speeds of about **1.7 to 3.7 meters per second** — roughly **four times faster** than when they slither. That is the difference between a gentle walk and a fast run, all packed into an animal smaller than your hand. The extra speed can be the difference between escaping a predator and becoming dinner.

## Try this at home

Take a book and lay it flat on a table. Roll a pencil along it like a tiny log. Notice how the pencil moves faster and smoother than if you dragged it sideways? That is a little bit like how the snake rolls. Now try tilting the book slightly — the pencil will curve, just like a rolling snake steering around an obstacle!

## Fun Fact

There are about **1,300 species of reed snakes** around the world, but only the dwarf ones are known to roll. The largest rolling record belongs to this Southeast Asian dwarf reedsnake — the only snake confirmed to roll at sustained high speed while keeping its head steady the whole way.

## for-grown-ups

The reclassification shows how much animal behavior can hide in plain sight. Rolling is rare among snakes, yet field guides have noted it for decades. The Toho University team’s kinematic analysis — measuring how each part of the body moves frame by frame — reveals that the snake achieves high-speed rolling through behavioral coordination alone, with no special joints or wheels. This kind of study helps biologists understand how brains control motion, how animals stay balanced at speed, and how evolution can turn a simple body shape into a high-performance escape tool.
