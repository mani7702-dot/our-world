# 5 ways El Niño is reshaping weather around the world

You might think a warm patch of water in the Pacific Ocean is just a local weather thing. But El Niño is the planet's thermostat gone slightly off-kilter — and it affects almost every weather pattern on Earth. The current El Niño is the strongest on record, and the World Meteorological Organization says it will stick around until at least 2027. Here are five places where it is already making its mark.

## 1. Worse wildfires in Indonesia

When El Niño shows up, Indonesia usually gets drier and hotter. This year has been no exception. Since January, fires have burned more than 895,000 hectares of land — nearly six times the size of Greater London. That is more than twice the area burned last year, when there was no El Niño influence.

The fires have sent thick, toxic haze across Sumatra and Kalimantan, affecting millions of people. Schools have closed, and hospitals have seen more visitors with breathing problems. The smoke travels so far because El Niño's stronger-than-usual high-pressure system sits over the region, blocking rainclouds and letting the dry conditions linger.

## 2. Stronger hurricanes in the Pacific

In the eastern Pacific, El Niño fans the flames of hurricane season. Warm ocean water feeds storms with energy, and the weaker high-altitude winds during El Niño mean less wind shear — the force that tears hurricanes apart.

So far this season, there have already been 22 named storms and 12 hurricanes, including 10 major hurricanes. That is more than an entire average season, and the season is not even over. Hurricane Polo is one dramatic example — it spun up so fast that wind gusts hit 180 mph (285 km/h), making it one of the strongest storms on record.

## 3. A quieter Atlantic

While the Pacific is stormy, the Atlantic Ocean has been eerily calm. El Niño's stronger winds over the Atlantic create wind shear that tears apart developing storms before they get big.

By this point in an average season, forecasters expect 10 named storms, five hurricanes, and two major hurricanes. This year, there have only been eight tropical storms and one hurricane — Isaias, which hit Florida and Alabama. That is the latest-forming hurricane on record in the satellite era, which began in the 1960s.

## 4. Drier monsoons in India

El Niño does not just affect oceans — it also shifts the great summer monsoon rains that billions of people depend on. This year, India's monsoon rainfall was about 13% below average.

The rain did not just come in smaller amounts — it arrived in fits and starts. Central India got about what it expected, but the northeast was 26% drier than normal. Southern states were 24% below average. Long dry spells followed by sudden heavy rains make farming harder, because crops need steady water, not surprise flooding.

## 5. More typhoons in East Asia

Over in the western Pacific, El Niño's warmer waters have fueled an unusually busy typhoon season. Eastern China and Japan have seen 10 tropical cyclones so far — the highest number in a season since 1966.

Storms like Maysak and Dujuan have brought widespread floods and landslides. The warmer Pacific waters that feed these typhoons are the same warm patch at the heart of El Niño itself.

## Why a warm patch of water matters

El Niño is part of a natural cycle called ENSO — the El Niño-Southern Oscillation. It is the warming phase, where trade winds weaken and warm surface water piles up in the eastern Pacific instead of the western Pacific. That warm water then heats the air above it, changing pressure patterns that steer weather all over the planet.

The result is a domino effect: dry where it should be wet, wet where it should be dry, stronger storms in some basins, and calmer seas in others. El Niño usually lasts about a year — but this record-breaking event is sticking around longer, which means its weather fingerprints will stay visible for more seasons to come.

> **Try this at home:** Fill a baking dish with water and sprinkle in some food coloring. Watch how the color spreads and swirls. In the ocean, warm water acts like the food coloring — it does not stay in one spot, but spreads out and changes everything around it.

> **Fun Fact:** The word "El Niño" means "the Christmas boy" in Spanish — Peruvian fishermen gave the warm-water pattern that name because it usually arrives around the December holiday.
