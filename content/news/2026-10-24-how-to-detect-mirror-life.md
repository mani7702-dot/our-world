# How could we detect mirror life if it existed?

Every living thing on Earth — from the tiniest bacterium to the tallest tree — is built from molecules that all twist the same way. It is a bit like having a pair of gloves where only left-handed ones exist: every single living thing wears the same kind of glove. But what if scientists one day created a right-handed version? The result would be a creature so strange that it could slip past our natural defenses like a burglar using a key that is almost, but not quite, the same shape.

## Left-handed life, right-handed life

The secret is in something chemists call chirality — a fancy word for "handedness." Some molecules come in two forms that are perfect mirror images of each other, just like your left and right hands. They have exactly the same atoms connected in the same order, but they are arranged in opposite three-dimensional shapes.

On Earth, life consistently uses only one set of these shapes. But if scientists ever built a living organism using the opposite set — so-called "mirror life" — it could behave in unpredictable ways. Medicines might not work, or could even cause harm, because our bodies would not recognize the reversed chemistry.

## A light-based alarm system

Researchers from the University of Strathclyde and Durham University have proposed the first detection method for such a creature. Their approach relies on a type of light called circularly polarized luminescence, or CPL.

Light can vibrate in different planes — imagine a jump rope wiggling as it spins. When that wiggle rotates in one direction, it is "left-handed"; when it rotates the other way, it is "right-handed." Molecules that are mirror images of each other will produce CPL signals that spin in opposite directions.

This is the same basic idea used in 3D movie glasses: each lens only lets through light polarized in a specific direction, which is how your brain sees a separate picture for each eye. By studying how light spins, scientists could in principle tell whether a sample contains ordinary life or its mirror twin.

## A molecular fingerprint

To find mirror life in a biological sample, the researchers suggest using special fluorescent probes — molecules that glow when they attach to structures inside cells. Once bound, these probes would emit CPL light, creating a distinctive pattern.

Just as a human fingerprint is unique, each type of probe produces a signature spin pattern. If the spin rotates one way, it signals ordinary life. If it rotates the opposite way, it could flag a sample containing mirror-image biology.

The catch? A single probe might give misleading results. To be safe, the team recommends using several different probes that recognize different parts of a cell. By combining their signals, scientists could build a more reliable test — like checking multiple fingerprints before opening a door.

## Testing without creating

One of the best parts of this plan is that scientists could develop and refine it without ever creating mirror life in the first place. The fluorescent probes can be tested on ordinary organisms, and the equipment — plate readers, microscopes, and cameras — already exists in many labs.

The research was published in the journal Royal Society Open Science, under the title "Circularly Polarized Luminescence (CPL) as a Contingency Assay for 'Mirror Life' Bio-Hazards." The team stresses that mirror life is entirely hypothetical — their method is a precaution, not a response to an existing threat.

## Try this at home

Find a pair of 3D glasses (the kind from a cinema, or paper ones). Hold them up to a light and rotate one lens. At certain angles, the lenses will darken and block the light almost completely. This happens because the two lenses only allow light vibrating in matching directions to pass through — just like the CPL method uses light's "handedness" to tell molecules apart.

## Fun Fact

Some butterflies and beetles naturally produce their own circularly polarized light — their wings reflect it so that only certain insects can see them clearly. Nature itself uses the same kind of light trick the researchers hope to harness for mirror-life detection.

## for-grown-ups
Mirror life is considered a low-probability but high-impact biosecurity concern. International bodies including the UN Secretary General's Scientific Advisory Board have discussed safeguards. The proposed CPL-based assay could be adapted to different lab equipment, from cell plate readers to microscopes, making it flexible for screening in multiple environments. No mirror organisms have been created; the method remains a research-stage contingency.
