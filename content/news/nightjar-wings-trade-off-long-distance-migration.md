Imagine a bird that flies from your hometown all the way to another continent — every single year. The European nightjar makes a journey of thousands of miles between Europe and the shores of southern Africa. But here is the twist: the very wings that carry it across oceans are not built for speed. They are built for **slowness**.

## The great migration

Each autumn, European nightjars take to the night sky and begin their journey south. They fly thousands of kilometers — from England all the way to the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where they spend the winter. That is a trip that would take you across many, many countries.

But nightjars are not like eagles or swallows, built for fast, steady flight. They are **nighttime hunters**, swooping down to catch moths and beetles while flying low over fields and forests. So their wings had to solve a puzzle: how do you carry a bird across continents AND help it catch dinner at the same time?

## The wind-tunnel test

Scientists at Lund University in Sweden studied nightjars in a special wind tunnel, filming them as they flew at different speeds. They measured the airflow patterns that swirled off the birds' wings.

Here is what they found. Nightjars have **broad wing tips** — like little paddles at the end of each wing. At slow speeds, those broad tips are a superpower: they let the bird hover, turn on a dime, and swoop down on insects hiding in the dark.

But when nightjars fly fast, those same broad tips start working against them. The lift they generate becomes less efficient, and the bird has to work harder to stay in the air. It is like driving a car with the parking brake gently on — you can still go, but it costs more energy.

## The upstroke secret

There was one more surprise. As a nightjar lifts its wings on the way up, it does not just let them float back down. Instead, the bird **actively pushes** to generate forward thrust on every upstroke.

That trick was known in bats and insects, but never before seen in a bird like the nightjar. It means each wingbeat — both the down and the up — helps push the bird forward. For a long-distance traveler, that extra push matters.

## What scientists are learning

The nightjar's wings are a perfect example of how nature balances trade-offs. Evolution did not make a single perfect wing for one job. Instead, the nightjar's wings are a **compromise** — good enough for crossing continents, good enough for hunting insects, good enough for surviving.

That compromise could even inspire new flying machines. Engineers building tiny drones with flapping wings face the same challenge: how do you fly long distances AND maneuver through tight spaces? Nightjars cracked that code millions of years ago.

## Try this at home

Watch for nightjars on a warm evening near fields or woodlands. Stand quietly and listen for their calls — a weird, wailing "booming" sound the males make to attract mates. If you see one swooping low, remember: that broad wing is built for slow, careful hunting, not speed.

**Fun Fact:** Nightjars get their name from the old belief that they "jar" or disturb other birds at night — but really, they are gentle, silent fliers that spend their days hidden in trees and their nights hunting insects.

## Quick recap

1. European nightjars migrate thousands of miles between Europe and Africa each year.
2. Their broad wings sacrifice speed efficiency for slow-flight agility and insect hunting.
3. They generate forward thrust even on the upstroke of each wingbeat.
4. This evolutionary compromise could inspire better drones and flapping-wing aircraft.
