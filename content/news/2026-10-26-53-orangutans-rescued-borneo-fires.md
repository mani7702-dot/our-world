# How Did 53 Orangutans Find Safety During Borneo's Fiery Season?

## Into the smoke

When forest and peatland fires sweep across Borneo, the thick, smoky air can travel for hundreds of miles. But even in the worst conditions, one team never stops looking for the island's most vulnerable residents.

Since August, an 18-year-old Indonesian nonprofit called **YIARI** (Yaak Indonesia Aman Lestari) has been racing into fire-affected areas across Borneo. Their mission? To rescue orangutans — the gentle, tree-dwelling great apes that have no fast way to escape encroaching flames.

So far this fire season, they've saved **53 orangutans**, including several mothers with babies clinging to their bellies.

## A tiny survivor named Irene

One photo tells the whole story: Dr. Karmele Llano Sanchez, YIARI's CEO, cradling a tiny, underweight baby orangutan she named Irene.

"Baby Irene is just one tiny example of their hard work," said Gavin Bruce, CEO of International Animal Rescue — a partner organization. "When you see their movements and facial expressions, you realize how similar we are."

Dr. Karmel says baby Irene looked stressed and very thin. "She may have gone for days without finding any food in this devastated forest."

That's the reality for Borneo's orangutans when fires sweep through. The island's peatland forests burn hot and fast, leaving no time for slow-moving animals to escape.

## From rescue to rewilding

But saving a life is just the beginning. Many of the survivors remain under veterinary care at YIARI's Rescue and Rehabilitation Centre, where caregivers work around the clock.

Once the orangutans are strong and healthy enough, they're **rewilded** — released into forest preserves across the island, like Gunung Palung National Park, five hours away by truck. There, they learn to fend for themselves and gradually rejoin wild populations.

A previous YIARI project proved that this works: rehabilitated orangutans released into an isolated forest section have started raising babies of their own, creating new wild populations.

## Why orangutans need our help

Orangutans are found only in Borneo and Sumatra — two islands in Southeast Asia. All three orangutan species are **critically endangered**, the highest risk level before extinction.

The biggest dangers they face are:

- **Forest fires** — often started to clear land, but they spread and destroy entire habitats
- **Habitat loss** — forests are cut down for palm oil farms and logging
- **Human-wildlife conflict** — orangutans may be hurt when they wander into farmland looking for food

## Try this at home

- **Think before you buy**: Look for "RSPO-certified" palm oil or products labeled "orangutan-safe" at the grocery store. Palm oil is in everything from chocolate to laundry detergent!
- **Support conservation**: Organizations like International Animal Rescue and the Orangutan Foundation work to protect forests and rehabilitate rescued animals.
- **Reduce, reuse, recycle**: Less waste means fewer forests cut down for resources.

## Fun Fact

A baby orangutan stays with its mother for **seven years** — longer than almost any other animal! During that time, the little one learns everything from how to swing between trees to which fruits taste best. It's why mother-calamity rescues are so important: without mom, baby orangutans depend entirely on human caregivers.

## for-grownups: The science behind the rescue

The Bornean orangutan (Pongo pygmaeus) is one of humanity's closest living relatives, sharing 97% of our DNA. Like all great apes, they have complex social bonds and remarkable intelligence — they use tools, recognize themselves in mirrors, and have distinct cultures passed down between families.

Borneo's peat swamp forests store massive amounts of carbon underground. When these forests burn, they release even more CO2 than the trees above ground. The fires also destroy the interconnected canopy bridges that orangutans use to move between feeding areas. During the dry season (typically June through October), windy conditions can cause spot fires to spread rapidly — sometimes jumping 100 meters or more.

The delayed seasonal rains (pushed from October into November due to climate patterns) mean the fires may continue burning longer than usual this year. That's why rescues like YIARI's are happening around the clock. The team is also asking for public donations to support their ongoing work at the rescue center.
