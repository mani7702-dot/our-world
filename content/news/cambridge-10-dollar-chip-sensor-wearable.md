# 1. How a $10 chip could turn your watch into a mini lab

## A speck of light that tells secrets

What if the watch on your wrist could check your blood sugar without a needle? What if your phone could sniff out whether that plastic bottle is recyclable, or tell if the juice in front of you is really orange?

Scientists at the **University of Cambridge** have built a tiny sensor — about the size of a fingernail — that does all this and more. And the best part? It costs about **$10**.

## How does it "see" without a lens?

Every object around you absorbs and reflects light in its own special way — like a fingerprint. **Plastic**, **coffee**, and **apple juice** each bounce light back differently. Scientists use instruments called **spectrometers** to read those light fingerprints, but the good ones are big, bulky, and expensive.

The Cambridge team took a completely different approach. Instead of splitting light into a rainbow like a prism, their device uses a mathematical idea called the **convolution theorem**. They lined up tiny optical parts that each repeat a simple pattern, then let the light run through them in sequence. By measuring how those patterns mix, the sensor can work backward and reconstruct the full light fingerprint — without the complicated machinery that usually makes spectrometers so big and costly.

## Testing it out: from snacks to skin

The researchers put their $10 chip through its paces. Here is what it did:

- **Identified materials**: It told the difference between plastics, medicines, coffee, flour, and tea — and got every single one right.
- **Measured liquids**: It checked how strong a solution was and beat the accuracy of commercial bench-top machines that cost thousands of dollars.
- **Checked health signs**: It read skin moisture, blood alcohol levels, and lactate through the skin — and even tracked **blood glucose** without a finger prick.

One participant wore the sensor for an extended period, and it kept measuring glucose accurately day after day. That could one day mean diabetics check their sugar with a patch on their arm instead of needles.

The sensor also shrugged off temperature swings — it worked perfectly from **-20°C to 80°C**. That matters because your wristwatch gets hot in the sun and cold in winter, but the sensor keeps working.

## Why tiny sensors matter

Big spectrometers sit in laboratories. They are powerful, but you can only use them in one place. A $10 sensor that fits on a chip could bring that same power everywhere:

- **Health**: Wearables that track hydration, alcohol, or glucose without needles.
- **Food**: A scanner at the grocery store that checks if produce is ripe or if packaged food is fresh.
- **Factories**: Sensors built into machines that watch quality 24/7 without stopping production.
- **Environment**: Handheld devices that test water or soil for pollution on the spot.

## Try this at home

Grab a clear glass and fill it with water. Add one drop of food coloring — that is your "sample." Now shine a flashlight through the glass and watch the colored light hit the wall behind it. The color you see is like the light fingerprint of your mixture.

Now add a few drops of milk to the water and try again. The light spreads differently! Real spectrometers do this same test but with hundreds of colors instead of just one — and they can tell exactly what is mixed in just by reading the light.

## Fun Fact

If you stretched out the tiny optical parts in this sensor in a line, they would add up to less than **2 centimeters** — smaller than a paperclip. Yet this little chip can perform measurements that used to require a whole laboratory bench!

**for-grownups:** Researchers at the University of Cambridge developed a convolutional spectrometer — a new type of optical sensor that uses the convolution theorem to analyze light. The device costs about $10, measures only centimeters across, and operates in the near-infrared range (1200–1700 nm). Unlike traditional mini-spectrometers that must choose between size and accuracy, this design uses repeating optical components (such as unbalanced Mach-Zehnder interferometers or micro-ring resonators) combined with fast Fourier transforms to recover light spectra with high precision. In tests, it identified plastics, pharmaceuticals, coffee, flour, and tea with 100% accuracy, and measured concentrations in solutions with detection accuracy surpassing commercial benchtop spectrometers. In healthcare demonstrations, it non-invasively measured skin moisture, blood alcohol, blood lactate, and blood glucose, and even tracked glucose levels over an extended period in a participant. The sensor operates reliably from -20°C to 80°C, making it suitable for real-world wearable devices. Published April 2026 in Nature Photonics (DOI: 10.1038/s41566-026-01891-6).
