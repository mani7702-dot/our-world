# Can an astronaut's heart survive the trip to Mars?

## A six-month heart check

Getting humans to Mars means spending months floating in a spacecraft where there is no "up" or "down." On Earth, the heart works hard every second to push blood upward against gravity. In space, that job suddenly becomes almost effortless — and the heart can shrink a little, just like the leg muscles astronauts have to keep exercising.

So doctors have wondered: **will an astronaut's heart bounce back from months of ease, and can it then handle the weak but steady pull of Martian gravity?** A new study watched 13 astronauts who each spent about six months living on the International Space Station, and the answer is encouraging.

## The daily two-hour workout that keeps hearts strong

The secret weapon is **exercise** — NASA's Exercise Countermeasures program asks every astronaut to work out for up to two hours each single day. Their routine mixes two kinds of training:

- **Endurance training** (30–45 min): makes the blood-pumping system stronger, the way jogging or brisk cycling does back home.
- **Resistance training** (60–75 min): keeps muscle and heart tissue from thinning out, the way weight-lifting does on Earth.

"The heart has to work differently in space," said Dr. James Thomas, a cardiologist who helped lead the study. "You start floating around, give a little tap with your foot, and you can sail over to say hello to a friend."

## Heart pictures from orbit

Here is the clever part. The astronauts learned to point an ultrasound wand at their own chests and scan one another every few weeks, beaming the moving pictures back to Earth. At 3 a.m. each night, Dr. Thomas watched the live footage and guided them to get clearer views.

What the scans showed: the heart did wobble at first, as it adjusted to the new floaty home. But over the months, it settled into a careful new rhythm. And after they landed back on Earth, when the team tested the astronauts under **simulated Martian gravity** (about one-third of Earth's pull), the hearts showed no major trouble — no dangerous drops in blood pressure, no strain alarms.

## Why Mars still needs more than a strong heart

Martian gravity is *weaker* than Earth's but *stronger* than the zero-g of orbit. That middle strength is exactly what scientists are unsure about. The study is the first to ask the question directly: after six months of floating, can the heart handle that middle pull, day after day, while working on another planet?

So far the signs are good, which is one more hopeful checkmark for the long trip ahead.

## Try this at home

Stand barefoot on one leg with your eyes closed for 30 seconds — that wobbles the same balance system space messes with. Then march in place for two minutes, pumping your arms. Astronauts do hours of this each day so their hearts stay ready.

## Fun Fact

Astronauts' hearts are not the only thing that changes shape in space. Without gravity pulling them down, a sleeping astronaut can literally grow a couple of centimeters taller overnight — they shrink back by morning once they return to Earth.
