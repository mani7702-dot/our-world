# How Can a Cat's Pee Carry a Secret 'ID Card'?

## 1. Messages left behind

Cats live in a world of smells. A single sniff can tell a cat who has been nearby, how long ago they passed, and whether they were a friend, a stranger, or a rival. Cats even leave messages of their own by spraying urine on fences, trees, and walls.

But this raises a tricky puzzle. A scent mark stays behind long after its owner walks away — yet the chemicals inside it slowly evaporate and change over time. So how can a cat still recognise exactly *who* left the mark? A team of scientists may have cracked the code.

## 2. A personal recipe of 13 molecules

Researchers from Japan, Germany, and Spain, led by Iwate University, studied domestic cats up close. First, they checked whether cats really can tell one cat's urine from another's. Cats sniffed the same sample less and less each time it appeared — but perked right back up when a *different* cat's urine showed up. They even remembered the scents for months.

Next, the scientists followed a clue on the cats' own faces. When a cat smells something interesting, it pulls back its lip in a funny open-mouthed look called the **flehmen response**. Cats did this more often for unfamiliar urine than for their own.

Following that clue led the team to a special group of 13 fatty acids in cat urine. Each cat has its own mixture and amounts of these molecules — like a personal signature. The recipe stays fairly stable for the same cat over time, and cats that are related tend to have more similar recipes.

## 3. A century-old kidney mystery

Here is the surprise: the scientists found these molecules in the **kidney**, inside little storage droplets, but not in other body parts they checked. Those kidney droplets have puzzled scientists for more than 100 years.

The team thinks the droplets may act like a reservoir that keeps a cat's scent signature steady, even when its dinner or health changes. The same chemistry turned up in lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, and lynxes too — though each species has its own version.

## Fun Fact

The 13 fatty acids in cat urine had never been reported before in any mammal's secretions. They evaporate much more slowly than ordinary smelly urine chemicals — so a cat's "calling card" can stay readable for at least a day.

## Try this at home

Watch your own cat sniff something new. If it freezes, opens its mouth slightly, and curls back its top lip, that's the flehmen response — your cat is pulling scent molecules across a special organ in the roof of its mouth to read them more closely. Try noticing *which* smells trigger it most.
