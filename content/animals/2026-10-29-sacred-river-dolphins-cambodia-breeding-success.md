# Why Are Sacred River Dolphins in Cambodia Having More Babies?

## A Baby Boom on the Mekong River

Deep in the jungles of southeast Asia, along a quiet stretch of the **Mekong River in Cambodia**, something wonderful is happening. After years of worrying news, the river's most special residents — the **Irrawaddy dolphins** — are having a baby boom!

The most recent calving season brought **six brand-new dolphin calves** into the world. That might not sound like a lot, but for one of Earth's rarest animals, it is fantastic news. These six tiny dolphins bring the total number of Irrawaddy dolphins living in this 75-mile stretch of the Mekong to **118 individuals**. And that number is growing — a sign that conservation efforts are working!

## Meet Cambodia's Sacred Swimmers

The Irrawaddy dolphin (*Orcaella brevirostris*) is not your typical ocean dolphin. It lives in rivers and looks a bit different — it has a rounded head with no beak, and it loves to swim in the fresh waters of Southeast Asia.

In Cambodia, these dolphins are considered **sacred**. The people who live along the Mekong believe the dolphins are reincarnations of their ancestors. Some say the dolphins have even saved villagers from crocodile attacks and dangerous waters. Because of these beliefs, local communities have protected the dolphins for centuries — and today, Cambodia has the world's **largest remaining group** of Irrawaddy dolphins left in a river.

But the dolphins face big dangers:
- **Fishing nets** that drown dolphins when they get tangled
- **Dams** that change the river and block the dolphins' path (there are 160 dams on the Mekong!)
- **Boat traffic** that can injure them or disturb their rest

## The Good News: Conservation Is Working

Since 2014, the **World Wildlife Fund in Cambodia** has been working with local communities to protect the Mekong dolphins. They help people find new ways to make a living that do not harm the river — and they teach sustainable fishing practices that keep dolphins safe.

The fact that six calves were born last season tells scientists something very important: **when people and dolphins work together, nature can bounce back.** Each new baby dolphin represents hope — and each calf born in the wild is one more chance for this special species to thrive.

## Fun Fact

A baby dolphin is called a **calf** — and when it is born, it is already about the size of a big dog! Irrawaddy dolphins use echolocation (that is, they make clicks and listen to the echoes) to find food in the murky river water, just like bats use sonar to fly in the dark.

**for-grownups:** The Irrawaddy dolphin (*Orcaella brevirostris*) is a euryhaline species of oceanic dolphin found in scattered subpopulations in the Bay of Bengal and Southeast Asia. It is classified as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List. In the Mekong River of Cambodia, the population has grown to 118 individuals following 6 successful births in the most recent calving season. Major threats include gillnet fishing bycatch, dam construction (160 dams on the Mekong), and boat strikes. Conservation efforts led by WWF Cambodia since 2014 focus on community livelihood diversification and sustainable fishing practices. In Cambodian and Lao Buddhist tradition and folk belief, Irrawaddy dolphins are considered sacred — reincarnations of ancestors — which has fostered centuries of coexistence. The population's growth indicates that community-based conservation and reduced fishing pressure can support recovery of this critically endangered species.
