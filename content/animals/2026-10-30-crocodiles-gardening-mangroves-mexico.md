# How Do Crocodiles Help Plant a Forest? The Aztec Trick Bringing Mangroves Back to Mexico

## 1. A forest that was cut and burned

Mangroves are tangled coastal forests that grow where rivers meet the sea. Their roots shelter baby fish, protect shorelines from storms, and store huge amounts of carbon. But around the Alvarado Lagoons in Veracruz, Mexico, people cut and burned mangroves to make room for cattle. When the trees were gone, the soil sank and collapsed — and new mangroves could no longer take root.

How do you rebuild a forest when the ground itself has given up?

## 2. An idea borrowed from the Aztecs

Biologist Aníbal Ramírez Soto first tried planting mangrove seedlings in neat rows, like corn. They died. Then, sitting high in a healthy mangrove to escape the view of another failed planting, he noticed something: the surviving mangroves grew in raised clumps.

So he scooped up wetland soil and built a small mound in the shallow water — a tiny island. It worked. The Aztecs once farmed exactly this way around their capital city, on raised plots they called *chinampas*, and Ramírez Soto borrowed the name. "It's a simple idea: Just put the soil back like it was," he told Mongabay.

The seedlings survived. The team planted about 2,000 trees into hundreds of hand-built mounds. When they came back to count, more than 4,000 had taken root — extra seedlings had floated in on the tide and rooted themselves. The forest was healing on its own. By the eighth year, the leafy canopy covered 80% of the area.

## 3. Bringing back the gardeners

A healthy mangrove needs more than trees — it needs animals. Ramírez Soto's team believes crocodiles help by pushing sediment around as they swim and keeping water channels open, so the forest isn't "clogged like veins." The lagoons had lost their crocodiles to hunting, so since 2017 the team has released around 80 Morelet's crocodiles back into the water.

Their inspiration came from Colombia's Cispatá Bay, where biologist Giovanni Ulloa Delgado spent 15 years releasing roughly 15,000 American crocodiles. Today the bay's mangroves stay healthy and the local community runs a tourism business built around the reptiles.

Not everyone is convinced yet. Crocodile expert Charlie Manolis points out that the research is thin, and that northern Australia's mangroves stayed fine for decades without crocodiles. He supports protecting crocodiles — but says big predators also need careful, clear safety education for nearby communities.

## 4. Try this at home

You can test the *chinampa* idea in your own garden or a classroom pot:
- Fill a small tray with damp soil and pack it into a low mound instead of a flat layer.
- Plant a bean or grass seed on top of the mound and another on flat soil beside it.
- Water both the same way for two weeks and compare which grows taller.

Raised soil drains and breathes differently — the same reason Ramírez Soto's mounds worked in the marsh.

## Fun Fact

A group of crocodiles is sometimes called a "float" or a "bask." And crocodile mothers are surprisingly gentle: after hatching, they carry their babies in their jaws to the water — the same jaws that can crush a wildebeest.
