"How did a lost volcanic chain help the ancient Tethys Ocean disappear?"

By Our World Published 28 October 2026 at 11:15 IST
Reading options

"Scientists have discovered a missing 100-million-year-old volcanic chain buried beneath northwestern Iran — a 'smoking gun' in the geological record of the ancient Tethys Ocean that helps explain how that vast prehistoric sea gradually vanished from the map."

How did a lost volcanic chain help the ancient Tethys Ocean disappear?

Buried beneath the mountains of northwestern Iran

Imagine an ocean so vast that it covered much of what we now call the Middle East. Now imagine that ocean slowly disappearing — not in a single catastrophe, but over millions of years, as the ground beneath it rose, folded, and turned to stone.

Around 100 million years ago, a chain of volcanoes erupted along the edge of a continent in what is today northwestern Iran. These volcanoes were part of a system that formed as one tectonic plate sank beneath another — like a giant underwater mountain range that never quite reached the surface. But over time, the mountains were torn apart by earthquakes, buried under layers of sand and rock, and hidden by later eruptions. Today, only careful detective work with tiny mineral crystals can reveal that they ever existed.

Reading Earth's diary in zircon crystals

The researchers needed a way to read the story locked in these rocks — even after millions of years of geological upheaval. Their tool of choice? Zircon crystals.

Zircons are tiny mineral grains that form in magma and are incredibly tough. They can survive erosion, burial, and even being cooked by later volcanic activity without losing their original chemical signature. It's like finding a message in a bottle that has survived shipwreck after shipwreck.

By firing a laser at individual zircons and measuring the radioactive decay of uranium into lead, the team could work out the exact age of each crystal. Some zircons revealed ages of 97 to 101 million years — confirming that a volcanic arc was active here during the Late Cretaceous period. Additional analysis of hafnium isotopes showed that the magma had been stirred by Earth's crust on its journey upward, exactly what you would expect from a continental volcanic arc.

The ancient ocean that became the Persian Gulf

But why does a lost volcano chain tell us about a lost ocean? The answer lies in the grand dance of Earth's tectonic plates.

The volcanic arc in Iran grew along the edge of the Eurasian Plate, where the oceanic plate carrying the floor of the Neo-Tethys Ocean was slowly sinking into Earth's mantle. For tens of millions of years, this subduction zone produced volcanic activity, mountain-building, and the gradual squeezing of the ocean basin. As the plates continued to move, the Neo-Tethys shrank, fragmented, and eventually vanished — leaving behind the Persian Gulf, the Zagros Mountains, and scattered clues in the rock record.

This newly discovered volcanic chain is like a missing piece in a very old, very large puzzle. It helps scientists reconstruct how the Tethys Ocean system evolved and how the modern Middle East was assembled from the pieces of a vanished world.

Try this at home

You can simulate subduction (one plate diving beneath another) with two slabs of chocolate digestive biscuits and a bit of soft butter — one biscuit represents the oceanic plate, the other the continental plate. Push the butter-side-down biscuit under the other; the top biscuit will crumple upward just like mountain ranges form above a subduction zone, while the "oceanic" plate sinks.

Fun Fact

The word "Tethys" comes from Greek mythology — Tethys was a Titan goddess of the oceans. Today her name lives on in the Tethys Sea, a prehistoric ocean, and even in Tethys, a moon of Saturn that scientists have studied for signs of underground water.

For grown-ups

"An international team of researchers led by Istanbul Technical University has identified a previously unknown volcanic system in northwestern Iran, named the Azerbaijan Continental Magmatic Arc. Dating back roughly 97 to 101 million years (Late Cretaceous period), the arc formed along the active continental margin where the ancient Neo-Tethys Ocean's oceanic plate was subducting beneath the Eurasian continental plate. The team used zircon U-Pb geochronology to determine rock ages, Hf isotope analysis to trace their origins, and whole-rock geochemistry to confirm signatures of continental arc magmatism. The volcanic system was subsequently obscured by later tectonic deformation, erosion, burial beneath sedimentary basins, and a younger Eocene volcanic episode. The Neo-Tethys Ocean was a vast body of water that, beginning in the early Mesozoic, separated the ancient continents of Gondwana and Laurasia. Over the Cretaceous and early Cenozoic, the Indian, African, Australian, and Arabian plates moved northward, colliding with Eurasia and closing the Tethys Ocean. The remnants of this long-vanished ocean today form the Indian Ocean, the Mediterranean Sea, and the Black and Caspian Seas. This study, published in Geoscience Frontiers (DOI: 10.1016/j.gsf.2025.102205), adds a crucial missing piece to our understanding of how the Tethys Ocean disappeared and how the Zagros and Caucasus Mountains formed."
