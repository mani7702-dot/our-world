How can DNA testing help save England's rarest tree?

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 12:17 IST
Reading options

Conservationists are using DNA testing to help bring back the black poplar — one of the UK's rarest native trees — along the Thames Valley.

How can DNA testing help save England's rarest tree?

1. The tree that is almost gone

Close your eyes and imagine a tree with a twisted, dramatic trunk — the kind that looks like it stepped right out of a fantasy movie. That is the black poplar, one of the UK's rarest native trees.

For hundreds of years, black poplars lined the rivers and floodplains of England. Their roots held the soil steady, and their wide leaves whispered in the wind. But today, conservation groups say there are only about 50 left in Oxfordshire alone. Decades of tree-felling, draining of wetlands, and changes to the land have made these trees incredibly rare.

2. Reading the trees' family secrets

How do you save a tree that is almost gone? You start by reading its DNA!

A project called the Swindon to Oxford: Regenerating the Thames and Tributaries is working with conservation groups to help the black poplar make a comeback. Scientists collect tiny samples from the remaining trees and run DNA tests. This tells them which trees are related to each other and which ones have the strongest, healthiest genes.

From the strongest trees, they take cuttings — small pieces of branch that can grow into brand-new trees. It is like making a photocopy of the best, most resilient family members. Then they plant these baby trees in the right spots along the River Thames and its flood plains.

3. Trees that protect our towns

Black poplars are not just pretty — they are nature's bodyguards.

When they grow in thick lines along rivers, their roots act like sponges. They soak up extra water after heavy rain and hold the soil in place. This helps prevent flooding in nearby towns and villages. Think of them as living flood barriers!

They also home a whole neighborhood of wildlife. The poplar hornet moth — yes, a real moth that visits flowers — depends on black poplars to lay its eggs. Birds, beetles, and butterflies all share these trees too. Saving one tree saves many.

Try this at home

Next time you walk near a river or pond, look for poplar trees. Can you spot the jagged, diamond-shaped leaves that tremble in the breeze? Try sketching one and labeling its parts. If you live near a nature reserve, ask a grown-up if you can volunteer to help plant young trees — many wildlife groups welcome junior helpers!

Fun Fact

The black poplar is so good at surviving tough conditions that some trees can live for over 300 years. That means a single tree might have been a tiny seedling when your great-great-grandparents were born!

For grown-ups

Black poplars (Populus nigra) are the type species of section Aigeiros of the genus Populus, native to Europe. They are among England's rarest native trees, with conservation groups warning that decades of felling, land drainage, and habitat loss have pushed them to the verge of extinction. The Swindon to Oxford: Regenerating the Thames and Tributaries nature recovery programme is working with the Long Mead Foundation and the Trust for Oxfordshire's Environment to regenerate the population along the River Thames and its flood plain using DNA analysis, propagation of cuttings, and replanting. Only 50 black poplars are currently recorded in Oxfordshire. The project is fundraising £20,000, with Oxfordshire County Council's Local Nature Recovery Strategy committed to match-funding up to £10,000. Black poplars play a role in reducing flood risk by lining rivers and floodplains and holding water back. They also support wildlife including the poplar hornet moth.
