How Did 53 Orangutans Get Rescued From Borneo's Fires?

A Daring Rescue Mission in the Land of the Red Apes

When forest fires sweep through the rainforests of Borneo, they don't just destroy trees — they put some of our closest animal relatives in serious danger. Since August 2026, a dedicated team of rescuers from an 18-year-old Indonesian nonprofit has saved 53 orangutans from the burning forests, giving them a second chance at life.

Orangutans are the gentle great apes that live only in the rainforests of Borneo and Sumatra. With their long reddish hair, big eyes, and incredibly long arms, they are the world's most tree-loving primates — spending nearly their entire lives swinging through the canopy. But when fires turn their forest home into a smoldering wasteland, these gentle giants need our help.

Who Are the Baby Orangutans?

One of the tiniest survivors is a baby orangutan named Irene. She was found looking very stressed and thin, having possibly gone days without enough food in the devastated forest. Dr. Karmele Llano Sanchez, CEO of the rescue team (YIARI), cradled baby Irene in her arms — a photo that shows just how similar baby orangutans look to human infants, with the same curious eyes and gentle expressions.

The International Animal Rescue, which partners with YIARI, says Irene and her fellow survivors are now under careful veterinary care at a special Rescue and Rehabilitation Centre. There, they receive food, medicine, and lots of tender care until they are strong enough to return to the wild.

Rewilding: Going Back to the Forest

Many of the rescued orangutans have already been rewilded — that's a special word meaning they were returned to forest preserves, including Gunung Palung National Park, one of Borneo's most important protected areas. The rewilding process is slow and careful: young orangutans learn from their mothers how to build nests, find fruit, and swing through the trees. Those who lost their mothers to the fires must learn these skills from the rescue team.

Why Are Orangutans in Danger?

All three orangutan species — the Bornean orangutan, the Sumatran orangutan, and the tiny Tapanuli orangutan (with fewer than 800 left!) — are listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN Red List. That means they are among the animals most at risk of disappearing forever.

The biggest threats are:

  • Forest fires (often started to clear land for palm oil plantations)
  • Deforestation for palm oil, logging, and mining
  • Poaching and the illegal pet trade (baby orangutans are stolen from their mothers)
  • Habitat fragmentation — when forests are chopped up, it's harder for orangutans to find mates and food

But thanks to rescuers like YIARI and the Gunung Palung rehabilitation teams, there is hope. Each rescued orangutan that returns to the forest is a victory — and each new baby born in the wild is a sign that the effort to save them is working.

Try This at Home

Build an orangutan nest! Orangutans build soft beds of branches and leaves every night in the treetops. You can make a mini version using a shoebox, strips of fabric or yarn, twigs, and leaves. Pretend you're an orangutan choosing the coziest spot high in the trees to spend the night!

Fun Fact

An adult orangutan can live over 30 years in the wild — one of the longest lifespans of any land mammal. Female orangutans are amazing moms: they care for their babies for up to eight years, longer than almost any other animal on Earth!

for-grownups: Orangutans (Pongo spp.) are critically endangered great apes endemic to Borneo and Sumatra. The Bornean orangutan (P. pygmaeus) had an estimated 104,700 individuals in the wild as of 2016. Major threats include habitat loss from palm oil expansion, logging, mining, and seasonal forest fires. The YIARI Foundation in Indonesian Borneo has been conducting rescue and rehabilitation since 2008, working with partners like International Animal Rescue to save orangutans from fires and release them into protected areas like Gunung Palung National Park.
