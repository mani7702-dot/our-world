"How Can a Tiny Particle Turn Invisible Light Into a Bright Green Glow?"

By Our World Published 2026-11-01T09:58:00+05:30 at 09:58 IST
Reading options

Imagine you could shine a light that our eyes cannot see onto a drop of water, and the drop answered back with a glowing green signal. That is exactly what scientists at the University of Toronto have built: a particle so small that thousands could sit on the tip of your hair, and it turns invisible light into a colour you can see.

A Light Trick That Goes the Wrong Way

Most glow-in-the-dark things work in one direction. They soak up strong, high-energy light, like ultraviolet, and give back weaker, low-energy light in a different colour. The new particles do the opposite. They absorb weak, low-energy light, called near-infrared, and release stronger, higher-energy light. Scientists call this upconversion.

The trick comes from two special metals, ytterbium and erbium, which belong to a family called the lanthanides. When near-infrared light hits the particle, the energy hops from ytterbium ions to erbium ions, and the erbium does the upconverting. The result? The particle glows a bright green.

Why Brighter Matters

Making the particles brighter was the hard part. Packing in lots of ytterbium helped capture more light, but it also stole the light back before it could escape, a problem called back-energy transfer. The team solved it by rebuilding the particle. Instead of a flat hexagon made of sodium, yttrium, and fluorine, they made a diamond-shaped particle with three layers, a dense core inside two shells, built from lithium, lutetium, and fluorine. Incoming light now flows in one direction, like a tunnel, toward the erbium.

The new particles glow about 150 times brighter than earlier upconversion particles that were not combined with dye. Even better, they can tell apart "twin" molecules that have the exact same atoms but arranged in a slightly different order.

Seeing the Stars in Daylight

So why bother? Bright signals are easier to spot. The lead author compares it to looking at the night sky. "The stars shine the same brightness all the time, but during the day the sun is so powerful that it overwhelms them," says Prof. Kai Huang. By shifting the incoming light to a lower energy, the background goes dark, "like turning off the sun, so you can see the stars better."

That super-sharp signal could help makers of medicine check whether a batch of a drug contains a slightly wrong version of a molecule, or help scientists hunt for tiny traces of pollution in water.

Try This at Home

Ask a grown-up for a white shirt and a blacklight (a UV torch). In a dark room, the shirt often glows bright blue-white because its special brighteners absorb invisible light and re-emit it as visible colour. You are watching energy change form, the same idea behind these tiny glowing particles, just in reverse.

Fun Fact

The team did not guess. They ran computer simulations to test dozens of particle shapes and recipes before making them in the lab, working right down to the level of single atoms.

For grown-ups

This piece covers a 2026 study from U of T Engineering published in the Journal of the American Chemical Society (DOI 10.1021/jacs.6c07018). Engineers led by Prof. Kai Huang and lead author Jiaze Wu built dye-sensitized upconversion nanoparticles with a lithium-lutetium-fluoride core-shell-shell design that emits roughly 150 times brighter than non-dye-sensitized versions. They absorb low-energy near-infrared photons and emit higher-energy green light, a trick called upconversion, which could help spot drug impurities and tiny water pollutants.
