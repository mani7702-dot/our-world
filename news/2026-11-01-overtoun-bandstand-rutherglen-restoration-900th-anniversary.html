"How Do You Rebuild a 112-Year-Old Bandstand From Old Photos?"

By Our World Published 01 November 2026 at 09:35 IST
Reading options

"A Victorian bandstand in Scotland burned down in 2024 — its wooden roof was gone, and the people who built it left almost no records behind. Here is how craftsmen solved a real-life jigsaw puzzle and brought it back for the town's 900th birthday."

Imagine a beautiful old bandstand — a little round pavilion in a park where brass bands once played on Sunday afternoons. This one, in the Scottish town of Rutherglen, stood for 112 years. Then, one night in June 2024, a fire tore through it. When the smoke cleared, the wooden roof and floor were gone.

So how do you rebuild something that old — when the people who made it are long gone, and their plans are missing?

The puzzle: no map, no instructions

The bandstand was built from cast iron by Glasgow's famous Saracen Foundry, a company that made fancy metalwork used all over the world, from palaces in Glasgow to bridges in Brazil and Australia. But the foundry closed back in the 1960s, and most of its records were thrown away.

That left the repair team with a real-life jigsaw puzzle. They had only three clues: pieces of the iron frame that survived the fire, a few old photographs, and rare old catalogue pages showing how such bandstands used to look. Craftsmen put the clues together, like detectives, to work out the exact shape the bandstand should be.

Why the iron mattered so much

Not everything was lost in the fire. The timber parts burned, but the cast-iron skeleton stayed standing. Iron does not burn like wood. Because the frame held its shape, the team could measure the original curves and decorations instead of guessing. Covanburn Contracts, the specialists who did the work, compared those surviving iron pieces with the photographs and catalogues until every swirl and curve matched.

They called it a "historical jigsaw puzzle" — and it took about 18 weeks of careful work to finish.

A birthday present for a 900-year-old town

The timing was special. Rutherglen is celebrating its 900th birthday. The town was made a Royal Burgh by King David I way back in 1126. The bandstand was unveiled on Halloween in time for the celebrations.

There is a brand-new touch, too. On the concrete base sits a mosaic, designed together with pupils from a nearby school. It shows the town's motto — letting children add their own hands to a 900-year story.

Try this at home: Pick an everyday object and try to draw it using only a photo and a single clue about how it was made. You will see why the bandstand team needed patience, teamwork and lots of comparing.

Fun Fact: Cast iron is poured as liquid metal into sand moulds, so it can be shaped into incredibly fine, curly patterns that wood could never hold — perfect for a fancy Victorian bandstand.

For grown-ups

"Overtoun Bandstand in Overtoun Park, Rutherglen, South Lanarkshire, is a category B-listed Victorian cast-iron structure dating to about 1913 (112 years old). It was badly damaged by a fire in June 2024 that Police Scotland treated as deliberate; the timber elements burned away but the cast-iron frame and roof survived well enough to allow restoration. In 2026 South Lanarkshire Council appointed heritage restoration specialists Covanburn Contracts; managing director Alex Stewart said the work took about 18 weeks and required piecing together the original design from surviving ironwork, old photographs and rare foundry catalogues, because the original maker — Glasgow's Saracen Foundry (W MacFarlane & Co., Possilpark) — closed in the 1960s and many of its records were lost. The bandstand was unveiled on Halloween 2026, in time for Rutherglen's 900th-anniversary celebrations (the town was made a Royal Burgh by King David I in 1126). A new mosaic designed with pupils from nearby Stonelaw High School was added to the base, featuring the town's motto.
