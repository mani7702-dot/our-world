Why does Western Australia have a giant spider-shaped crater visible from space?

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 20:39 IST
Reading options

A giant ancient impact crater in Western Australia looks like a spider when seen from space — and new satellite photos show its eight legs spreading across the red earth.

Why does Western Australia have a giant spider-shaped crater visible from space?

A spider hidden in the red earth

Somewhere in the vast Kimberley region of northern Western Australia, a giant spider is frozen in stone. But this is no living creature — it is an ancient impact crater, and its eight legs are made of hard rock, not flesh and bone.

From your kitchen table you would never know it is there. But from space, the Landsat 9 satellite can see all eight ridges fanning out like a spider's legs. That is why geologists call it Spider Crater.

What happened when the sky fell

Between 600 and 900 million years ago, a chunk of space rock slammed into this spot at incredible speed. The force of the impact was so great that the ground bounced back upward in the center — like a drumhead pushed from below. That rebound created a central dome of rock, while the outer rim collapsed and shattered.

Over the hundreds of millions of years since, wind and water have been carving away at the landscape. The softer rock wore down faster, but the tough quartzite ridges stayed tall — slowly spreading outward like the legs of a spider, exactly as they appear in satellite photos today.

The clue that gave it away

For decades geologists were puzzled. The circular shape in satellite images screamed "impact crater," but there was one key piece of evidence missing: shatter cones. These are cone-shaped patterns in rock that form only under the extreme pressure of a meteorite impact.

In the late 1970s, geologists finally found shatter cones right at the center. That confirmed Spider Crater was not just any old geological oddity — it was the deeply eroded remnant of a massive space rock collision.

Seeing it from orbit

The image above was captured by the Landsat 9 satellite, a joint mission between NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey. Launched in September 2021, Landsat 9 carries an Operational Land Imager that snaps photos of the entire Earth's surface every 16 days. Each week it photographs the whole planet — and sometimes it finds something that looks a lot like a cosmic spider.

Try this at home: On a clear night, step outside with an adult and look for the brightest patch of stars you can see. Every one of those dots is a sun — and some of them have planets that may one day reveal their own impact scars. You can also explore Spider Crater (and hundreds of other impact sites) on Google Earth by searching for "Marble Bar, Western Australia."

Fun Fact

Spider Crater is one of only about 190 confirmed impact craters on Earth. That might sound like a lot, but our planet is 4.5 billion years old — if every impact since day one had been preserved, the surface would look like the Moon, covered in craters. Instead, Earth's dynamic surface of erosion, volcanoes, and shifting tectonic plates erases most scars over time. Spider Crater has survived because its quartzite ridges are simply that tough.

For the grown-ups

Spider Crater is a deeply eroded impact structure in the Kimberley region of northern Western Australia. It formed 600–900 million years ago (Neoproterozoic era) when a meteorite impact created a central rebound uplift surrounded by an 11 × 13 km (8 × 7 mile) disturbed zone. The spider-like radiating ridges of quartzite were carved by erosion after the crater floor rebounded following the initial impact. Shatter cones discovered in the late 1970s confirmed the impact origin. The visible image was captured by the Operational Land Imager (OLI) aboard Landsat 9, a NASA/USGS Earthobservatory satellite launched in September 2021 that continues the 50-year Landsat record of planetary change.

For grown-ups

The short version for curious grown-ups: Spider Crater is a deeply eroded impact structure in the Kimberley region of northern Western Australia, 18 km east of the Mount Barnett Roadhouse on the Gibb River Road. It formed when a meteor slammed into Earth between 600 and 900 million years ago during the Neoproterozoic era. The distinctive spider-like radiating ridges of quartzite were carved by erosion after the impact floor rebounded upward and then gradually wore down over hundreds of millions of years. The structure is 11 x 13 km across (8 x 7 miles) with a central uplift roughly 4 km (2.5 miles) wide. Shatter cones found in the late 1970s confirmed its impact origin. The image was captured by the Operational Land Imager (OLI) aboard NASA/USGS Landsat 9, launched in September 2021.
