"How Did AI Help Scientists Count Millions of Bat Calls?"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 09:15 IST
Reading options

"Scientists recorded 98 million bat calls across 310 British woodlands over three years. Without AI, sorting through all that data would have taken more than 30 years — but smart computer tools did it in a flash, helping find some of the rarest bats in the UK."

How did AI help scientists count millions of bat calls?

The great bat-sound detective story

Imagine you are a bat, flying silently through a dark forest. You send out thousands of tiny sound pulses every second — too high for human ears to hear — and listen for the echoes that bounce back off trees, insects, and your friends. This is called echolocation, and it helps bats navigate and hunt in the dark.

Now imagine trying to listen to 98 million of those calls. That is exactly what a team of scientists in the United Kingdom ended up with — and it would have taken a single person more than 30 years to sort through them all by hand. So the researchers did something clever: they let a computer do it for them.

Step 1: 310 woodlands, three years, one giant recording

Scientists from University College London (UCL) and the Bat Conservation Trust placed special recording devices on poles in 310 different woodlands across Britain. These little devices sat quietly among the trees for three full years, capturing every bat squeak, chirp, and click they could detect.

The result? Over 90,000 hours of audio — and roughly 98 million bat calls that needed to be identified and counted.

Step 2: Why a human ear is not enough

Bats do not meep like ghosts in cartoons. Each of the hundreds of bat species makes its own unique sound pattern — some chirp, some buzz, some sweep in frequency like a violin slide. Telling them apart by listening is hard enough for one call, but doing it for 98 million calls is almost impossible without a super-fast helper.

As Dr. Kate Jones from UCL explained, "AI has fundamentally changed the scale at which we can monitor wildlife." What used to be a job that would take decades was finished in the blink of an eye — all thanks to open-source AI tools that can recognize each species' "voice."

Step 3: Finding the rarest neighbors

One of the most exciting discoveries from the survey was the Barbastelle — a bat so rare that it is on the UK's official Red List of threatened species. Its name comes from Latin words meaning "star-beard," because of the tiny bristles around its nose that look like a star-shaped beard.

The Barbastelle usually roams deep, ancient forests, and scientists rarely see it. But AI helped spot where it was calling the most: concentrated in the south-west of England, especially in Somerset, Devon, Cornwall, and Dorset.

"We were looking at huge volumes of data and 90,000 hours of recording — there was no way we would be able to do this manually," said Lia Gilmour from the Bat Conservation Trust. The project, she said, was "unprecedented."

Why this matters for bats — and for us

Bats might seem small and spooky, but they are actually incredibly important. A single little brown bat can gobble up 1,000 insects in one hour — including mosquitoes and crop pests. That means healthier forests, fewer bug bites, and better food growing in farmers' fields.

But many bat populations are in trouble from habitat loss, disease, and climate change. Surveys like this one help scientists understand where bats live, where they are struggling, and where to focus conservation efforts. And thanks to AI, they can now scan millions of calls in minutes instead of decades.

Try this at home

You can be a bat-sound detective yourself!

  1. Go outside at dusk (early evening is when bats wake up).
  2. Turn on your phone's voice recorder and stand quietly near a tree for five minutes.
  3. Listen back with headphones — if a bat flies by, you might catch a faint, high-pitched

chirp that your ear almost misses.

  1. To hear what you actually caught, use a free app like Bat Detective or Kaleidoscope

that shifts the ultra-high bat calls down to a range humans can hear.

Remember: never try to touch a bat. If you find one on the ground, it may need help from a wildlife expert — tell an adult to call a local bat rehabilitator.

Fun Fact

There are more than 1,400 bat species in the world — about one out of every five mammal species on Earth! The smallest bat is tinier than your thumb, while the largest (called flying foxes) have a wingspan wider than a child is tall.
