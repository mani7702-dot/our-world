"Glowing Salt Crystals Tracked Radiation Aboard Artemis I's Moon Trip"

By Our World Published 29 October 2026 at 05:29 IST
Reading options

"Before any humans ride along on an Artemis mission, NASA needs to know exactly how much dangerous radiation waits in deep space. On the uncrewed Artemis I flight around the Moon, scientists tested a clever new kind of detector: tiny salt crystals that light up like glow-stick trails when cosmic rays pass through them."

1. How Do You Catch Invisible Rays from Across the Galaxy?

2. Tiny glowing crystals keep count of every hit

When NASA launched Artemis I around the Moon in November 2022, there were no astronauts inside the capsule — just in case anything went wrong. But two human-shaped mannequins named Zohar and Helga were strapped in, ready to do something very human: feel the effects of space radiation.

Deep space is filled with a roaring storm of atomic nuclei — leftover pieces of exploded stars, the Sun's own solar wind, and particles from distant galaxies whose origins scientists are still guessing at. These tiny bullets travel near the speed of light. Earth's magnetic field stops most of them, but once you sail beyond the Moon, you are exposed to the full blast.

To measure that exposure, researchers placed special detectors on the mannequins. Most are made of plastic sheets that record etches from each particle — like a photographic negative for radiation. But plastic has limits when it comes to catching the heaviest, highest-energy ions, which can blur together in the tracks.

That is where the salt crystals come in.

3. Why salt? Because it lights up like a galaxy of tiny stars

The crystals are made of lithium fluoride — the same family of salts you might sprinkle on a snake bite (although this kind is far too small to see without a microscope). Each crystal is only a few millimeters across, but it acts like a tiny telescope for invisible rays.

Here is the trick: when a cosmic-ray nucleus smashes through the crystal lattice, it knocks loose some electrons and leaves behind a "color center" — a tiny defect that glows an electric blue when a laser is swept across it. Scientists at the Institute for Nuclear Physics in Poland (IFJ PAN) then scan thousands of microscope images, layer by paper-thin layer, and trace every glowing trail back to its origin.

Think of it like a snow globe. The crystal is the glass, the cosmic rays are invisible fingers stirring the snow, and the glow-in-the-dark trails are the only evidence that something brushed past. By counting and measuring those trails, the team can tell how strong the radiation was, where it came from, and how dangerous it might be to a real astronaut's body.

The best part? Unlike plastic detectors, lithium fluoride can be scanned over and over without using chemicals or heat. That means faster results and more detail for missions that last months at a time.

4. A small mismatch — and how scientists fixed it

When the team compared their glowing crystals to the traditional plastic detectors placed side-by-side on the Zohar mannequin, something surprising happened. For medium-energy radiation, the readings matched almost perfectly. But for the very highest-energy particles — the ones that pack the most biological punch — the crystals overestimated the dose by about 30%.

Why? The scientists think stray secondary neutrons were the culprits. Sometimes a high-speed neutron slams into a lithium atom inside the crystal and knocks it out of place, creating its own glowing trail that looks just like a cosmic ray hit. The team figured out that swapping in lithium-7 — a special isotope that does not react as easily — sharpens the signal and quiets the false alarms.

Even with that mismatch, the experiment was a major step forward. Lithium fluoride crystals may one day ride along on the first crewed Artemis missions, giving astronauts an early warning system and helping doctors predict their long-term health risks after a journey to the Moon — and eventually, to Mars.

Try this at home

You can build a glow-in-the-dark detector of your own! Take a small flashlight and a clear glass or jar of water. Shine the light through the water in a dark room and sprinkle in a pinch of tonic water (which contains quinine, a fluorescent compound). Now turn off the main light and look at the water from the side — you should see a faint blue glow! That is the same basic idea: a material that absorbs invisible energy and re-emits it as light. Real space scientists use salt crystals instead of tonic water, but the glow is just as magical.

Fun Fact

The name "Artemis" comes from the Greek goddess of the Moon — but NASA's choice was also fitting because Artemis I was the first mission to use the new Space Launch System (SLS) rocket, the most powerful American rocket ever built. The same crystals that tracked radiation on that first flight are descendants of detectors first tested on the International Space Station during the 1990s — meaning these glowing salts have been on a long journey from Earth's orbit to the Moon and back, learning lessons along the way.

For grown-ups

"Artemis I (launched November 2022) carried the Matroshka AstroRad Radiation Experiment (MARE) — two anthropomorphic mannequins named Zohar and Helga representing crew. To measure cosmic radiation that traditional plastic detectors struggle to fully capture, researchers used millimeter-sized lithium fluoride (LiF) crystals as fluorescent nuclear track detectors. When high-energy galactic cosmic ray nuclei pass through the crystal lattice, they knock electrons loose and create persistent 'color centers' that glow blue under a laser. These tracks were scanned by IFJ PAN scientist Paweł Bilski and team and published in Radiation Measurements (2026). The crystals matched plastic detectors for medium-energy particles but overestimated absorbed dose by ~30% at very high energies because secondary neutrons striking lithium nuclei create their own spurious glow tracks; the team used lithium-7 isotopologues to suppress this effect. The technique is a step toward better real-time passive dosimeters for crewed lunar and Mars missions."
