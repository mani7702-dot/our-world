How did one cell become a whole body — and can we read its secret diary?

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 11:26 IST
Reading options

"Scientists used a DNA 'typewriter' to trace every cell in a developing mouse embryo back to its very first division. The result is a detailed family tree showing how cells choose their jobs — and why some turn into brain while others become skin."

A cell's family tree written in DNA

Imagine if every time a cell in your body split in two, it quietly typed a tiny note into its own genetic notebook — a mark that gets passed down to every future copy. By the time a baby mouse was ready to be born, that notebook would contain a complete family tree showing which cell became which, all the way back to the very first split.

That is exactly what a team of scientists from the University of Washington and Dartmouth College has done. They built a "DNA Typewriter" — a molecular machine that writes a record directly into a cell's DNA as it divides. Then they read it back, cell by cell, to trace the entire family history of a developing mouse embryo.

The first division that matters most

The recording covered the first 13.5 days of embryo growth — that is, the time from a single fertilized egg to a tiny bundle of cells that will soon grow into all the body's organs. (Baby mice are born after about 19 to 21 days.)

One of the most striking discoveries came from the very first split: the fertilized egg divided into two cells, and the DNA Typewriter captured a clear, permanent mark on each. That let the researchers trace nearly every cell they studied back to one founding cell or the other.

One of the two original cells went on to produce more descendants than its partner — a bit like one sibling having more children in a family tree. But both cells ultimately gave rise to a remarkably similar mix of different cell types, showing that even at the very start, each cell was already "allowed" to become anything.

Cells pick their jobs on their own schedule

As the embryo grew, its cells did not all rush to pick a job at the same time. Some cell types committed early — blood cells and retina cells settled on their fates first. Others, like the outer layer of skin, stayed flexible for longer before finally deciding what to become.

This is like a classroom where some students raise their hands on the very first question, while others wait until the final lesson to pick their favorite subject. The DNA Typewriter revealed that the timing is built into each cell type itself — not a single, grand switch that flips for the whole embryo at once.

What this could mean

Understanding how cells learn who they are could help explain what goes wrong when bodies develop — things like birth defects, and someday maybe even guide how we grow new tissues in a lab. The lead scientist, Dr. Jay Shendure, summed it up: "This kind of large-scale recording gives us a map of how one cell becomes a whole body — which helps explain how organs form correctly, how that process can go wrong, and how we might guide cells for healing."

Try this at home

Take a notebook and make two columns. Mark the first page as "Cell A" and the second as "Cell B." Every time you flip to a new page, draw a line to show which cell "divided." Soon you will have a branching family tree — just like the one the scientists built inside DNA. The more branches you add, the more you will see how a single starting point can fill an entire page.

Fun Fact

The DNA Typewriter works a bit like a typewriter from an old office: it adds one character at a time in a fixed order, never erasing what came before. Earlier methods cut the DNA to leave marks, which could damage the cell — and sometimes erase the older "typewritten" notes. The new machine avoids that problem entirely.

for-grownups: The DNA Typewriter inserts a redesigned "tape" sequence into the genome of a fertilized egg. As cells divide, the tape accumulates ordered mutations that serve as a lineage barcode. Haedong Kim et al. (UW Medicine / Howard Hughes Medical Institute), co-first authors Qi Yu and Sophie Seidel, with senior authors Jay Shendure (UW) and Chengxiang Qiu (Dartmouth), published the study in _Science_ (2026, DOI 10.1126/science.ael0508). The study analyzed 10 embryos from an initial 100, focusing on Embryo No. 3 whose recording was most complete.
