"How Can Smashing Oxygen Atoms Recreate the Universe's First Moments?"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at 18:58 IST
Reading options

Imagine squeezing something so hard that the tiny particles inside atoms break free and swirl around like soup. That is what scientists just did — and they did it with oxygen atoms, one of the lightest kinds of atom there is.

Their experiment helped recreate a strange, super-hot form of matter that filled the universe a split second after the Big Bang.

What is the universe's "first liquid"?

Every atom has a core called a nucleus, made of protons and neutrons. Inside those are even tinier building blocks: quarks and gluons.

Normally, quarks are locked tightly together and can never wander off alone. But if you crash nuclei together hard enough, the locks break. For a fraction of a second, quarks and gluons fly free in a scorching soup scientists call quark-gluon plasma (QGP).

This soup is extremely hot — trillions of degrees — and it existed just after the universe began. The problem? It vanishes faster than a billionth of a billionth of a second, so nobody can ever see it directly.

How do you know it was there?

You look at what flies out.

Scientists at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) in New York use a giant detector called STAR to watch the spray of particles after a crash. When two nuclei collide, a quark or gluon from one can strike one from the other, firing out two narrow jets of particles in opposite directions. These pairs are called "di-jets."

Here is the clever part. If one jet has to push through the hot soup, it loses energy — like a bullet slowing down as it travels through water. Scientists call this jet quenching. If the jets come out uneven, with one much weaker, that is a strong hint that a quark-gluon plasma formed.

The new twist: doing it with oxygen

For years, scientists proved jet quenching by smashing huge gold nuclei together.

This new experiment used oxygen nuclei instead — far smaller — and at much lower energy than before. And it worked! The jets were quenched, showing the quark-gluon plasma can form even in this smaller, gentler system.

"It's the same thing with these high-energy particles. When they go through the QGP, they will lose energy," explained physicist Li Yi of Shandong University, comparing it to a bullet slowing in water. Finding the point where the signal turns off helps scientists map exactly what it takes to make this primordial matter.

Try this at home

Fill a glass with water and another with air. Flick a small paper ball through each one. Which one slows the ball down more? The water! That is exactly what happens to particle jets inside quark-gluon plasma — the "thicker" the material, the more energy the jets lose along the way.

Fun Fact

RHIC's quark-gluon plasma is the hottest liquid ever made by humans — far hotter than the surface of the Sun. And oddly, it flows almost perfectly, with barely any friction at all, like a liquid with no stickiness.

For grown-ups

"The STAR collaboration at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) at Brookhaven National Laboratory has reported strong evidence of quark-gluon plasma (QGP) formation in oxygen-oxygen collisions at substantially lower energy than any previous small-system result. Published in Physical Review Letters, the analysis detects jet quenching — energetic particle jets losing energy as they traverse hot, dense matter — a signature previously confirmed only in larger collision systems (gold-gold at RHIC, oxygen at the LHC's much higher energies). RHIC ran from 2000 until earlier in 2026. Li Yi (Shandong University) oversaw the STAR analysis; Rongrong Ma is a STAR physicist at Brookhaven. The result sets a new lower energy boundary for producing QGP in a small nuclear system. Sources: ScienceDaily (release 261030002935) and the Brookhaven National Laboratory press release, 'Oxygen Collisions Quench Jets' Energy at RHIC'."
