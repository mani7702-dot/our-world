How did a kangaroo end up swimming three kilometres out to sea

By Our World Published 28 October 2026 at 16:15 IST
Reading options

A dive crew heading out across Boston Bay in South Australia spotted something in the water that nobody believed at first. It was a kangaroo, roughly three kilometres from the nearest beach, and it was worn out.

A shout from the skipper

The morning started like any other workday on the water. A dive crew left the marina at Port Lincoln, a fishing town on the coast of South Australia, and headed out across Boston Bay. They had gear to check and a long day ahead.

Then the skipper, Ryan Smith, turned around and said he thought he had seen a kangaroo.

Nobody believed him. Kangaroos belong on dry land, in paddocks and scrub and national park, not in the middle of a cold bay. The crew told him he was imagining things. He insisted. So they turned the boat around to go and look.

It was a kangaroo. A male, swimming — or rather, floating and paddling — roughly three kilometres from shore. That is about the length of thirty football fields, and far enough out that most people would not want to swim it.

Why a kangaroo can swim at all

If you have only ever seen kangaroos hopping, this seems impossible. But kangaroos are actually strong swimmers. Zoologists who have studied them describe a dog-paddle: the animal kicks its back legs one at a time and paddles with its front legs, while its big tail swings side to side to keep it balanced.

They do not do it for fun. Kangaroos usually swim only when they have to — to cross a river, to cool down, or to escape something on land. In deep water they are slow and quickly tired, because a body built for bouncing is not built for the sea.

That is exactly what the crew found. The animal was exhausted and waterlogged, with its head low in the water.

The ride home

Fishing boats have a section of hull called a sea door that can be lowered almost to the waterline, so divers can slide in and out. The crew opened it. Then diver and farmer Jake Murray reached under the kangaroo's front legs and lifted.

He held on carefully but firmly, because a kangaroo's back legs are powerful and can kick hard when the animal is frightened. Murray kept hold of them the whole way back, cradling the kangaroo against himself to keep it calm. After a while, he said, it seemed to fall asleep in his arms.

Also on board was Miguel Villarroel, a diver from the Galápagos Islands. He had lived in Australia for a while, but this was his first kangaroo ever — and it arrived by sea. He asked whether this happens often. Murray told him he had never seen anything like it in his life.

Back at the beach, the kangaroo was set down on the sand. It sat for a while, resting and drying out. Then it got up and hopped away.

Nobody knows how it got there

Here is the honest part: there is no confirmed answer. The water that morning was unusually calm and flat. Murray's best guesses are that a tide carried the animal out, or that it simply kept swimming further than it meant to. He also joked that it was probably a teenager showing off for its friends.

Two things are worth keeping from the story. The first is that the crew turned the boat around for a report they did not believe — and were right to. The second is that wild animals sometimes turn up in the wrong place for reasons we never work out, and the helpful thing is not to explain it, but to get them home.

Try this at home

Why is it easier to float in the sea than in a swimming pool? Test it in the kitchen.

  1. Fill a tall glass with water and gently lower in a fresh egg. It sinks.
  2. Stir in several big spoonfuls of salt until it dissolves. Lower the egg again.
  3. The egg now floats.

Salt makes water denser, so it pushes up harder on anything in it. Seawater holds a tired swimmer up a little more than fresh water does — one small reason the kangaroo was still afloat after such a long swim. Rinse the egg and ask a grown-up before using the kitchen.

For grown-ups

Reported by ABC News (Emma Pedler, ABC Eyre Peninsula, 21 October 2026) and independently by 7NEWS, whose crew detail differs slightly (7NEWS says the boat was the dive company Van Dorn's, leaving about 7am to inspect kingfish farms, and describes the animal as a red kangaroo; ABC and the skipper's own account give the distance as an estimate of roughly 3 km, not a measurement). Both agree on the skipper, the sea door, the cradle-and-carry and the release onto a beach. Kangaroo swimming itself is well documented in the zoological literature: the San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance fact sheets (Dawson 1995, 2013) describe macropods as competent swimmers that dog-paddle with alternating hind-limb kicks and use the tail for balance, and note that swimming is not typical behaviour for them — it happens when they have to. Note the ambiguity in the reporting: no one involved knows how the animal got that far out, and the tide explanation is the rescuer's guess, not a finding. Treat the distance as approximate.
