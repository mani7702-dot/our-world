"Hera has arrived: the hidden half of an asteroid we crashed into"

By Our World Published 18 November 2026 at "11:05" IST
Reading options

"In 2022 NASA smashed a spacecraft into an asteroid on purpose. Now a small European probe has caught up with it - and sent back a picture of a side of the bigger asteroid that no camera had ever seen."

In 2022, humans did something that had never been done before. We hit an asteroid on purpose.

NASA's DART spacecraft flew 11 million kilometres and crashed straight into a small moon-shaped rock called Dimorphos. The crash worked: the little asteroid's path around its bigger companion changed. But DART's camera only had a few seconds to look before it was destroyed.

Now a second spacecraft has arrived to see what happened. It is called Hera, it belongs to the European Space Agency, and this month it sent home a picture of a side of an asteroid that no camera has ever seen.

A two-year chase to a pair of rocks

Hera launched on 7 October 2024 and spent almost exactly two years travelling. It is now 196.8 million kilometres from Earth.

Its destination is not one asteroid but two. The bigger one, Didymos, is about 780 metres across - a little smaller than the world's tallest skyscraper. Circling it is the smaller one, Dimorphos, about 151 metres across, roughly the size of the Great Pyramid of Giza.

The spacecraft itself is about the size of a small car. In October, two gentle thruster firings slowed it down to just 20 metres per second relative to the asteroids, so it could creep up on them slowly instead of flying straight past.

On 18 October, Hera's camera finally showed the two rocks as two separate dots - from 1,280 kilometres away. That was the first-light picture we wrote about in October.

The side nobody had seen

On 5 November, ESA shared Hera's first close-up. Dimorphos is almost completely hidden in shadow. But Didymos is lit up - and it is the half of it that DART never photographed.

"This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART's camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world," said Patrick Michel, the scientist in charge of Hera's science.

Since then, Hera has crept closer and closer:

  • 12 November: from 56 kilometres away, showing one face of Didymos with a tiny piece of Dimorphos peeking out near the top.
  • 17 November: from just 28.5 kilometres away, Didymos seen three ways at once - ordinary light, colour (25 different shades), and heat.

Those pictures show Didymos is "dominated by its dramatic equatorial ridge" - a raised band running right around its middle. Michel thinks he knows why. Didymos spins fast, once every 2.2 hours, and that spin may push loose rubble towards its equator, where it piles up into the ridge.

Hera is now only 25 to 30 kilometres away.

What happens next

On 25 November, ESA will reveal the first detailed pictures of Dimorphos itself - the crash site. After that, Hera will release two shoebox-sized mini-spacecraft called CubeSats to fly even closer, and eventually Hera itself will land.

Why does any of this matter? Because one day, an asteroid might be found heading towards Earth. DART showed that hitting one can work. Hera's job is to work out exactly how well it worked - so that, if we ever need to do it for real, we know what to do.

Try this at home: build your own impact crater

You can copy what DART did, using a tray and some flour.

  1. Fill a deep tray or box with flour and smooth the top flat.
  2. Dust a thin layer of cocoa powder over the flour.
  3. Drop a marble straight down onto it from 10 centimetres.
  4. Lift the marble out and look. You have a crater - and a ring of powder thrown outwards.
  5. Do it again from 30 centimetres, then 60 centimetres. Measure the crater each time.

Watch the powder that flies out. That is exactly what happened at Dimorphos: the material DART threw into space pushed back on the asteroid and gave it an extra shove. That extra push is part of why the orbit changed so much more than scientists first expected.

For grown-ups

"ESA's Hera mission (launched 7 October 2024) arrived at the (65803) Didymos system in November 2026 and is in its early characterisation phase at 25-30 km range, 196.8 million km from Earth. The 5 November 2026 close-up was taken with the monochromatic Asteroid Framing Camera (AFC) and shows the hemisphere of the 780-m Didymos not imaged by DART before its 26 September 2022 impact on the 151-m moonlet Dimorphos; Dimorphos itself is almost entirely in shadow in that frame. The 17 November release combined AFC (about 2.7 m per pixel at 28.5 km), HyperScout H (25 spectral bands, visible to near-infrared) and JAXA's Thermal Infrared Imager (TIRI). Didymos rotates once every 2.2 hours, fast enough for centrifugal migration of regolith towards the equator - the likely origin of its prominent equatorial ridge (spin-up/mass-shedding morphology). DART's kinetic impact shortened Dimorphos's orbital period around Didymos by about 32 minutes, and the momentum enhancement factor (beta) exceeded 2 because ejecta carried extra momentum away; Hera's job is to measure Dimorphos's mass and the crater dimensions to pin down beta and turn a one-off experiment into a repeatable deflection technique. Detailed images of both bodies are due 25 November 2026, followed by two CubeSats and a landing. Note: some ESA media captions give the 5 November frame distance as 600 km while the accompanying article says 60 km; the article text is used here and no distance is asserted in the piece."
