5 ways hidden tidal power could light up coastal towns like a pocket-sized power plant

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 09:15 IST
Reading options

A Scottish engineer's "Swimmer Turbine" lies flat on the seabed and spins in the tide like a underwater kite — a 50kw generator that can power 30 homes and fit together from a shipping container's worth of parts.

The ocean never stops moving. Twice a day, tides rush in and pull back, carrying enough energy to power every home in Britain several times over. The problem has always been the same: how do you catch that power without building giant machines that scare fish and cost a fortune?

Engineer David Mummery has an answer that started with him floating face-down in a Scottish river.

2 min read: the swimmer turbine

More than a decade ago, Mummery tied a cord tight across a fast-flowing Highland river, swam out, and let the current sweep over him. Lying on the water, he could feel how it twisted and turned — and he started picturing a turbine built to move with the river instead of fighting it.

That daydream became the Swimmer Turbine: a flat, fiberglass-and-steel disc anchored to the seabed that rides the tide like an underwater kite. Where the big tidal farms put three-bladed windmills in the sea, Mummery's machine lies invisible below the surface, spinning in the horizontal flow.

The five numbers that matter

  1. 50 kilowatts of clean power — enough for about 30 homes, all from one quiet disc on the ocean floor.
  2. ~20 parts make up the whole machine. Mummery calls it the "Ikea kit" version of tidal power: open a shipping container and you get numbered pieces that bolt together.
  3. Fishermen and village workers can assemble and install it themselves from their own harbor — no heavy-lift ships or million-pound cranes.
  4. Flat-pack size: it's roughly a shipping-container's footprint, not the size of a house.
  5. Ultra-low cost compared to the giants, because it skips the expensive moorings and seabed foundations.

Scotland: the world's tidal test kitchen

Mummery's turbine is tiny next to Scotland's tidal giants, but the country is where the big ideas live:

  • MeyGen (Pentland Firth): the planet's largest tidal stream array, with four 1.5-megawatt turbines on the seabed — a single one holds 27 times the output of the Swimmer Turbine.
  • Nova Innovation (Bluemull Sound, Shetland): the world's first commercial tidal stream array, deployed in 2016.
  • Orbital O2 (Fall of Warness, Orkney): the most powerful floating tidal turbine ever built, at 2 megawatts.

All of that fits a pattern: Scotland's chilly, fast-flowing waters — the Pentland Firth funnels tides up to 5 knots — make it the perfect lab for turning the sea's pull into electricity.

The catch (and the comeback)

Tidal power is clean and predictable: where wind gusts and sun fades, the ocean obeys the moon. But it's also brutally expensive to build and maintain underwater, and the sea is a harsh landlord.

The famous cautionary tale is the Pelamis machine — a 180-metre wave-energy "sea snake" that in 2010 became the first floating wave device to feed power into the national grid. It worked. The company still went into administration two years later, because the market never caught up fast enough.

Mummery knows that story. That is why his turbine bets on small: one fisherman's kit, not one billionaire's power plant. If a village can buy one with savings and share the electricity, it does not need a stock listing to survive.

Try this at home

  • At a beach or riverbank where the water flows fast, stick a pencil upright in the sand and watch which way the ripples push it — that is the direction a tidal turbine would spin.
  • Find a spoon and run it under a trickling tap at an angle: the water pushes the handle. That tiny twist is the same force that turns Mummery's seabed disc.
  • Look up "tidal range" maps of your coastline — places where the water level changes a lot twice a day have the strongest, most predictable tide power.

Fun fact

The word "tide" comes from a Old English word for "time" — the ocean has been keeping humanity's schedule for longer than any clock.

For grownups

Mummery's "Swimmer Turbine" concept trades single-machine megawatt output for modularity and low capital cost, a defensible niche for tidal energy where grid-scale arrays struggle to compete on levelized cost. The Scottish tidal sector (MeyGen, Nova Innovation, Orbital O2) remains the global reference fleet for tidal-stream technology; Pelamis's 2014 administration remains the cautionary precedent for the capital-intensity trap.
