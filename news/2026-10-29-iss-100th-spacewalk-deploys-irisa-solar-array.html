What Happened on the 100th Spacewalk from the Space Station?

1. The 100th Walk in space

On Thursday, October 29, astronauts did something truly special — they completed the 100th U.S. spacewalk launched from the International Space Station. That is 100 times humans have left the safety of the space station and worked outside in the blackness of space, all without the Space Shuttle that used to be there.

NASA's Anil Menon and Canada's Joshua Kutryk spent 7 hours and 11 minutes moving around the station in their bulky white spacesuits, doing work that would take seconds on Earth but hours in the vacuum of orbit.

2. A solar array that rolls out like a carpet

Their job was to install a brand-new solar panel called an iROSA — that is short for ISS Roll-Out Solar Array. Unlike the stiff, old-fashioned panels, the iROSA is more like a carpet made of flexible solar cells. It arrives rolled up tightly inside a cylinder, just like a rolled-up sleeping bag.

Once Anil and Joshua unhooked it, the stored energy in the panel's carbon-composite booms did all the work. With no motor needed, the panel slowly unfurled to its full size: 63 feet long — that is longer than two school buses parked end to end!

The new panel clips right onto the station's older, worn-out solar wings, giving the whole station a fresh burst of power. After more than 20 years in space, the original panels are starting to fade and lose their efficiency — just like an old phone battery.

3. Why power matters in space

Sunlight is free in space, but collecting that energy is how the station stays alive. Everything needs electricity: the lights, the computers, the experiments, even keeping the air breathable and the water drinkable for the crew.

With the new iROSA online, the station gets a 20 to 30 percent boost in power. That extra juice lets scientists run more experiments and charge more equipment. It also helps the station stay healthy until its planned controlled landing in 2030, when it will be guided safely into the ocean.

4. A milestone for Canada, too

This spacewalk was special for Canada as well. Joshua Kutryk became only the fifth Canadian to ever walk in space — and the first since David Saint-Jacques in 2019. As the robotic arm swung the astronauts into position, Mission Control could see Canada's flag patch on his suit, a reminder that space exploration is a team effort across the whole world.

Menon and Kutryk are even planning a follow-up walk on November 5 to install one more solar panel — the eighth and final iROSA — completing the station's five-year makeover project that started in 2021.

Try This At Home

Next time you're outdoors on a sunny day, grab a small solar calculator (the kind with no battery) and cover part of its solar cell with your hand. Watch how the numbers dim or disappear the moment you block the light! Just like the ISS solar panels, the calculator depends entirely on sunlight. You can also try unrolling a rolled-up measuring tape — notice how the spring inside helps it extend? That is the same idea as how the iROSA unfurls in space, except instead of your hand, the panel deploys itself using stored spring energy in its carbon-composite frame.

Fun Fact

The space station itself is bigger than a football field and weighs about 420 tons. It orbits Earth at 17,500 miles per hour, which means it circles our entire planet every 90 minutes. That works out to 15.5 orbits per day — and 100 spacewalks is how far human ingenuity has come in keeping such a massive home flying.

For grown-ups

"On Thursday, October 29, 2026, NASA astronaut Anil Menon and Canadian Space Agency (CSA) astronaut Joshua Kutryk completed the 100th U.S. spacewalk performed from the International Space Station (ISS) without the now-retired Space Shuttle. Over 7 hours and 11 minutes, they installed and deployed the seventh ISS Roll-Out Solar Array (iROSA), a 10-by-2-by-4-foot (3-by-0.6-by-1.2-m) flexible solar panel that unfurled to its full 63-foot (19-m) length using stored spring energy in its carbon-composite booms — no motor required. The iROSA was delivered to orbit inside SpaceX Dragon's cargo trunk on the CRS-35 mission and was bolted onto the ISS's 2A power channel using the station's Canadarm2 robotic arm. When paired with the older legacy panels, the iROSAs boost ISS power generation by 20 to 30 percent, supporting ongoing science and preparing the station for its planned controlled deorbit in 2030. This was Menon's fourth EVA and Kutryk's first — also making him the fifth Canadian to walk in space and the first Canadian since David Saint-Jacques in 2019. A follow-up EVA on November 5 will install the eighth and final iROSA, completing the station's five-year power-upgrade campaign. Wikipedia's International Space Station article independently confirms the ISS orbits at ~400 km altitude in low Earth orbit, the Integrated Truss Structure supports the solar arrays, Canadarm2 provides robotic assistance, and that the station is expected to be deorbited by the end of 2030 using a controlled deorbit vehicle."
