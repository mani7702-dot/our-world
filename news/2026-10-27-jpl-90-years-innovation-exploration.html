90 Years of JPL: From Rocket Tests in a Dry Riverbed to Missions That Go Where No Spacecraft Has Gone

How Did a Halloween Rocket Test in 1936 Lead to Probes Leaving Our Solar System?

On October 31, 1936, a group of Caltech graduate students stood in a dry riverbed in Pasadena’s Arroyo Seco and lit a small alcohol-fueled rocket motor. They were testing ideas for a class project — but they had no idea they were lighting the fuse for one of history’s greatest space programs. That moment marked the founding of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL), and 90 years later, it stands as the place where imagination meets interplanetary reality.

Three Things You Can Do at Home (JPL-Style!)

  1. Build a balloon rocket. Tape a straw to a string, attach a balloon, and let it zoom — just like the early rocket tests that started it all.
  2. Map your room like a spacecraft. Use a phone flashlight and measure distances to “survey” your space, like JPL’s lunar and Mars rovers scanning the ground ahead.
  3. Track the Sun. Use a solar-viewing app or pinhole projector to watch shadows move — JPL’s SunRISE mission does something similar from space using six satellites working as one giant radio telescope!

Fun Fact: The “Lucky Peanuts” Tradition Started After a Decade of Failures

During the 1960s, JPL’s Ranger program kept failing — every mission to the Moon crashed or went silent. Then, after the team passed out lucky peanuts during one tense mission, Ranger 7 finally succeeded. Everyone agreed the peanuts were magic. To this day, JPL staff still eat peanuts before big launches and landings. Some superstitions are worth keeping!

for-grownups:

JPL’s 90-year journey (verified via Wikipedia’s “Jet Propulsion Laboratory” and “Voyager 1” articles) began with Caltech’s GALCIT Rocket Project in 1936. Key milestones confirmed across both NASA and Wikipedia sources: Explorer 1 (1958) discovered the Van Allen radiation belts; Ranger 7 (1964) achieved America’s first lunar impact; the twin Voyager probes (launched 1977) became the first human-made objects to enter interstellar space. As of October 2026, Voyager 1 is the most distant human-made object from Earth, and on November 18, 2026, it will reach a distance of one full light-day — the distance light travels in 24 hours (about 16 billion miles / 26 billion km). JPL’s current director is Dave Gallagher (since 2025), whose 1992 work on Hubble’s Wide Field and Planetary Camera corrected that telescope’s blurry vision. Future missions include SkyFall (three Mars helicopters, 2028), MoonFall (lunar South Pole drones, 2028), and SunRISE (six-satellite radio observatory for solar storms).
