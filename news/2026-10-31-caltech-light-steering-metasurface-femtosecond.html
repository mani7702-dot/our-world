"How Can You Steer a Beam of Light With Another Beam of Light?"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at 21:05 IST
Reading options

"Usually light ignores light. Engineers at Caltech built a nanoscale surface that lets one flash of light bend another — in 74 quadrillionths of a second."

Light is the fastest thing in the universe, and it carries enormous amounts of information. So why is it so hard to steer? The trouble is that light usually ignores other light — two beams can cross paths without either one changing direction.

A team at the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) has now shown a way around that. They built an ultra-thin surface that lets one flash of light redirect another in just 74 femtoseconds — 74 quadrillionths of a second. That is roughly how long light takes to cross the width of a single human hair.

Why fast light control is tricky

Many everyday devices steer light by changing the electrical properties of a material — think of the liquid-crystal panels inside a projector, or the optical chips that carry internet signals. In those systems, electrons get pushed into higher energy states and then fall back down, releasing their extra energy.

That falling-back step takes time. It usually limits how quickly a device can switch, pinning most technology to nanoseconds or picoseconds — thousands of times slower than the new record.

The trick: let light nudge light

The Caltech team removed the electrical signal entirely. Instead, they used a strong "pump" beam of light with a carefully shaped pattern to briefly change how a material responds, then sent a weaker "probe" beam through it. The probe bent according to the pump's pattern.

This relies on a phenomenon called the optical Kerr effect: when intense light passes through a material, it slightly changes the material's refractive index, the number that describes how much light slows down and bends as it travels. Crucially, the effect comes from electrons shifting inside their orbitals, not from jumping into long-lasting excited states — so it can appear and vanish almost as fast as the light pulse itself.

On its own the effect is far too weak to be useful. So the team built a metasurface: a thin film of silicon covered in nanoscale pillars, each smaller than the wavelength of the pump light. By choosing the pillars' size and spacing carefully, they trapped light inside the surface a little longer, amplifying the tiny refractive change. The result: the probe beam steered by up to 13 degrees in either direction.

Fun fact

Because the switching speed was limited by the laser pulse — not by any fundamental property of the material — the researchers think even faster control is possible with shorter pulses. They point toward exotic new ideas like "time crystals" and materials whose optical properties change in time, not just in space.

Try this at home

You can watch light bend without any lab equipment. Fill a clear glass with water, place a pencil in it, and look from the side — the pencil appears to snap in two at the water's surface. That bending is refraction, the same slowing-and-bending of light that the Caltech device tunes billions of times a second. Try adding a drop of milk to the water and shine a torch through: the milky water scatters light the way a metasurface scatters and redirects a beam, letting you see light's path instead of just its destination.
