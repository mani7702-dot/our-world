"How did a V-2 rocket take the first photo of Earth from space?"

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 14:32 IST
Reading options

"On October 24, 1946, a captured German V-2 rocket flew higher than anyone had ever gone before — and its motion-picture camera snapped the very first pictures of our home planet from the blackness of space."

How Did a V-2 Rocket Take the First Photo of Earth from Space?

The rocket that changed how we see home

On October 24, 1946, a loud whoosh echoed across the New Mexico desert — and humanity saw itself for the first time. A V-2 rocket soared up through the darkening sky, carrying something new: a tiny 35-millimeter motion-picture camera. When the film was recovered days later, scientists discovered the first photographs ever taken of Earth from space.

The V-2 had begun life during World War II as Germany's "vengeance weapon," but after the war the United States tested captured rockets at the White Sands Missile Range. On that October day, a modified V-2 flew up past 65 miles (about 105 km) above the ground — well beyond the edge of space. As it climbed, the camera clicked away, taking a new frame every second and a half.

A race to save the film

The rocket came back down at 340 miles per hour, crashing into the desert as designed. Both the rocket and camera were broken on impact, but the precious film — protected inside a steel case — survived. Teams rushed out into the New Mexico sands to dig it up.

When they finally saw the images, the scientists were overwhelmed. For the first time in history, humans could look back and see their blue planet floating in the blackness — a fragile world with no visible borders or boundaries.

Why the first photo matters

Before 1946, no human eye had ever seen Earth from above. Those grainy, black-and-white pictures may look simple now, but they sparked something huge. They showed people a new view of home — a "pale blue dot" hanging in endless space. This view helped grow the idea of Earth as one shared, delicate world, planting seeds that would bloom into the environmental movement decades later.

The ability to photograph our planet from above also proved that cameras and instruments could survive the journey into space — and that idea quickly grew into today's fleet of weather, mapping, and climate satellites that help us watch over our home every single day.

Try this at home

Stand in a dark room holding a small flashlight (your planet) next to a bright lamp (your star). Shine the lamp on a wall — the lamp dominates everything. Now, very slowly cover the lamp with your hand, leaving just a tiny gap. Look at that gap through a cardboard tube, and you might just make out the faint glow of your "planet." That narrow opening is like the space between Earth and space, where the very first photos were born.

for-grownups: The technology behind the first space photo

The October 24, 1946 launch was V-2 No. 13, a U.S. Army-modified variant of the German Aggregat-4 (A-4) ballistic missile recovered under Operation Paperclip. The rocket carried a 35mm Mitchell astroation camera (later dubbed "Camera B") firing every 1.5 seconds, designed by the Naval Research Laboratory to study atmospheric density, temperature, and cosmic ray effects. The mission reached apogee of approximately 105 km (65 mi), crossing the Kármán line (100 km) — the internationally recognized boundary of space — making these the first suborbital Earth-observation photographs. The film was housed in a steel container with a balsa-wood impact cushion and a radar beacon for recovery. The V-2's B-Stoff alcohol/water fuel and LOX propellant combination, guidance by gyroscopes and a Mischgerät autopilot, enabled the vertical launch trajectory necessary to reach space-entry altitudes. The recovered images, declassified and archived, are catalogued as the "First Camera-B" series at the National Air and Space Museum.

Fun Fact

The very same V-2 rocket design that took the first space photos was originally built as a weapon — but after the war, the scientists who built it (including a young Wernher von Braun) turned it into humanity's first reusable scientific probe to visit space, laying the technological groundwork for every satellite and human spaceflight that followed.
