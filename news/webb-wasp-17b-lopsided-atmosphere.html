Why is this alien planet's atmosphere lopsided?

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 22:30 IST
Reading options

A scorching gas giant 1,300 light-years away has an atmosphere that bulges out more on one side than the other — a new 3D mapping technique with the James Webb Space Telescope confirmed the prediction.

Some alien planets are so hot they make our own Sun look cool by comparison. But a new discovery about one of these blistering worlds — a gas giant named WASP-17b — shows that the real surprise is not just how hot it is, but how lopsided its atmosphere has become. Scientists using NASA's James Webb Space Telescope have made the first 3D map of this planet's outer atmosphere, and it looks like someone gently squeezed one side.

A planet with a lopsided balloon

WASP-17b is what astronomers call a hot Jupiter — a massive gas giant that orbits so close to its star that its daytime side can reach temperatures of 2,000 degrees Celsius or more. It sits about 1,300 light-years away from us.

Thanks to a new technique called transmission strings, researchers have now mapped how far the planet's atmosphere extends at every angle around its edge. Instead of being a smooth, even circle, the atmosphere bulges outward on one side and pulls in tighter on the other. The planet is not actually lopsided itself — its overall shape stays nearly round — but the atmosphere extends farther on the evening side than on the morning side.

The power of planetary winds

Why does one side bulge? It comes down to heat and motion. This planet is tidally locked, which means one side — the dayside — always stares directly at its star while the other side — the nightside — is forever dark.

Powerful winds carry heat from the scorching dayside to the cooler nightside. On WASP-17b, those winds flow from the star-facing side toward the dark side, carrying extra heat and causing the atmosphere there to expand outward. This makes the atmosphere on the so-called "evening" hemisphere stretch farther than on the cooler "morning" side, creating the visible asymmetry.

A new way to read the skies

The transmission strings method was developed by Dr. David Grant and Dr. Hannah Wakeford at the University of Bristol. Instead of treating the planet's atmospheric ring as a single average circle, the technique peels apart light coming through different slices of the atmosphere — like reading individual pages of a book instead of skimming the whole chapter.

This breakthrough means scientists can now study the weather and chemistry of alien atmospheres in 3D detail. And WASP-17b is just the beginning: researchers hope to apply the same method to dozens more worlds that Webb has already observed.

Why it matters

Being able to see the 3D shape of an atmosphere tells us how heat moves around a planet, what chemicals are present, and how clouds form. It is the first step toward reading the weather on worlds we could never visit. Someday, the same technique might even help us look for signs of air and oceans on smaller, rockier planets.

Try this at home: Blow up a balloon and squeeze one side gently. The surface bulges on one side and flattens on the other — just like an atmosphere pushed around by invisible winds. That little squeeze is a tiny version of what Webb sees on a world 1,300 light-years away.

For the grown-ups

The transmission strings mapping technique was pioneered by Dr. David Grant and Dr. Hannah Wakeford at the University of Bristol. Lead author Dr. Lili Alderson (Cornell University) applied it to JWST observations of WASP-17b taken as part of the JWST Telescope Scientist Deep Reconnaissance of Exoplanet Atmospheres through Multi-instrument Spectroscopy (TST DREAMS) Guaranteed Time Observations program (GTO-1353). The observations used JWST's NIRISS/SOSS, NIRSpec/G395H, and MIRI/LRS instruments in March 2023. The technique uses Fourier-series parameterization of the atmospheric limb to extract angle-resolved transmission spectra, revealing the expected temperature contrast between the day and night hemispheres. Results appear in Nature Astronomy, DOI: 10.1038/s41550-026-02986-4.
