How did one long river become a 'link of life' for jaguars and pink dolphins?

By Our World Published 27 October 2026 at 21:58 IST
Reading options

A new wildlife reserve in Bolivia protects 715,000 acres along a 780-mile river — a watery road that animals use to travel between two countries.

A river can be a road. That is the big idea behind a brand-new wildlife park in Bolivia, where a group of conservationists and local communities have set aside about 715,000 acres of forest, wetland and grassland.

The park is called the Agua Dulce Municipal Wildlife Reserve, and it sits along the Iténez River. The river is about 780 miles long and makes up most of the border between Bolivia and Brazil. Scientists call it a "link of life," because animals can follow the water from one protected patch of forest to the next instead of getting stuck in small, separate islands of green.

Who lives there?

Quite a crowd. Jaguars pad through the trees. Pink Amazon river dolphins swim in the river. Giant otters chase fish, lowland tapirs wander the muddy banks, and black caimans sun themselves on the shore.

The reserve counts more than 70 kinds of mammals, 489 kinds of birds, 47 reptiles and amphibians, and 55 kinds of fish. The wider river is even busier — about 260 fish species live in it, and 25 of them are found nowhere else on Earth.

Why a river, and not just a fence?

Animals do not read maps, and they do not stop at borders. If a jaguar's forest is protected on one side of a river but cut down on the other, the jaguar is still in trouble. The new reserve joins up with six other protected areas in Bolivia and Brazil, so the wild places touch each other like puzzle pieces.

People live here too. The plan was written together with the communities around Baures, and it aims at three things: helping wildlife, keeping the river clean and healthy, and letting local families earn a living from the forest in ways that do not wreck it.

The bigger scoreboard

The group behind the reserve, Rainforest Trust, works with local partners in many countries. This project pushed its total protected land past 70 million acres — an area about the size of Nevada — and 10 million of those acres were added in just eight months.

Try this at home: Draw a river on paper, then draw three small parks on one side and two on the other. Now draw thin "bridges" of green between them. That is what a wildlife corridor is — and it is the same reason your school might plant a strip of flowers between two empty lots so bees can travel.

For grown-ups

Agua Dulce is a municipal wildlife reserve, created by local legislation rather than a national decree — which is why the Faunagua-led process emphasized municipal consensus, legal designation, and management instruments. The reserve covers 11 distinct vegetation types (Amazonian forest, floodplain, pampas, riparian zones) and forms part of a transboundary corridor across the Bolivia–Brazil border. Note the two fish counts floating around: 55 species recorded inside the reserve, versus roughly 260 in the Iténez/Guaporé system as a whole. The 70-million-acre figure is cumulative for Rainforest Trust since 1988, not a single purchase.
