What happens in the sky above a hurricane? NASA spots invisible waves

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 14:47 IST
Reading options

Hurricane Simon stirred up more than wind and rain — NASA satellites saw enormous invisible waves rippling through Earth's upper atmosphere far above the storm.

Most of us think of hurricanes as something happening close to the ground — wind, rain, and waves crashing on the coast. But a powerful hurricane does something else too: it sends invisible ripples racing upward, all the way into the edge of space. And NASA's eyes in the sky are now catching those ripples in action.

Waves in the sky

When Hurricane Simon — a strong Category 4 storm that swept across the Pacific near Mexico's coast in mid-October 2026 — spun over the ocean, something remarkable was happening far above the clouds.

NASA satellites saw enormous atmospheric gravity waves spreading outward from the storm. These are not ocean waves — they are ripples in the atmosphere itself, like the rings you see when you toss a stone into a pond, but stretched a million times taller.

How a storm makes sky-ripples

Here is what scientists think happens. Inside Hurricane Simon, warm, moist air rose up in towering clouds, some reaching 10 miles (16 kilometers) high or more. As that air surged upward in powerful updrafts, it pushed against the layers of atmosphere above it.

The atmosphere is like a giant, invisible blanket made of many layers. When the punch of rising air from the hurricane hit the blanket, it sent ripples racing sideways — not just once, but continuously, as long as the storm kept spinning.

The ripples then traveled high into the stratosphere, the layer of the atmosphere 10 to 30 miles above Earth. Up there, far from the storm's surface winds, the waves can spread out over hundreds of miles — sometimes even all the way around the planet.

Why NASA is watching

NASA's Suomi NPP satellite — orbiting 500 miles above Earth — carries a sensor called VIIRS (Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite). On the early morning of October 11, VIIRS caught Simon's eye from space, and another satellite instrument spotted the invisible waves fanning out from the storm's center.

Scientists want to understand these waves because they act like messengers between Earth's weather and its space environment. When waves crash into the upper atmosphere, they can stir up enough energy to heat the air there — sometimes by several degrees in a single night.

What it means for us

Even though the waves themselves are invisible and harmless down on the ground, they are part of a bigger picture: they help carry energy and momentum from our weather systems up into the part of the atmosphere where our satellites and communications signals travel.

That makes them important not just for understanding hurricanes, but for keeping our space-based technology working well — because the same atmospheric disturbances that ripple above a storm can also jumble radio signals and nudge satellites slightly off course.

Try this at home

You can see the same effect on a small scale. Blow gently across the surface of a bowl of water, or flick a jump rope and watch the waves travel to the far end. A hurricane does the same thing — except the river of air it is flinging waves into is hundreds of miles deep, and the waves travel all the way to the edge of space.

Fun Fact: The waves above Hurricane Simon were thousands of times taller than any wave you could surf — some stretched more than 50 miles (80 km) from crest to trough as they rose through the atmosphere.

For the grown-ups

Atmospheric gravity waves generated by tropical cyclones are a known but understudied coupling mechanism between tropospheric weather and the middle/upper atmosphere. NASA's Suomi NPP satellite, carrying the VIIRS instrument, captured infrared imagery of Hurricane Simon (2026) on October 11, 2026. The imagery revealed concentric gravity wave structures propagating outward from the storm center into the stratosphere. These waves form when convective updrafts within the hurricane's eyewall inject momentum and energy into the overlying atmosphere, creating buoyancy oscillations that propagate vertically. The phenomenon was confirmed by co-located observations from the NOAA-21 satellite's VIIRS sensor. Hurricane Simon was a Category 4 storm on the Saffir-Simpson scale, with peak sustained winds of 155 mph (250 km/h), as confirmed by the National Hurricane Center (Wikipedia: 2026 Pacific hurricane season).
