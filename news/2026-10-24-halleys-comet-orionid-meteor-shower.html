"Where do October's shooting stars come from? Halley's Comet left a trail of dust 6 trillion miles long"

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 18:42 IST
Reading options

"Every October, Earth plows through a vast trail of dust shed by Halley's Comet, setting the stage for the Orionid meteor shower — one of the most reliable sky shows of the year."

Where Do October's Shooting Stars Come From?

Halley's long dust trail across the Solar System

Imagine a comet as it dives close to the Sun — the icy nucleus heats up, gas and dust blast away from it, and what is left behind is a trail of tiny rocks and pebbles stretching across millions of miles of space. Earth must plow through this invisible river of dust every year, and when those specks hit our atmosphere at 66 kilometers per second (that's 150,000 miles per hour), they light up as shooting stars.

This is exactly what happens every October when we see the Orionid meteor shower. These meteors don't come from just anywhere — they are the dusty remains of the most famous comet in our sky: Halley's Comet.

Every 76 years, Halley's Comet swings in from the frozen edge of the Solar System. On its last visit in 1986, the European Space Agency's Giotto spacecraft snapped the iconic photo you see above. As it loops around the Sun, the comet leaves behind a trail of debris so vast it wraps all the way around the Sun in orbit.

Why the Orionids hit peak in late October

Earth's orbit crosses Halley's debris trail at just the right angle, and every year between October 2 and November 7, our planet sweeps through the densest part of that trail. The shower typically peaks around October 21, which means the best viewing is right around now.

The meteors appear to stream from the constellation Orion — specifically from a point about 10 degrees north of the bright red star Betelgeuse. That's how the shower got its name. The Orionids are fast meteors, moving at nearly 67 km/s, which makes them some of the quickest shooting stars you can see all year.

Try this at home

Head outside on a clear October night, at least an hour after sunset. Find the darkest spot you can — a backyard, a park, anywhere away from street lights. Lay back on a blanket or comfortable chair and just look up. No telescope or binoculars needed! Give your eyes 15-20 minutes to adjust to the dark. You should start spotting fast-moving streaks of light. On a good night, you might see 10-20 meteors per hour. The Moon will be a thin crescent in these early days of the Orionids, so it won't wash out the show.

Fun Fact

Halley's Comet won't return to the inner Solar System until October 2061 — that's 35 years from now. But you don't have to wait! Its debris will keep putting on the Orionid meteor shower every single October for the next generation.

If you look closely on a still night, some Orionid meteors leave glowing persistent trains behind them that can last for seconds after the meteor itself has faded. That's because they burn up 90 kilometers up, where Earth's atmosphere is thin but still dense enough to ionize the air.

for-grownups: The Orionid meteor shower is produced by debris from 1P/Halley, a short-period comet with a 75.7-year orbital period. Halley's last perihelion passage was February 8, 1986 (observed by ESA's Giotto probe), and its next will be July 28, 2061. The comet's nucleus measures approximately 15 x 8 km and traces an orbit inclined 162 degrees to the ecliptic. Earth intersects Halley's debris stream each October-November; the meteoroids enter the atmosphere at ~66.9 km/s from the direction of Orion's radiant (RA 06h21m, Dec +15.6°). Zenithal hourly rates reach ~20 per hour during typical years, with occasional outbursts (ZHR 100+ recorded in 2006). The Orionids are one of only two showers linked to Halley's Comet — the other being the Eta Aquariids in May.
