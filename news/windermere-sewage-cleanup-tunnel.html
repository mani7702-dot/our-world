Why a 12-mile underground tunnel might save England's largest lake

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 08:30 IST
Reading options

A bold new plan would send sewage flowing through a 12-mile tunnel deep underground so it never touches Windermere, England's largest lake, again.

Why a 12-mile underground tunnel might save England's largest lake

If you have ever visited England's Lake District, you know the water in its lakes looks like melted glass. But for Windermere — the region's biggest lake — that clear blue can sometimes turn a scary shade of green. The culprit? Sewage.

1. The problem below the surface

Windermere is England's largest lake. It stretches 10.5 miles long, holds enough water to fill nearly 1 million Olympic swimming pools, and sits inside a landscape so beautiful it was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017. Close to 18 million people visit each year.

But under the surface, something has been going wrong. Homes and businesses around the lake send their wastewater through a patchwork of old drains, septic tanks, and small treatment plants. When heavy rain hits, those systems overflow — dumping raw sewage straight into the lake. Even on sunny days, treated water that still carries extra nutrients (mostly nitrogen and phosphorus from detergents and soap) pours out. When the water warms up in summer, those nutrients feed explosions of algae that paint the surface a bright, unnatural green.

2. The giant tunnel plan

A new idea is floating above the lake like morning mist — but it runs 12 miles (19 km) underground.

Engineers from the water company United Utilities want to build a single, mammoth wastewater treatment plant connected to a 12-mile tunnel. The tunnel would quietly carry sewage away from Windermere so that only rainwater — hence the slogan “Only Rainwater into Windermere” — ever touches the lake again.

The plan would hook up 12,800 homes that currently rely on older, leaky systems. After treatment, the clean water would be sent either down the River Leven or out into the Irish Sea — nowhere near the lake.

3. The price tag of cleaning up

The tunnel and treatment plant would cost about £3.6 billion (roughly $4.5 billion). That is a lot of money for one lake — so much that the government has to decide whether the plan can move forward and whether that price is acceptable.

Supporters say the lake is worth protecting. At 18 million visitors a year, tourism keeps many local businesses open. And the algae blooms — called "harmful algal blooms" — can make the water unsafe for swimmers and hurt the tiny plants and insects that fish depend on.

Campaigners are excited but cautious. As one local activist said, “This is the beginning, and the end of this is the moment that sewage stops entering Windermere.”

4. What happens next

For now, the plan stays on paper. The UK government must decide whether to develop it further and how to pay for it. Some people hope the cost can be shared across the whole country — not just paid by water-bill customers in the Northwest.

But the idea itself is making waves. A 12-mile tunnel is not a quick fix, and building one takes years. Still, if a single pipe can stop a lake from turning green, it might be worth the wait.

Try this at home

Next time you visit a lake or pond, look at the water. Is it calm and clear, or does it look pea-soup green? Algae loves nutrients — so a lake that gets too much fertilizer (from lawn sprinklers, farming, or even too many ducks) will often turn green first thing in summer. Draw what you see, then check again a week later. Nature is always changing the picture.

Fun Fact

Windermere is so big that if you could drain it completely, it would take a person rowing 8 hours a day 97 days just to row from one end to the other — without stopping for tea.

For grown-ups

Windermere is England's largest lake, stretching 10.5 miles (18 km) and holding 300 billion liters of water in the Lake District National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2017. The lake sits at the heart of a region that draws close to 18 million visitors each year. For decades, raw and treated sewage has been discharged into the lake, and on several occasions in the last five years, high nutrient levels combined with warm temperatures to trigger algal blooms that turned parts of the water a lurid green. The new "Only Rainwater into Windermere" (ORiW) proposal would connect 12,800 more homes to the sewage network and build a single wastewater treatment plant linked by a 12-mile (19 km) tunnel. The project would cost about £3.6 billion and is backed by United Utilities plus clean-water campaigners. Government decision pending.
