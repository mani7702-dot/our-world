"Why Is Mercury's Surface Missing So Much Sand?"

By Our World Published 01 November 2026 at "13:05" IST
Reading options

"Mercury's surface has far less of a common ingredient called silicon dioxide than scientists expected. A new study finally works out how much is there — and what the surprise tells us about the smallest planet's fiery past."

Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun — a scorched, cratered world that looks a lot like our Moon. So here is a puzzle: Mercury's surface has far less of a common ingredient called silicon dioxide than scientists expected. A new study says it makes up only about 37% of the surface, up to a quarter less than earlier estimates.

Why "sand" matters to a planet

Silicon dioxide (SiO₂), or silica, is the main ingredient of sand, and it shows up in rocks almost everywhere. Earth's basalt, andesite and granite can be up to 75% silica. Mercury, it turns out, is unusually poor in it.

Knowing how much silica is on a world is like knowing a little about how its rocks were "cooked." As a young planet's molten interior cools, the first minerals to harden take up very little silica, so the leftover melt grows richer in it over time. Lava that erupts late carries lots of silica; lava that erupts early carries little.

So Mercury's low silica hints that its volcanic rocks came from deeper, hotter layers of the mantle that melted a lot — a very different story from Earth's.

The trick: tiny glass beads and a Moon test

You can't exactly bring Mercury into a lab. No spacecraft has ever landed there, and there are no Mercury rock samples on Earth. Scientists have to read the surface's colour from a distance — specifically, the infrared glow it gives off.

To trust those readings, researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research and the Universities of Münster and Göttingen made tiny glass beads, each about half a millimetre across, with precisely known amounts of silica. "The glass beads serve a similar function to calibration weights on a scale," explained Iris Weber.

Before pointing their "scale" at Mercury, they tested it on the Moon — where we have real rocks, brought home by Apollo, Luna and Chang'e missions, to check the answer. Only then did they aim it at Mercury, using telescope data, including measurements from the Bok Telescope in Arizona.

What happens next

In November 2026, the European-Japanese spacecraft BepiColombo is due to slip into orbit around Mercury. Its MERTIS instrument — an infrared spectrometer — will measure the surface in much finer detail, giving the team a chance to check whether their surprising number is right.

One honest note: the study doesn't prove Mercury was once a lava world forever. It simply shows how careful measurement can turn a distant dot of light into a story about a planet's past.

Try this at home

You can build your own "calibration" experiment. Fill three clear glasses with the same amount of water, and stir in one, two, then three spoonfuls of cocoa powder. Line them up and look from across the room. Can you tell them apart? Now walk one step closer at a time — the differences slowly appear. That is exactly the problem astronomers face: reading what a surface is made of from far away, using only the light it sends us.

Fun Fact

Mercury's volcanoes went quiet around one billion years after the planet formed. Earth's are still going today — which is why our planet keeps recycling its crust while Mercury stays frozen and grey.

"Renggli, Morlok, Weber, Reitze, Di Rocco, Berndt, Pack & Hiesinger, 'The SiO2 abundance on the surfaces of the Moon and Mercury', Planetary Research (Diamond Open Access), DOI 10.53480/bf74-m226, published 27 August 2026; press release from the Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research (MPS) / Max-Planck-Gesellschaft (mpg.de/26948255). Method: laboratory infrared measurements on synthetic glass beads (≈0.5 mm) with controlled SiO2 fractions established a calibration relation, which was validated against NASA Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter infrared maps and returned Apollo/Luna/Chang'e lunar samples (yielding the first global lunar SiO2 map), then applied to Earth-based infrared spectra of Mercury including Bok Telescope (Steward Observatory) data. Result: ≈37 % SiO2 by mass at Mercury's surface, up to 25 % lower than prior estimates; interpreted as volcanic rocks sourced from more deeply melted, higher-temperature mantle, with an alternative hypothesis of crustal oxygen loss. No sample-return from Mercury exists; ESA/JAXA BepiColombo (MPO + Mio) is scheduled to enter Mercury orbit in November 2026, with the DLR/Münster-built MERTIS infrared spectrometer to test the finding at far higher resolution. Cross-checked against the SD release, the MPS/mpg.de release carrying the researchers' own quotes, SciTechDaily, and the Crossref record for the DOI."
