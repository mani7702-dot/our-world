How a rocket launch painted a jellyfish in the sky

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 18:32 IST
Reading options

A SpaceX Falcon 9 lifted off for the Space Station at sunrise and lit up the eastern sky with a glowing shape that looked just like a jellyfish floating in space.

At six thirty-three in the morning, the sky over the eastern United States turned into a science-fiction picture.

A SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket rose from Cape Canaveral, heading to the International Space Station. As it climbed into the dark sky, its exhaust lit up from above — and suddenly everyone looking out their window saw something that looked like a giant jellyfish floating high above the trees.

Why did it look like a jellyfish?

It happens on rockets that launch near sunrise or sunset. Down on the ground it is still dark, but way up high — thirty, forty, fifty kilometres up — the sun is already shining. So sunlight reaches the rocket's exhaust and makes the spreading gas shine like a torch.

The hot gas pours out and fans outward, making a glowing, umbrella-shaped "bell" just like a jellyfish's head. Then thinner trails stretch down beneath it like the creature's trailing tentacles. From the ground it looks unreal: a living thing drifting across the sky instead of a machine leaving Earth.

What was the rocket carrying?

This was SpaceX's 35th cargo run to the Space Station for NASA — called CRS-35. The Dragon capsule carried more than 6,300 pounds of food, supplies, and science experiments.

One of the most exciting items was the final set of new solar panels for the ISS — known as IROSAs. These will help power the space station for crews living and working above us.

There were also experiments that study how the human body changes in space. Tiny "organoid" models of heart tissue and brain cells float in microgravity, letting scientists watch how they grow without the pull of gravity. Researchers hope these studies will help doctors back on Earth understand heart disease and brain conditions like Alzheimer's.

Try this at home

You can see the same glow effect with a flashlight in a dark room! Shine a flashlight upward toward the ceiling while lying on the floor. Watch how the beam spreads out and creates a glowing cone — just like a rocket's exhaust plume lighting up the sky.

Fun fact

The "jellyfish" shape has a real name in astronomy: it is similar to what scientists call a "noctilucent cloud" — night-shining clouds that form 80 kilometres above Earth and glow silver-blue when sunlight hits them after sunset.

Every launch is different, and so is every glow. But the feeling is the same: the simple, quiet wonder of watching a machine leave our world and carry human curiosity with it.

For grown-ups

On 13 October 2026, SpaceX's Falcon 9 launched from Cape Canaveral SLC-40 at 6:33 a.m. EDT on the CRS-35 mission, the 35th commercial resupply flight to the International Space Station. As the rocket climbed into the dark morning sky, sunlight hit its expanding exhaust plumes high in the atmosphere, making them glow like an illuminated jellyfish bell with trailing tentacles. This "rocket jellyfish" effect is common during sunrise/sunset launches because the ground is still dark while the high-altitude plume is in sunlight. The cargo Dragon carried 6,322 lb (2,868 kg) of supplies and experiments: the final set of ISS Roll-Out Solar Arrays (IROSAs) to boost station power, LambdaVision-1 artificial retina research, Stellar Spheroids 3D heart tissue models, MINDS brain organoid studies for neurodegenerative disease research, and ELF-Unconventional Glass 2 studying how glass forms in microgravity.
