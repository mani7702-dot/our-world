How a giant wasps' nest took over a wildlife reserve office for four months

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 19:42 IST
Reading options

A paper wasps' nest grew so big in a Norfolk nature reserve office that it took over the shelves — and the manager had to dismantle it layer by layer.

How a giant wasps' nest took over a wildlife reserve office for four months

When you share your office with 10,000 roommates

Imagine coming back from a walk, opening your office door, and finding thousands of tiny roommates have moved in — and built a house the size of a paperback book right on your bookshelf.

That is exactly what happened to Eilish Rothney, a nature reserve manager at the Norfolk Wildlife Trust's Trinity Broads site in eastern England. When she returned from doing wildlife surveys in June, she found wasps buzzing around her office — and a nest had been growing on her shelves for who knows how long.

A paper mansion made of wood pulp

Wasps do not build nests out of mud like beeswax. Instead, paper wasps chew up tiny bits of wood — from old fences, trees, even cardboard — mix it with their own saliva, and create a papery material that hardens into a nest.

Over four months, Rothney's office nest grew from the size of a paperback book to something much larger, wrapping around binders and files. She said there were dozens of wasps coming and going — probably 10s or 20s at any one time — all going about their business on her office shelves.

The slow dismantling

By autumn, the wasps had left their nest behind — as all wasp colonies do in the UK when the weather turns cold. The queen dies, and the colony ends its yearly cycle from spring to autumn.

Rothney waited until she was sure no wasps were home, then carefully began taking the nest apart. She cut away layer after layer, amazed to find hexagonal cells — the same perfect six-sided shape that honeybees use — where larvae would have developed.

"It's kind of a fitting tribute," Rothney said. "We've welcomed what many people see as a pest... and we've helped them survive, which will help this whole ecosystem that we rely on."

Why wasps matter more than you think

People often see wasps as pests, but they are actually important helpers in nature:

  • They eat aphids and other garden insects that damage plants
  • They pollinate flowers while hunting for food
  • Their larvae feed on dead insects, cleaning up the environment

Rothney said some people are surprised that a nature reserve would welcome wasps, but every creature has a role to play. "They have a really, really important role to play, as do all the insects."

Try this at home

You can make your own mini wasp nest model using paper! Tear up some newspaper into small strips, mix flour and water into a paste, and layer the strips over a small balloon. Let it dry, then pop the balloon. You will have a paper sphere — just like a real wasp nest, but made of recycled paper!

Fun fact

A single wasp colony can contain up to 8,000 wasps at its peak! The queen lays 200 to 300 eggs every single day during the summer. But each colony only lives for one year — the queen starts it in spring, tends to it all summer, and the nest is abandoned by autumn.
