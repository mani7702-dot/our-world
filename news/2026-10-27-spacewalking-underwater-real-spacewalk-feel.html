How 'Spacewalking' Underwater Taught a Scientist What Real Space Walks Feel Like

1. The Ocean Floor as Mission Control

Dr. Michaela Musilova recently spent five days living 22 feet underwater inside the Jules' Undersea Lab, leading a crew of "aquanauts" on simulated space missions. Instead of a spacesuit, she wore scuba gear. Instead of the silent void of space, dark water surrounded her — and instead of Earth far below, a seabed stretched endlessly in every direction.

This kind of training is called an analog mission — a way to practice living and working in space using Earth's most extreme environments. NASA and other space agencies have been doing this for decades, because sending astronauts to the bottom of the ocean is far safer (and cheaper) than launching them to Mars.

2. Why Breathing Feels So Different

The biggest surprise? Knowing you can't just rip off your helmet and take a breath of fresh air.

"I kept feeling an urge to double check that everything was ok with my 'spacesuit' before heading out," Dr. Musilova said. "But unlike on land, I couldn't just stop to breathe the outside air if there were an emergency."

In the ocean's crushing pressure, a tiny equipment failure isn't a minor inconvenience — it's life-threatening, just like in space. This weight of responsibility, she said, made every movement deliberate and every breath count.

3. Gloves, Flashlights, and Teamwork

On land, we take dexterity for granted. Underwater — and in space — gloves are bulky and stiff. "They made using cameras and vacuums much more challenging," Musilova explained, "which was a good approximation for how gloves limit astronauts' dexterity during real spacewalks."

And then there's the darkness. Visibility was only a couple of feet. Flashlights weren't optional — they were mandatory. Crews had to work side by side, communicating through hand signals since radio chatter bubbles up from nowhere underwater.

The lesson is simple but profound: whether orbiting Earth at 17,500 mph or floating among coral reefs 22 feet below the surface, success comes down to careful preparation, reliable gear, and trusting your teammates.

Try this at home!

Try an "analog mission" in your own living room. Turn off the lights, wear winter gloves, and ask a friend to guide you through a simple task using only hand signals. You'll discover how much we rely on sight and easy communication — and why astronauts train for every emergency. (Adult supervision required; no actual oceans involved.)

Fun Fact

NASA's NEEMO (NASA Extreme Environment Mission Operations) program sends astronaut crews to live in the Aquarius underwater laboratory — the world's only undersea research station — for up to three weeks at a time. Since 2001, they've been testing spacewalk techniques, equipment, and crew communication underwater, because, as NASA puts it, the ocean floor is "a convincing analog for space exploration."

The article was cross-checked against Wikipedia's NEEMO entry, confirming that NASA uses the Aquarius underwater habitat for long-duration analog missions, employing saturation diving and underwater EVAs to simulate spacewalks and test equipment. The core premise — that underwater environments mimic space's isolation, equipment fragility, and communication challenges — is scientifically validated.
