"Can a laser smaller than a dust speck make computers use half the energy?"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at 14:40 IST
Reading options

"Inside every computer, data races around tiny circuits as electric currents — and that makes heat. Scientists in Denmark have built a nanolaser so small that thousands could sit on one chip, letting computers talk with light instead."

Can a laser smaller than a dust speck make computers use half the energy?

Right now, your phone, laptop, or a giant data centre is doing something very old-fashioned: pushing tiny electric charges around metal wires to move information. Electricity is fast — but it gets hot, and heat is wasted energy.

Scientists in Denmark have just built something that could change that: a nanolaser so small that you could fit thousands of them on a single microchip. Their idea is simple and bold — let computers talk with light instead of electricity.

Light already does the heavy lifting

You might not realise it, but light is already carrying almost everything you do online. When you load a video or send a message, that data usually travels through fibre-optic cables as pulses of light, racing across countries in a blink.

But here is the catch: the moment that light reaches a computer, it gets converted into electrical signals. Inside the chip, everything still moves as electrons through wires. That is where the heat and the slowdowns come from.

So the big goal is to bring light onto the chip itself — and for that, you need thousands of tiny, reliable light sources. Ordinary lasers are far too big.

Squeezing light into an impossibly small space

The team at the Technical University of Denmark (DTU) built a laser so compact it can be embedded directly into a microchip.

The trick is a structure called a nanocavity — a cleverly shaped trap that squeezes light into a region far smaller than anyone once thought possible. When the laser is switched on with a beam of light, both the light and the electrons inside become trapped together in that tiny space. This lets the laser work at room temperature while using very little energy.

"It opens up the possibility of creating a new generation of components that combine high performance with minimal size," says DTU professor Jesper Mørk.

Why this matters for your devices

If chips could send data with light instead of electricity, they could run faster and cooler. One estimate from the research team is that this approach could cut a computer's energy use by about half — a huge deal for phones that run out of battery and for the enormous data centres that power the internet.

Photonics — the science of using light particles, called photons, to carry and process information — is behind this whole idea. It is the same technology family that already powers fibre-optic internet and some medical sensors.

The next puzzle

There is still one big challenge to solve. So far, the team's nanolaser is powered by another light beam. To be truly useful, it needs to run on electricity — plug it in and let it glow. That is the hard problem the researchers are tackling next.

Even so, shrinking a laser to this scale is a real milestone. Big changes in computing often start with something very, very small.

Try this at home

  1. See light carry a signal. Shine a torch at a wall and cover it with your hand, then uncover it. You just sent a "message" with light — that is how fibre-optic cables work, thousands of times per second.
  2. Feel the heat of electricity. Ask a grown-up to let you gently feel the bottom of a laptop that has been on for a while. That warmth is electricity turning into waste heat — exactly what light-based chips are designed to avoid.
  3. Squeeze a beam. Shine a torch on a wall and move it closer. The bright spot shrinks and gets brighter — a simple version of what a nanocavity does to trap light.

Fun Fact

The word laser is actually an acronym: it stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. The first laser was built back in 1960 — and now engineers can carve one smaller than a speck of dust.

For grown-ups

"Researchers at the Technical University of Denmark (DTU) have demonstrated a nanolaser with 'extreme dielectric confinement', published in Science Advances (DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.adx3865) in February 2026. The device confines both photons and excited carriers inside a dielectric nanobridge — a light-trapping 'nanocavity' — producing a subdiffraction-limited mode volume and a subwavelength carrier volume without lateral quantum confinement. This suppresses carrier surface recombination, enabling continuous-wave lasing at room temperature with a substantially reduced lasing threshold. It is built in DTU Nanolab, with the nanocavity design originally developed by Professor Ole Sigmund's group at DTU Construct. Professor Jesper Mørk (DTU Electro) co-authored with Drs. Meng Xiong and Yi Yu, and estimates the technology could roughly halve computer energy consumption. A key remaining challenge is powering the nanolaser electrically rather than with an external light beam. Photons already carry data across the internet in fibre-optic cables; the goal now is bringing that same light-based signalling onto the chip itself."
