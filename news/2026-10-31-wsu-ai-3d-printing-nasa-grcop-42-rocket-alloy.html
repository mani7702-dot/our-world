Can AI Find the One-in-100-Million Setting to Print Rocket Metal?

Imagine a recipe with 100 million possible combinations of temperature, speed, and power — where almost all of them fail. That is the challenge engineers face when 3D-printing a special metal alloy used inside rocket engines. Now, a clever computer program has cracked it in just 40 tries.

The metal that refuses to be printed

The alloy is called GRCop-42. It is made of three metals — copper, chromium, and niobium — and NASA developed it for one brutal job: staying strong while getting extremely hot, and moving heat away fast. That makes it perfect for the combustion chambers of liquid rocket engines, where fuels burn at fierce temperatures.

There is just one problem. Printing GRCop-42 layer by layer with a laser is expensive and fussy. It normally needs very powerful lasers, and roughly 90% of ordinary commercial metal printers simply cannot handle it.

"Sometimes they printed a certain configuration, and the product just melted," said Azza Fadhel, a PhD student who led the experiments.

Testing settings one at a time would be hopeless. A single print run costs hundreds of dollars, and checking the finished part can take days.

Teaching a computer to guess smartly

So a team at Washington State University tried a different approach. They started with results from 37 printing attempts that had already failed, and let an AI model learn from them.

The model then recommended small batches of new settings to try — balancing "promising" ideas with "risky but informative" ones. The researchers would print those, feed the results back, and the AI would get smarter each round.

Out of more than 100 million possibilities, the team found six successful configurations — using only 40 experiments in three months. For the first time, they printed GRCop-42 with a laser of just 500 watts.

"Given that we were able to find these feasible process parameters, it allows us to use those commercial printers, and we are essentially democratizing the printing of this alloy," said Jana Doppa, who led the research.

Why it matters

Lower-power printing means less energy used, less wear on machines, and cheaper parts — and it opens the doors for small labs, universities, and companies that could never afford giant industrial printers. Even better, the same AI trick could help scientists tackle other problems where only a few attempts out of millions actually work.

Try this at home

Grown-up helper needed! Take a paper cup and try to balance it on a pencil, then on three pencils, then on a flat base. Each time you change the setup you get a new result. Engineers do the same thing — but instead of testing every idea, a computer helps them guess which change to try next.

Fun fact: Copper moves heat away so well that GRCop-42 can survive temperatures hot enough to melt ordinary metals — and its name is short for "Glenn Research Center Copper," after the NASA lab that invented it.

For grown-ups

"Researchers at Washington State University (School of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science and School of Mechanical and Materials Engineering) used an AI-guided adaptive experimental design to find printable process parameters for GRCop-42, a NASA-developed copper-chromium-niobium alloy used in liquid rocket engine combustion chambers. Of more than 100 million possible laser-printing configurations, the team identified six successful ones using only 40 experiments over three months, starting from 37 failed configurations. For the first time they printed GRCop-42 at just 500 watts of laser power, which previously required far higher wattages available only on specialised machines. Around 90% of commercial metal printers could not print the alloy before; the new parameters widen access to ordinary commercial equipment. The work was published in the Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence (DOI 10.1609/aaai.v40i47.41428) and received the Innovative Deployed Application Award. Lead researcher: Jana Doppa (Huie-Rogers Endowed Chair Professor of Computer Science); first author: Azza Fadhel."
