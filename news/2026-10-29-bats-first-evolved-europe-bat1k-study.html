"Where Did Bats First Evolve? Europe, Say Scientists Studying Ancient DNA"

Where Did Bats First Evolve? Europe, Say Scientists Studying Ancient DNA

A Mystery Hundreds of Years Old

For a long time, nobody could agree on where bats first appeared on Earth. Some scientists thought bats began in Africa. Others guessed Asia. A few pointed to North America. But now, an international team of 137 researchers from 64 countries has solved the mystery using a brand-new approach: comparing the DNA of every living bat family with clues from ancient fossils.

The answer? Bats first evolved in Europe — around 65 million years ago.

The Bat1K Project: Sequencing Every Bat

The team worked through something called the Bat1K consortium, a global effort to sequence the genomes of all living bat species. In their landmark study (published in the journal Nature), scientists examined 103 bat genomes, including 42 brand-new, chromosome-level assemblies. They also studied 44 bat fossils to build the most complete "family tree" of bats ever made.

Here is what they discovered: after bats first appeared in Europe, their descendants spread to Africa, then on to the Americas, Asia, and Australia. Each new home gave bats a chance to evolve new features — and today, there are over 1,400 different bat species worldwide, from the tiny bumblebee bat (smaller than your thumb!) to the giant flying fox that can stretch its wings over 5 feet wide.

Flight and Echolocation: Born Together

Bats are special for two big reasons: they were the first mammals to evolve true powered flight, and most of them can navigate using echolocation — sending out sounds and listening for echoes to "see" in the dark.

By studying an ancient fossil bat called Vielasia, scientists found that echolocation had already appeared near the very start of bat evolution. This means both powered flight and echolocation emerged early — probably in prehistoric Europe — giving bats their remarkable evolutionary edge.

Think of it like this: while other mammals were still stuck on the ground, bats took to the sky and developed their own built-in sonar all at the same time. No wonder they became one of the most successful mammal groups on Earth, making up about one in every five mammal species!

Why Bats Matter More Than You Think

Bats are not just interesting — they are essential. A single bat can eat up to 1,000 insects in one hour, helping control pests that damage crops. Some bats spread seeds and pollinate plants, including the cacao tree that makes chocolate! Many bat species are also unusually resistant to disease and can live much longer than other mammals of similar size — a trait that scientists hope could eventually help with human medicine.

Try This at Home

Fill a small bowl with water and drop a few peas in. Close one eye and try to find the peas using only a straw as a "beak." Move slowly and carefully, just like a bat using echolocation to find food. Can you find all the "insects" before they "escape"?

Fun Fact

Bats are the only mammals known to have evolved true powered flight — no other living mammal can match their aerial agility. The smallest bat is the bumblebee bat (Craseonycteris thonglongyai), which fits on your fingertip. The largest is the flying fox, with a wingspan of up to 5 feet 7 inches!
