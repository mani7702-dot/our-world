A 12-foot shark got stuck in a city canal — and the internet made it famous

19 October 2026
Reading options

A bronze whaler shark chased its lunch into a canal in South Korea and could not find its way out. Then 600,000 people came to wave.

A big shark swam into a city in South Korea this September, and it is now the most famous animal in the country.

Its name is Bukangi (boo-KANG-ee). It is a bronze whaler shark about 12 feet long, and it was spotted on September 18 circling a narrow canal in Bukhang Port, in the southern port city of Busan. The nickname comes from that canal's name, plus a cuddly-sounding Korean ending.

Experts think the shark chased prey into the waterway, took a wrong turn, and got stuck. Waterways are confusing like that.

The rescue that did not work

City workers did not give up easily. They aimed high-pressure streams of water behind the shark to herd it out — videos showed Bukangi calmly dodging the jets. A net-assisted attempt was planned next.

Moving a 12-foot shark in a shallow, narrow canal turned out to be far too risky. Sedation and dragging could injure or kill it. So the rescue went quiet, and the watching carried on.

Six hundred thousand visitors

The crowds came anyway. Reuters and the Associated Press counted about 600,000 people in the first two weeks — at a spot that normally sees around 2,000 visitors on a weekend. Four days of a public holiday alone brought roughly 480,000 of them. Later reports put the running total past a million.

Then the city did something unusual: it ran an official Instagram poll. About 8,000 people voted, and 91 percent said yes — so Bukangi was appointed Busan's honorary promotional ambassador.

A shark. Ambassador of a city of 3.3 million people.

Bronze whalers, briefly

Bronze whaler sharks are not maneaters. They eat fish, they are found along coasts in warm and temperate seas, and they are named for the bronze shine on their backs. They can grow longer than a small car and they are strong swimmers.

If you ever see one from a boat in the wild, it is a lucky day — sharks out in the ocean almost never bother people.

What happens next?

Officials are still working out a plan to lead Bukangi back to deeper water, and some experts warn that a long stay in a shallow canal could damage the shark's navigation. Meanwhile the watch continues, the memes multiply, and the shark — which has survived 400 million years of ocean — now has a job title.
