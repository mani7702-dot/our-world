"Where does Earth's oxygen come from? Scientists find a hidden reservoir deep underground"

By Our World Published 29 October 2026 at 04:30 IST
Reading options

"Deep beneath your feet, molten rock may be quietly stockpiling oxygen — and some of that hidden stash may have helped fill our skies with the air we breathe."

Where does Earth's oxygen come from? Scientists find a hidden reservoir deep underground

When you breathe out on a cold morning and see your breath, you are sharing something ancient: the oxygen in the air around you did not always exist in our skies. For billions of years Earth's atmosphere had almost no free oxygen at all. Then, slowly, something began filling the world with the very air we need today.

Scientists used to think that oxygen came almost entirely from tiny sea creatures called cyanobacteria — microscopic plants that bubble out oxygen as they photosynthesize. And those do play the biggest role. But now a new discovery shows that some of our planet's oxygen may have been made — and stored — far below our feet, inside rivers of molten rock.

1. The surprising secret inside molten rock

Imagine a pot of thick, bubbling soup. Now imagine that soup could quietly soak up extra of one of its ingredients — more salt, more sugar — even though every recipe said it was already full.

That is what magma (molten rock) appears to do with oxygen.

For a long time, scientists believed that the oxygen locked inside magma was fixed: it could only be as much as the other ingredients (silicon, magnesium, iron) allowed. Oxygen was treated like a side dish, not something magma could carry on its own.

But a team of researchers testing magma under crushing pressure found something odd. They heated a fake chunk of mid-ocean-ridge basalt — the kind of rock born where tectonic plates pull apart beneath the oceans — and squeezed it to 7 gigapascals of pressure. That is about 69,000 times the air pressure you feel right now at sea level.

At that depth, they discovered, the molten rock soaked up far more oxygen than it had any right to — more than 5 percent of the sample's weight was excess oxygen that no textbook predicted.

2. A deep-Earth oxygen vault

The big question: was the extra oxygen really inside the rock, or just tiny bubbles trapped in it?

To find out, the scientists used a tool called Raman spectroscopy — a way of identifying substances by watching how they dance with light. The results were clear: there were no oxygen bubbles. The extra oxygen had genuinely dissolved into the molten rock itself.

They also ran computer simulations — giant number-crunching models that act like virtual atoms — and sure enough, the more pressure they added, the more oxygen the magma could hold. It behaves a lot like water or carbon dioxide: harder to hold at the surface, easier to swallow deep underground.

So here is the picture: miles down beneath volcanoes and mid-ocean ridges, magma quietly bottles up extra oxygen the way a deep-sea sponge soaks up water.

3. Could volcanoes have breathed life into Earth's atmosphere?

That raises a thrilling possibility. As oxygen-heavy magma rises toward the surface, the pressure drops — and just like a shaken soda losing its fizz, the extra oxygen could escape.

Where would it go? Some might rise into the crust, some into the air. The scientists noticed something remarkable: between about 2.5 billion and 2.0 billion years ago, Earth's oxygen levels surged in what scientists call the Great Oxidation Event. That same window saw the birth of some of Earth's largest volcanic outpourings — vast regions of lava the size of continents, called large igneous provinces.

Coincidence? Maybe not. The team calculated that if ancient magma erupted and released just 0.27 percent extra oxygen by weight, it could in theory match all the oxygen currently floating in our atmosphere. That is a bold "could" — not proof that volcanoes did it, but proof that even a small deep-Earth contribution might have had enormous consequences.

For the first time, oxygen joins the short list of invisible passengers that magma can carry between Earth's hidden interior and the world above.

Try this at home

Grab a clear soda or sparkling water and a still one. Open them both at the same time and listen. The bubbly one hisses — that gas was forced into the liquid under pressure and pops out the moment the pressure drops. That is a little like what may happen when oxygen-rich magma rises and the great weight of rock above lightens up. Can you think of other things that stay hidden under pressure but burst out when the squeeze is released?

Fun Fact

Earth is the only planet in our solar system with an atmosphere that is 21 percent oxygen. Mars and Venus have almost none. If magma really did help ferry oxygen from our deep interior to the skies, it may be one of the secrets that made our world uniquely alive.

For grown-ups

"For curious grown-ups: Researchers at HPSTAR (Center for High Pressure Science and Technique Advanced Research, China) and international collaborators (Northwestern Polytechnical University, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam) published in National Science Review (DOI: 10.1093/nsr/nwag539) that magma at 7 gigapascals (≈69,000× surface pressure) and 1200–1800 °C can absorb 'excess oxygen' — extra O beyond what its normal chemistry predicts — accounting for >5% of the melt's weight. Raman spectroscopy confirmed the oxygen was dissolved into the glass structure, not trapped as bubbles. Deep neural-network simulations reproduced the pressure-dependent solubility, showing oxygen joins the melt until an equilibrium balance is reached and that some atoms form peroxide-like (O–O) bonds. The authors propose that when this oxygen-rich deep magma rises and pressure drops, the excess oxygen is released, potentially oxidizing the upper mantle and crust — and maybe even contributing to the atmospheric oxygen rise 2.5–2.0 billion years ago that Wikipedia documents as the Great Oxidation Event, coinciding with Earth's largest large igneous provinces (LIPs). Even a 0.27% oxygen release from erupted magma, they estimate, could in theory match the entire mass of oxygen currently in Earth's atmosphere. This gives planetary scientists a possible new term in models of how rocky worlds like our own (and Mars, Venus) evolve atmospheres. Wikipedia's 'Great Oxidation Event' confirms the atmospheric oxygen rise peaked 2.06–2.46 Ga from cyanobacterial photosynthesis; the new paper adds a deep-Earth delivery mechanism as an intriguing complement."
