5 Secrets Hiding in Elizabeth I's Angriest Letters

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 14:22 IST
Reading options

>-

A letter that could scorch paper

On a winter day in February 1586, England's Queen Elizabeth I sat down to write one of the most furious letters in British history. Her target? Her former favorite, Robert Dudley — a man she had raised up "above any other subject of this land."

But here is the sneaky part: the only surviving copy of that letter is not in the queen's own handwriting. It was written by a secretary named Francis Mylles, who worked for Elizabeth's principal secretary, Sir Francis Walsingham (yes, the same spymaster who uncovered the Babington Plot).

So who was really speaking when Elizabeth seemed to rage?

Three clues from 500 letters

A historian named Nadine Akkerman studied more than 500 of Elizabeth's letters — part of a project called "Feathers" at Leiden University in the Netherlands. Here is what she discovered.

1. Elizabeth collaborated, not dictated. Most of the 3,000 surviving letters were "scribal letters" — typed out by clerks, not the queen herself. Far from being carbon copies, they were teamwork.

2. Secretaries amplified the drama. The analysis shows that Elizabeth's advisers did not just copy her words — they crafted them. They sharpened the tone, made grief sound griefier, and anger sound angrier. A letter that began as a polite reminder could leave the desk sounding like a royal thunderclap.

3. We read Elizabeth through their filter. Because the secretaries polished the queen's voice, historians have spent centuries mistaking the secretaries' flair for Elizabeth's own temper. The "voice" we imagine — cool, calculating, terrifying — may partly belong to Walsingham's clerks.

The Dudley letter, line by line

Elizabeth's 1586 rebuke to Dudley survives in the hand of her secretary. The opening line still stings:

"How contemptuously we conceive ourself to have been used by you."

That "we" — the royal "we" — was not always Elizabeth speaking alone. Sometimes it was her. Sometimes it was her secretaries, shaping the most powerful woman in Europe into whoever England needed her to be on any given Tuesday.

Try this at home

Find an old letter in your house (a grandparent's note, a postcard from a trip). Ask an adult to help you write your own reply — using their handwriting style. Then swap. Notice how the same message can sound sharp, sweet, or stern just by changing the hand that holds the pen.

Fun fact

Elizabeth's secretaries literally held the keys to the kingdom — and to her tone. The surviving letter to Dudley is signed "E" for Eliza, but the body? That was Francis Mylles's flourish.

For grown-ups

Historians found that Elizabeth's male secretaries often rewrote her letters to sound more dramatic. The queen and her team ran a 16th-century royal communications office from the Palace of Whitehall.
