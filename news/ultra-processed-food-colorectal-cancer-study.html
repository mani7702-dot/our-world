Massive study of 460,000 people challenges ultra-processed food cancer fears

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 14:30 IST
Reading options

A 19-year study of 461,000 Americans found that ultra-processed foods don't raise colorectal cancer risk overall — but some types matter more than others.

Why some ultra-processed foods look harmless and others don't

The 460,000-person question

Americans get more than half their daily calories from ultra-processed foods — things like sugary cereals, frozen pizza, packaged snacks, and yes, even some granola bars. That has made people nervous, especially when it comes to colorectal cancer, one of the most common cancers.

But here is the thing: "ultra-processed" is a big umbrella. A bowl of sugary Frosted Flakes and a fortified whole-grain cereal bar sit under the same label, even though their ingredients and effects are nothing alike.

So does eating more ultra-processed food raise your cancer risk? A new study of over 460,000 Americans set out to find one clear answer.

Tracking 19 years of health

The researchers pulled data from the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study, one of the largest ongoing nutrition studies in the world. They started with 461,682 adults aged 50 to 71 who filled in detailed diet questionnaires in the mid-1990s. Then they watched for nearly 19 years (through 2018), seeing which people developed colorectal cancer.

The headline result:

  • People who ate more ultra-processed food were NOT more likely to get colorectal cancer overall.

That already challenges a lot of headlines that treat all processed food as automatically harmful.

The twist: not all ultra-processed foods are the same

When the team broke ultra-processed foods into groups, the picture sharpened:

  • Ultra-processed whole grains, dairy, and calcium were linked to lower cancer rates.
  • Ultra-processed meats (think hot dogs, deli slices, frozen chicken nuggets) stayed linked to higher rates.
  • Swapping even 10% of ultra-processed food for less-processed alternatives was tied to a 2% lower risk.

The message the researchers hope people take away: judging a food by how "processed" it is tells you almost nothing. What matters is what is actually in it — and the balance of nutrients matters far more than the processing label.

What this means for your plate

The study, published October 15, 2026 in PLOS Medicine, lines up with long-standing advice: fill up on whole grains, fruit, vegetables, and calcium-rich foods, and keep processed meat to a minimum. The new data simply says that the ultra-processed label itself is a blunt tool — some of those foods help and some hurt.

As one of the authors put it: the nutritional contents, not the degree of processing, should guide our choices.

Try this at home

Look at three foods in your kitchen and check their ingredient lists. Which ones have a short list of things you recognize (like oats, milk, fruit)? Which ones have long lists with words you do not understand? Processing level is not magic — it is just what happens between the farm and the package.

Fun fact

"Ultra-processed" is a term defined by the NOVA classification system: any industrial formulation with five or more ingredients, including things like flavor enhancers, colors, or preservatives you would not usually have in your kitchen. A store-bought blueberry muffin made with artificial blueberries and emulsifiers? Ultra-processed. A plain rolled-oats-and-banana bowl? Not.

For grown-ups

The short version for curious grown-ups: this is an observational cohort study (the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study), not a medical trial — it can spot links, not prove cause and effect. Researchers followed 461,682 Americans aged 50-71 for 18.8 years (1995-2018) and found no overall link between ultra-processed food intake and colorectal cancer. The nuance is real: processed meats stayed linked to higher risk, while ultra-processed whole grains, dairy, and calcium stayed linked to lower risk. Published Oct 15, 2026 in PLOS Medicine (DOI 10.1371/journal.pmed.1005251).
