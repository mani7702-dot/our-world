How a UK estate found 5,242 species — even during a drought

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 23:14 IST
Reading options

At Wimpole Estate in Cambridgeshire, England, conservationists recorded more than 5,000 different plants and animals over just six years — including rare bats, newts, and birds — even as southern England faced its worst drought in decades. The secret? Working with nature, not against it.

How a UK estate found 5,242 species — even during a drought

Most of the news about drought sounds gloomy: empty reservoirs, dry crops, brown lawns. But at Wimpole Estate in Cambridgeshire, England, a group of conservationists proved something different — when you give nature the right space and care, life finds a way to thrive.

1. A wildlife jackpot in one of England's driest spots

Wimpole sits in Cambridgeshire, a county that — along with much of southern England — was hit hard by severe drought in 2026. Yet between 2021 and this year, the National Trust recorded an incredible 5,242 different species living across the estate's parkland, farmland, woodland, and wetlands. That is more species at one farm than most people will ever see in a lifetime!

Among the finds: rare barbastelle bats (one of the UK's most elusive mammals), great crested newts (protected by law), corn buntings (a bird whose name sounds like it belongs in a cereal box), and even a tiny pygmy ant that shows up at only one other place in the whole country.

2. How did they do it?

David Hassall, the estate's general manager, explained that the answer is simple: "nature can flourish alongside farming, public access and heritage conservation." Over five years the team planted 125,000 trees and created 35 hectares of new woodland plus 52 hectares of wood pasture — a type of grazing land that lets trees and grass grow together.

The key idea is called habitat diversity. Instead of having one big field or one big forest, Wimpole mixes many different homes: a pond for frogs, a meadow for bees, a grove of young trees for birds, and a patch of wildflowers for butterflies. Every type of plant and animal likes different things, so having more habitats means more visitors.

3. Thriving despite the dry

You might expect a drought to make things harder for wildlife. But the estate's head of nature conservation, Ben McCarthy, says that's exactly why places like Wimpole matter more than ever. When one area gets dry, the mixed habitats act like a safety net — if the meadow dries up, the pond still holds water; if the field cracks, the woodland stays cool.

"It reflects five decades of careful stewardship," Hassall said. "And it shows that nature can flourish even under pressure."

The estate is now planning 77 more hectares of tree planting — because they learned something important: protecting nature is not just about leaving things alone. It's about actively building more homes for the creatures that share our world.

Try this at home

Walk around your neighborhood with a grown-up and keep a simple nature journal. Draw or list everything living you can find: a worm, a dandelion, a spider's web, a bird's nest. Try to spot at least 20 different living things. Then ask: what kinds of homes do they need? A worm wants soft, damp soil. A bird wants a high branch and some twigs. You don't need 5,000 species — but every garden, park, and schoolyard can be a tiny wildlife estate if you give it a chance.

Fun Fact: The pygmy ant that was found at Wimpole is so small that 20 of them lined up would fit on your little fingernail. It is one of the tiniest animals in the world! Scientists identify ants this small by their wing muscles and pollen baskets — not by eye, but with a magnifying glass.

For grown-ups

The Wimpole Estate in Cambridgeshire, England, managed by the National Trust, recorded 5,242 species across its parkland, farmland, woodland, and wetlands between 2021 and 2026. Rare species spotted include barbastelle bats, great crested newts, corn buntings, and a tiny pygmy ant found at only one other UK site. The estate planted 125,000 trees and created 35 hectares of woodland and 52 hectares of wood pasture over that period. This positive conservation result comes even as southern England faced severe drought conditions in 2026, with roughly 30 million people under hosepipe bans and reservoirs at exceptionally low levels (confirmed by BBC News drought report).
