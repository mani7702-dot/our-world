Why Did the Early Solar System Choose "Fire" Over Ice to Build Its First Worlds?

By Our World Published 01 November 2026 at 10:40 IST
Reading options

"When the Solar System was brand new, it had two flavours of building material to choose from — hot, rocky droplets and cold, icy dust. A new study shows it picked the hot stuff almost every time."

The Solar System's first shopping list

Imagine you are building a planet, and the shop only sells two things. The first is a bag of tiny, round, sun-baked rocks. The second is a heap of cold, dusty ice mixed with bits of organic (carbon-rich) material.

Which would you pick?

About 4.6 billion years ago, the young Solar System faced exactly this choice as it began assembling its first solid bodies — the small "planetesimals" that would grow into planets, moons and asteroids.

Two flavours of cosmic material

The hot option is called chondrules — millimetre-sized spheres of rock that were melted at high temperatures in the swirling cloud of gas and dust around the newborn Sun. The cold option is called matrix: a fine, fluffy dust full of water ice and organic molecules.

Both were floating around in the same huge disk. So which one actually got used?

The rocks won — and quickly

A team led by Damanveer Grewal at Yale University found the answer by studying the chemistry of ancient iron meteorites, which preserve the fingerprints of the earliest planetesimals in the outer Solar System.

Their result was striking: those first bodies were built from 83% to 92% chondrules, with only a little of the icy dust. "This assembly process was remarkably selective from the very beginning," Grewal said.

And it happened fast. Earlier studies could only show this sorting in bodies that formed 2 to 4 million years after the Solar System began. This new work pushes it back to the first million years.

Space as a giant sorting machine

How does nature sort space dust? The same way a river sorts pebbles: the flow of material carries light, fluffy grains differently from dense, solid ones.

The disk's gas dragged particles around according to their size and density, funnelling the denser chondrules into the growing planetesimals while much of the icy matrix drifted elsewhere. Astronomers call this aerodynamic sorting, and it now looks like it was working from the very start.

Try this at home

Fill a clear jar with water and add a mix of sand and small gravel. Swirl it gently, then watch where the grains settle. The heavier pieces sink faster and separate from the lighter ones — the same idea that let flaming-hot rock droplets bunch together to build worlds.

Fun fact

Chondrules are so well preserved that you can hold one in your hand. They are found inside primitive meteorites called chondrites, which make up about 86% of all meteorites that land on Earth — little time capsules from the Solar System's fiery childhood.

For grown-ups

"For curious grown-ups: researchers led by Damanveer S. Grewal at Yale University studied the earliest planetesimals (the building blocks of planets) using two independent geochemical proxies — bulk sulfur content and iron valence state — in carbonaceous (CC) iron meteorite parent bodies, which formed less than 1–2 million years after the Solar System's start. Published 18 September 2026 in Nature Astronomy (DOI 10.1038/s41550-026-02976-6), the work shows these first outer-Solar-System bodies were built from 83% to 92% chondrules by mass, with very little of the volatile-rich, icy matrix that dominates later-forming objects. Chondrules are millimetre-sized igneous spheres formed at high temperatures in the protoplanetary disk; matrix is cold, fine-grained dust rich in water ice and organic material. The finding extends evidence for 'aerodynamic sorting' — the disk's gas dragging particles by size and density like a river sorting pebbles — back to the very first million years of planet formation. Co-authors are Zhongtian Zhang and Joanna Drążkowska."
