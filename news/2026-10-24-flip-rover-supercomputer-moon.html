"What is NASA sending to the Moon aboard a rover named FLIP?"

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 18:47 IST
Reading options

"A California space company is sending a rugged supercomputer and AI computer to the Moon's south pole this year — riding on a rover named FLIP."

What will a supercomputer and AI do on the Moon?

A robot rover carrying NASA's first space computer

Later this year, a California company called Astrolab is preparing to send a small robot rover to the Moon's south pole. The rover is named FLIP — short for Flexible Logistics and Exploration — and it is carrying something extraordinary: a real, working supercomputer with artificial intelligence built right in. If the mission succeeds, it will be the very first time a high-performance computer and AI system operate on another world.

Why bring a supercomputer to space at all?

You might wonder: why not just use the same kind of computer that sits in your home or backpack? The answer is that space is an unforgiving place. Extreme heat swings, freezing cold, radiation, and — on the Moon — long nights that last two weeks (about 300 hours of total darkness), make ordinary electronics fail.

FLIP's computer comes from HPE (Hewlett Packard Enterprise). It is called the Spaceborne Computer-4, the newest in a line that started with experiments on the International Space Station. This version is the first one ever built especially for the lunar surface. It is lightweight, compact, and fanless — no spinning fans to break. It has a low-power central processing unit (CPU) and, crucially, a GPU (graphics processing unit), which is what makes AI tasks possible. The computer is powered by a mix of batteries and solar cells on the rover, and it stores data on solid-state drives made by KIOXIA.

Braving the Moon's toughest neighborhood

The computer is built with heat-conductive aluminum and is designed specifically for the brutal conditions at the Moon's south pole — where water ice hides in permanently shadowed craters. NASA picked the south pole because it is where humans will eventually live and work, so learning how technology behaves there is key for the Artemis program, which aims to return astronauts to the Moon.

"NASA's given us a real challenge," said Jaret Matthews, Astrolab's founder and CEO. "We have to design for a full year on the lunar surface. That means surviving multiple lunar nights — upwards of 150 hours of complete darkness where we are not receiving energy or warmth from the Sun." The computer must also operate semi-autonomously for months at a time, since radio signals from Earth take more than a second each way and cannot reach the rover inside the deep, shadowed craters.

What will the AI actually do?

First, the mission will test how well the computer works in this extreme environment. But the Spaceborne Computer-4 will not just sit idle. It will analyze visual data captured on the mission, enable autonomous observations, and help with navigation, mapping, and terrain analysis. This means the rover can think for itself: recognizing rocks, planning routes, and choosing safe places to drive without waiting for instructions from Earth.

One clever experiment comes from NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) — the same team that built the rovers on Mars. They have developed a machine-learning system that lets FLIP figure out where it is by matching what it sees around it against a map stored in the rover's memory. This solves a big problem: there is no GPS on the Moon or on Mars. Instead of satellites, FLIP looks at the ground beneath it and matches the shapes of craters and hills to its digital map — like finding your way through a dark house by feeling the furniture.

A delivery truck for the Moon

FLIP is not just a computer delivery truck, though. It is also a platform that can carry different science instruments. As part of Astrobotic's Griffin Mission 1, FLIP shares the ride with other payloads: a small CubeRover, an optical navigation camera called LandCam-X, and even a tiny Galactic Library (Nanofiche GLPH) — a microscopic archive of human history and culture.

NASA's CLPS program (Commercial Lunar Payload Services) hires private companies like Astrobotic to deliver cargo to the Moon cheaply and regularly. The south pole is the chosen landing zone because that is where astronauts will eventually walk, so every piece of equipment tested there is building the foundation for a future lunar base.

Fun Fact

The FLIP rover's name is a play on words. In oceanography, a "FLIP" (Floating Instrument Platform) is a special buoy that tips upright in the water to study waves and currents. Astrolab borrowed the name because their rover is designed to be equally adaptable — able to carry many different types of science tools, just like the ocean buoy carries different instruments for different experiments.

For grown-ups

"Astrolab, a California-based space technology company founded by former NASA JPL engineer Jaret Matthews, is launching its FLIP (Flexible Logistics and Exploration) rover to the Moon's south pole as part of Astrobotic's Griffin Mission 1 under NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program. The rover carries HPE's Spaceborne Computer-4 — the third generation of HPE's spaceborne computing line (preceded by Spaceborne Computer flown to the ISS in 2017 and Spaceborne Computer-2 in 2021). The Spaceborne Computer-4 is the first computer purpose-built for the lunar surface, featuring a low-power 64-bit CPU and an AMD GPU, making it the first GPU and first AI hardware deployed on the Moon. The computer is fanless, built with heat-conductive aluminum, and runs on a hybrid power system combining batteries and solar cells. It also includes solid state drives from KIOXIA for memory storage. NASA selected Astrolab as one of two companies (alongside Lunar Outpost) in May 2026 to develop a Lunar Terrain Vehicle for the Artemis human landing program, with the FLIP rover serving as a commercial lunar mobility platform. The mission demonstrates how commercial lunar rovers can support technology development, autonomous operations, and scientific research in preparation for future human Moon bases. The JPL-developed localization software uses machine learning to match terrain features against stored maps, solving the challenge of navigation without GPS on airless bodies.
