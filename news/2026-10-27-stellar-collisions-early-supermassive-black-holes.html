Could crashing stars be the seeds of the universe's mightiest black holes?

A cosmic puzzle: black holes too big, too fast

When the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) peered back toward the dawn of time, it found a shocking sight: supermassive black holes—objects millions or billions of times heavier than our Sun—already chowing down at the centers of galaxies just 400 to 500 million years after the Big Bang. That is less than 4% of the universe's current age! Our best theories said these monsters needed much longer to grow, yet here they were, already enormous. Where did they come from?

When stars crash and merge into giants

One compelling answer comes from a new study led by Claire Williams, a doctoral student at UCLA. Her team ran supercomputer simulations of the dense star clusters that were common in the early universe—places where stars were packed close enough together that direct collisions were routine.

In these crowded environments, stars didn't just graze each other—they slammed together. Each collision merged two stars into one even bigger star, and over time, a process the team calls runaway sequential collisions built stars so massive they would have weighed in at 100 times the mass of our Sun or more. Scientists call these Very Massive Stars (VMS).

"Perhaps the strangest outcome of our simulations is that stars can merge repeatedly within the cluster until they form one gigantic star with a mass equal to that of an entire cluster," said Williams.

From giant star to baby black hole

When a regular star runs out of fuel, it explodes as a supernova and leaves behind either a neutron star or a black hole. But a Very Massive Star has a different fate. So monstrously heavy that it cannot explode outward, it collapses directly into itself, forming a black hole in a single, silent step—no fireworks, just gravity winning.

The team's simulations suggest that this process created intermediate-mass black holes in the early universe, surrounded by dense clusters of stars. These black holes then began gobbling gas at super-Eddington rates (faster than thought possible), growing rapidly into the supermassive black holes we see today.

The connection to "Little Red Dots"

JWST has observed mysterious objects the astronomy community calls "Little Red Dots"—compact, red-tinted smudges scattered across the early universe. Over 300 of these curious objects have been spotted, and most appear to be active galactic nuclei (AGN) with supermassive black holes at their hearts, rapidly pulling in gas.

The new research shows how stellar collisions could explain these dots: the crashes produced not just black holes, but also the gas clouds and stellar clusters around them—creating exactly the environment that makes a Little Red Dot glow red and compact from across the cosmos.

Why the early universe was perfect for collisions

In today's universe, stars are spread far apart. A collision between two Sun-like stars would be rarer than lightning striking the same spot twice on Earth. But in the early universe, young galaxies were packed with stars in dense clusters—so crowded that close encounters and physical collisions between stars were common as the research team explains:

"Observations and cosmological simulations indicate that the early universe hosted numerous compact, high-density stellar systems, where close encounters and physical collisions between stars were likely common."

Our own Milky Way's central black hole, Sagittarius A*, sits in just such a dense stellar environment today—and the team's model shows that once these very massive stars turn into black holes, the system resembles our own galactic center: a black hole surrounded by a dense star cluster.

Try this at home

Take two balloons and rub them together vigorously. When they touch, they stick—that's a tiny taste of how gravity binds colliding stars. Now imagine stacking 100 balloons into one super-balloon. That single balloon is like the Very Massive Star. Drop it, and it collapses in on itself. You just simulated a primordial black hole forming!

Fun Fact

The research is published in The Astrophysical Journal under the title "A Cosmological Framework for Stellar Collisions at High Redshift in Proto-globular Clusters, Nuclear Star Clusters, and Little Red Dots." The term Little Red Dot doesn't mean these objects are actually red or dot-shaped—they're simply compact and appear as faint red smudges in JWST's infrared vision, earning them this charming nickname in the astronomy community.

for-grownups: The study addresses the long-standing puzzle of how supermassive black holes (SMBHs) existed so early in the universe (400–500 Myr post-Big Bang) when standard growth models could not explain their mass. The proposed mechanism: in dense proto-globular clusters and nuclear star clusters of the early universe, repeated stellar collisions create Very Massive Stars (>100 M☉) that collapse directly into intermediate-mass black holes. These then grow via super-Eddington accretion, producing the Little Red Dot AGNs observed by JWST. The simulations used AREPO cosmological initial conditions with 1000 stars per cluster within 1 parsec. Wikipedia's "Little Red Dots" article confirms LRDs are red-tinted objects observed at z=6–10 (600 Myr post-Big Bang), originally identified as AGN with black holes, with the most direct evidence coming from Abell 2744-QSO1's ~50 million solar mass point mass (2026). Wikipedia's "Supermassive black hole" article confirms every large galaxy hosts an SMBH at its center. Sources: Universe Today (Claire Williams / UCLA, published in The Astrophysical Journal); Wikipedia "Little Red Dots"; Wikipedia "Supermassive black hole."
