Hera opened its eyes on an asteroid — and saw two

By Our World Published 28 October 2026 at 16:45 IST
Reading options

Two years after launch, Europe's Hera spacecraft has photographed its asteroid target as two separate objects for the first time — a big rock called Didymos and the small moon Dimorphos that NASA nudged in 2022.

Hera opened its eyes on an asteroid — and saw two

For two years, a spacecraft has been chasing a rock across the Solar System. Now it is close enough to see what it was chasing — and there are two of them.

The spacecraft is Hera, built by the European Space Agency (ESA). On the evening of 18 October 2026, it pointed its camera at its destination and took a picture. In that picture, the asteroid Didymos and its little companion Dimorphos appear as two separate dots in the dark, instead of one blurry speck.

At the time, Hera was 1,280 kilometres away from the pair. Earth was about 197 million kilometres behind it. That is like standing at one end of a very long beach and photographing two grains of sand at the other end.

Two rocks, one orbit

Didymos is the big one: about 780 metres across — a bit shorter than the world's tallest skyscraper is tall. Dimorphos is the small one: about 151 metres, roughly the size of the Great Pyramid of Giza.

Dimorphos circles Didymos like a moon circles a planet, about 1.15 kilometres out. It is in no hurry. It travels around its partner at around 18 centimetres per second — slower than a beetle walking across a kitchen floor.

In the new image, Dimorphos is barely one pixel wide. One pixel! And yet it is clearly a separate object, sitting off to the side of its bigger companion. Sometimes "we finally saw it" means exactly that: a single bright dot that was not there before.

Why this particular rock matters

In September 2022, NASA flew a spacecraft called DART straight into Dimorphos on purpose. It hit at about 6.1 kilometres per second, threw up a plume of dust and rubble that stretched some 10,000 kilometres into space, and changed the little asteroid's orbit by roughly half an hour.

That was the first time humans measurably moved a natural object in space. It is the closest thing we have to a practice run at planetary defence — the idea that if a dangerous asteroid were ever heading for Earth, we might be able to nudge it off course.

But a practice run only helps if you understand what happened. Did DART punch a crater into Dimorphos? Or did it reshape the whole asteroid? How heavy is Dimorphos, and what is it made of? The nudge worked better than many predictions, and scientists want to know why.

That is Hera's job: fly to the scene of the crash and look closely.

What happens next

Hera did not arrive by luck. Earlier in October, the mission team fired its thrusters twice, a week apart, slowing the spacecraft until it was drifting towards the asteroids at just 20 metres per second — about the speed of a fast bicycle. From there it can creep closer, step by step.

On 25 November, ESA plans to release the first really detailed pictures of Dimorphos. After that, Hera will send out two shoebox-sized CubeSats for an even closer look, and eventually the mission aims to land on the smaller asteroid.

One honest note: the 18 October picture is a milestone, not a masterpiece. It proves Hera's camera works and its aim is good. The pictures worth framing are still a month away.

Try this at home

Make your own double asteroid. You need a football (or a big cushion) and a marble, plus about a metre of string. Put the football on the floor — that is Didymos. Tie the marble to the string and hold the other end, then walk slowly around the football letting the marble swing in a circle at about a metre out. That is Dimorphos's orbit, scaled up.

Now time it. Dimorphos takes about 11 hours and 55 minutes for one lap around Didymos. In your model that is a very slow walk indeed — a full circle at beetle pace.

Be the camera. Stand across the room and look at a friend holding two small balls close together. From far away, they look like one blob. Walk closer and two shapes appear. That is exactly what happened to Hera — not because the asteroids changed, but because the spacecraft got close enough to tell them apart. Astronomers call that resolution: not more light, just finer detail.

For grown-ups

"Summary of ESA's Hera mission first resolved image of the (65803) Didymos system, released 27 October 2026. Hera's monochromatic Asteroid Framing Camera imaged the pair on the evening of 18 October 2026 from 1280 km, after two braking burns a week apart reduced the spacecraft's relative velocity to 20 m/s; Dimorphos subtends roughly one pixel but is clearly separated from Didymos. ESA gives Didymos 780 m diameter, Dimorphos 151 m, mean separation 1.15 km, and a relative orbital speed of about 18 cm/s. Context: NASA's DART impacted Dimorphos on 26 September 2022 at 6.1 km/s; the kinetic impact measurably shortened Dimorphos's orbital period (NASA's measured result is about 32 minutes, ESA quotes ~33) and produced a debris plume extending roughly 10,000 km — the first demonstration of a measurable orbital change of a Solar System body by human action. Open questions Hera was built to answer: whether the impact excavated a crater or globally reshaped the body, and Dimorphos's mass, internal structure and material properties (the observed momentum transfer exceeded pre-impact model predictions). ESA plans first detailed close-up images on 25 November 2026, followed by CubeSat release and eventually a landing. Framing caution: the 18 October frame is a resolution milestone, not a detailed image; no impact-risk claim is made anywhere in this piece. Cross-checked against the ESA release, ESA's 7 October 'Destination sighted' image release, contemporaneous trade coverage (EconoTimes, OrbitalHub) agreeing on all figures, and NASA's DART mission page for the 2022 impact date and launch."
