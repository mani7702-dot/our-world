Why Do the Universe's Earliest Galaxies Look So Strange?

The Puzzling First Galaxies

When the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) started taking pictures of the very early universe, astronomers got a surprise: the first galaxies looked weird. They were bigger, brighter, and more organized than scientists expected them to be at that young age. It's like finding a fully built house where you expected only a foundation.

Why the mismatch? The answer may lie in the massive stars that lit up those early galaxies — and how differently they behaved compared to stars in our modern Milky Way.

The TEMPOS Survey: Studying Giant Stars

A new survey called TEMPOS (short for Treasury of Extremely Metal-Poor O Stars) is helping solve this mystery. Led by Dr. Grace Telford at the University of Utah, the team used NASA's Hubble Space Telescope to study 29 giant stars across six nearby dwarf galaxies.

These dwarf galaxies have very low "metallicity" — that's the astronomer's term for how much of an element is heavier than hydrogen and helium, the two simplest building blocks of the cosmos. The early universe had almost no heavy elements, so these dwarf galaxies are the best local copies of what the very first galaxies were like.

With Hubble's Cosmic Origins Spectrograph, the team measured the faint ultraviolet light from each star. Each star has been observed for up to 35 hours — making this the largest and most detailed dataset of its kind.

Stellar Winds: How Stars Shed Their Clothes

Big stars — those with more than 10 times the mass of our Sun — don't live quietly. They blast out intense radiation and continually shed material through their stellar winds. It's a bit like a cosmic snow globe: the star blows gas into space, which then floats around and eventually forms new stars.

But here's the key: the strength of those stellar winds depends heavily on metallicity. Metal atoms act like tiny sails that catch the star's radiation and get pushed away. Stars with fewer metals have fewer "sails," so their winds are weaker.

The TEMPOS observations confirmed this overall pattern — as metallicity decreases, wind speeds drop. But then something surprising happened at the very lowest metallicities: the wind speeds plunged far more sharply than expected, dropping by far more than a smooth trend would predict.

What This Means for the Early Universe

If the earliest stars lost less mass through weaker winds, they kept more of their original heft. That changes everything — how long they live, how they explode, and how much energy and material they dump into their surroundings.

These heavyweight stars may have shaped their young galaxies in ways our models don't yet capture, which is exactly what JWST's strange-looking galaxies are telling us. The TEMPOS data gives astronomers their best shot yet at fixing those models.

As Dr. Telford put it: "Webb opened up a whole bunch of new questions about the evolution of these early galaxies — they're weird. That's the scientific motivation behind TEMPOS: to help understand what's going on in these early galaxies."

Try This at Home

You can see the same principle on a tiny scale! When you blow on a cup of hot cocoa, the steam carries away more energy than if you just let it cool on its own. Massive stars do something similar — but instead of steam, they're losing entire layers of their bodies into space through stellar winds. The difference is that in the early universe, the "wind" was much fainter, so the star kept more of itself.

Fun Fact

The universe's very first stars likely burned with no metals at all — 100% pure hydrogen and helium. Those Population III stars were probably 100 to 300 times heavier than our Sun, and they lived fast and died young in titanic explosions, seeding the cosmos with the first heavy elements that later stars (and planets like Earth) would be made of.

For grown-ups

"Massive stars (more than 10 times the Sun's mass) are rare but mighty. They burn hot and fast, shed material through stellar winds, and explode as supernovae — seeding their host galaxies with energy and heavy elements. Astronomers use 'metallicity' to describe how much of an element is heavier than hydrogen and helium; the early universe had very low metallicity. A new University of Utah-led survey called TEMPOS (Treasury of Extremely Metal-Poor O Stars) used Hubble's Cosmic Origins Spectrograph to study 29 massive stars across six nearby dwarf galaxies with very low metallicity. The team found that as metallicity drops below about 10% of the Sun's, stellar wind speeds plunge far more sharply than expected. Weaker winds mean those stars kept more of their original mass, which changes how they evolve, explode, and shape the first galaxies. This helps explain why the James Webb Space Telescope sees early galaxies that don't match our models — the physics of massive stars was simply different when the universe was young. The TEMPOS survey was published on Sept 21, 2026 in The Astrophysical Journal Supplement Series."
