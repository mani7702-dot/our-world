10 times animals surprised scientists with skills they never saw coming

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 21:30 IST
Reading options

Orcas flee from smaller whales. Elephants may call each other by name. Bumblebee queens can hold their breath underwater for a week. Nature keeps inventing tricks we never guessed existed.

10 times animals surprised scientists with skills they never saw coming

When the ocean's top predator runs away

Orcas — also called killer whales — are the ocean's fiercest hunters. They chase seals, take down great white sharks, and hunt in smart family teams. You would not expect anything to scare an orca.

Yet in the cold waters near Iceland, something strange happens. Smaller whales called pilot whales swim right up to orcas and sometimes even chase them away. Researchers watched 24 meetings between the two species from 2007 to 2020. In 68 percent of these meetings, the orcas backed off. The pilot whales swam straight at them, leaping from the water at speeds up to 16 miles per hour.

When scientists played recordings of pilot-whale calls for orcas that were tagged with sensors, the orcas changed direction, sped up, and huddled closer together. It looked just like the way small birds swarm around a hawk to drive it off — scientists call this "mobbing." One researcher said no pilot whale has ever been seen hurting an orca. They may simply be so annoying that the orcas give up and leave.

Try this at home: Stand in a circle with friends. If one person slowly walks toward the center and everyone else steps in at an angle (like a swarm), the center person usually backs away. That is the same "mobbing" trick birds and whales use.

Do elephants have names for each other?

Dolphins use signature whistles. Parrots copy each other's voices. But elephants may do something even closer to how humans name each other — they might invent their own calls for specific family members.

A research team in Kenya (at Samburu National Reserve and Amboseli National Park) used a computer program to listen to 469 low rumbles from African elephants. The computer could tell which elephant a rumble was aimed at — far better than random guessing. When the scientists played back a call that had been meant for a specific elephant, that elephant walked over and answered more eagerly than when they played a call meant for someone else.

This could mean elephants create their own name-labels. It is a big deal because until now, only humans were thought to do this.

Fun Fact: An adult elephant can recognize the voices of at least 100 different elephants just by listening.

How a bee holds its breath underwater

Bumblebee queens spend the winter curled up asleep underground, waiting for spring. But if a flood fills their burrow, the queen might have to survive fully underwater for days.

Scientists were studying this by accident — laboratory queens were flooded with condensation and 90 percent of them survived, some for up to seven days. On purpose, they submerged more queens. Many lived through a full week underwater.

How? Their bodies go into slow motion. Metabolism drops way down. They soak up oxygen from the water through their fur. And they switch to a backup energy system that needs no oxygen. Some scientists think a thin layer of air trapped in their fuzzy fur works like a tiny scuba tank.

Try this at home: Hold a balloon underwater and let it go. It sinks, pops, and the rubber shoots up like a jellyfish. That is the kind of slow, floaty motion these bees enter when they sleep underwater.

A bear family that learned to be peaceful

In the mountains of central Italy, a small group of brown bears (fewer than 100) live near farms, roads, and tourist trails. Unlike most bears, these Apennine brown bears are known for being calm and non-aggressive.

Scientists looked at the bears' genes and found signs of positive selection in the genes that control aggression. Over thousands of years, humans cleared the forests and killed bears that fought back. The bears that stayed peaceful survived and had more babies. It is one of the clearest cases of evolution shaping behavior — the bears did not just get smaller or slower, they changed how they act around people.

Fun Fact: A baby bear is called a cub, and a whole group is called a sloth (not to be confused with the animal)!

Bees that weave silk like architects

When you think of silk, you think of spiders and silkworms. But scientists found that several kinds of bees also spin silk — and they do it in a way that looks like architecture.

A blue orchard bee larva does not spin one long thread. Instead, it attaches a strand, stretches it across the inside of its nest with its head, then fastens the far end. Over and over, it builds a strong woven wall around its cocoon. This keeps parasites out. Scientists recently figured out how to gently unwrap these silk fibers without harming the bee, and they hope the material could inspire biodegradable bandages or eco-friendly clothing.

Whales that blow bubble rings

You have probably seen dolphins blow rings of bubbles underwater. But humpback whales do it too — and sometimes they do it right at snorkelers and boats.

Researchers watched 12 meetings between 11 humpback whales, and they saw 39 bubble rings. The whales made the rings from their blowholes, let them expand like underwater donuts, and then swam through them. These events were calm and playful, not about eating or fighting. One scientist said it may be the whale version of blowing bubbles at someone just to see what happens.

Bugs that share their bodies with their babies

Most animals need males and females to mix genes. But one tiny soil mite has not had sex in more than 20 million years. How does it stay healthy?

Scientists sequenced the genes of the oribatid mite and found that even without sex, the mite's pairs of chromosomes have stayed different and evolving. Mobile genes (yes, genes that jump around) and borrowed DNA from other organisms give the mites new tricks. It is like they skipped the "dating app" of reproduction but still throw in new mutations by other means.

Fire birds that use flame as a hunting tool

In northern Australia, Aboriginal rangers noticed that some birds follow wildfires and feed on animals that flee the flames. Now, scientists watching prescribed burns (controlled fires) in Arnhem Land found that black kites and brown falcons gather thickly around the fire zone.

The birds caught lizards, snakes, toads, grasshoppers, and other animals at about ten times their normal hunting rate. Black kites became 23 times more common when fire was near. The birds did not just wait for dinner — they positioned themselves to catch the fleeing prey, turning a disaster for most animals into a buffet.

Rotifers that rebuild their shattered DNA

Bdelloid rotifers are microscopic animals that can survive being dried out, frozen, and blasted with radiation. Their newest trick: they can repair DNA that has been broken into dozens of pieces.

Scientists zapped rotifers with intense proton radiation that shattered their chromosomes into 30 to 50 pieces. Some rotifers still laid eggs. Their babies inherited the broken DNA, and over several generations the chromosomes were gradually stitched back together. It is like tearing a book into confetti and then slowly gluing the pages back in order.

Try this at home (bees and bubbles)

  • Place a straw in a glass of water. Blow gently — you will see a ring of tiny bubbles. Some whales do the same, just much bigger.
  • On a rainy day, look for bumblebees under a leaf. If you see one moving slowly on cool morning, it is probably a queen gathering energy for her colony. Leave her alone — she is busy.

Fun Fact roundup

  1. Orcas have no real natural enemies. The main thing that scares them? Smaller whales with loud voices.
  2. Elephants may give each other names — using rumbles that are unique like signatures.
  3. Bumblebee queens can survive a full week underwater during floods.
  4. A group of bears is called a sloth, a group of whales is called a pod, and a group of bees is called a colony or swarm.

Quick recap: Animals are full of surprises. From whales that flee from smaller whales to bees that hold their breath underwater, nature keeps inventing new tricks. The more we watch, the less we know — and the more we discover, the more amazing the world becomes.
