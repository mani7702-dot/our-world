Why are drought fires turning the sky over New Guinea a thick white-gray?

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 20:45 IST
Reading options

Strong El Niño has dried out New Guinea's savannas and grasslands. Human-set fires are spreading across the unusually parched landscape, sending smoke visible from satellites and stirring up air-quality concerns for nearby communities.

Why are drought fires turning the sky over New Guinea a thick white-gray?

If you have ever seen satellite pictures of the island of New Guinea from September to October 2026, you might have noticed a thick, dusty blanket of smoke hanging over the southern half of the island. That is not normal clouds — it is smoke from dozens of fires spreading across land that has become far too dry to burn this way under ordinary circumstances.

1. The dry season that ran too long

New Guinea is normally one of the wettest places on Earth. The southernmost part of the island — called the Trans-Fly region — is dominated by savannas and grasslands that are used to regular rain. By mid-October 2026, those areas had gone more than 90 days without any meaningful rainfall, according to Papua New Guinea's National Weather Service. Some places had seen only 5 to 10 percent of their normal precipitation.

That kind of drought does not happen by accident. It is driven by Earth's most powerful natural climate pattern — a phenomenon called El Niño — which shifts the planet's rainfall belts and winds. When El Niño is strong, the atmosphere moves heat away from the western Pacific, and the islands that usually catch steady rain instead bake under a stubborn sky with almost no clouds.

2. When the landscape catches light

Once the grass and scrub of the savannas dry out, even a small spark can grow fast. Researchers who study these events say that nearly all of the fires on New Guinea are set by people — often to clear land for farming or gardening. Under normal conditions, the vegetation stays damp enough that a fire burns slowly and stops when it hits a wetter patch. But in 2026, the entire region was primed to burn.

Satellite instruments aboard NASA's NOAA-20 spacecraft spotted the result on October 11, 2026: a river of white-gray smoke stretching from the southern coast across the island. The image, captured by the VIIRS sensor (that is short for Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite), shows how much thicker the smoke became compared to a typical year.

3. How much is too much?

Scientists who track fire around the world use a system called the Global Fire Emissions Database — or GFED for short. It combines satellite pictures of fires with data about how much plants and trees are growing, then calculates how much carbon the fires are releasing into the air.

By October 19, 2026, fires on the Papua New Guinea side of the island had released roughly 32 megatons of carbon — already more than the entire year of 2015, which was itself an El Niño year. The team that runs GFED, led by Guido van der Werf at Wageningen University & Research, said the 2026 numbers were on track to become the highest ever recorded for the island. On the Indonesian side — sometimes called West New Guinea or Papua — emissions topped 35 megatons, the third-highest after 2015 and 1997.

Thirty-two megatons is a staggering number. It means the fires are not just burning trees — they are burning the very soil and root systems of the savannas, where decades of dry plant matter has piled up like invisible tinder.

4. Where the smoke lands

The smoke does not stay over New Guinea. High winds and the island's geography help carry it across the Pacific, and the thick haze has already drifted toward neighboring islands. Air-quality monitors recorded unhealthy levels of tiny particles, and local officials in some areas asked children and older adults to wear masks when they had to go outside. Flights were delayed and schools were closed as the air turned gray.

The problem with smoke is that it carries more than just ash. It holds invisible gases — carbon dioxide, methane, and other compounds — that trap heat in the atmosphere. So each fire season that burns hotter and longer makes the next El Niño event even more likely, setting up a loop that scientists are still watching carefully.

Try this at home

You can see how El Niño works with a simple experiment. Fill a large bowl with warm water and drop in a few drops of food coloring near the center. Watch how the color swirls and spreads — that is like warm ocean water piling up in one spot during an El Niño year, pushing the usual weather patterns far away. You can also check a world map or weather app to see where it has been drier or rainier than usual this year — El Niño's effects cross oceans, and they show up everywhere from Australia to California to East Africa.

Fun fact

New Guinea is not just one island — it is the world's second-largest island (after Greenland), and it sits right on the equator, where rain usually falls almost every day. The fact that so much of it went 90 days without rain in 2026 is partly why the fires got so intense. Even more surprisingly, the smoke was so thick that NASA satellites could measure it from space — and the amount of carbon it released was enough to fill over 300,000 Olympic swimming pools if it were packed into the atmosphere as pure carbon.

For grown-ups

The 2026 El Niño event shifted rainfall patterns across the Pacific, leaving the southernmost part of Papua New Guinea (the Trans-Fly region) more than 90 consecutive days without rain by mid-October. Combined with the island's fire-prone savannas and grasslands, this created tinderbox conditions. Carbon emissions from fires on the Papua New Guinean side of the island exceeded 32 megatons by October 19 — already surpassing the full-year total from 2015 and on track to become the highest on record, according to the Global Fire Emissions Database (GFED), which combines satellite fire observations with vegetation data. The Indonesian side (West New Guinea / Papua) exceeded 35 megatons, the third-highest after 2015 and 1997. The vast majority of these fires are deliberately set by people, and once started in the dry savanna-grassland mosaic they spread rapidly. NASA's NOAA-20 satellite carrying the VIIRS instrument captured thick smoke streaming from the southern part of the island on October 11, 2026. Local news reported flight delays, school closures, and health officials urging children and older adults to wear masks.
