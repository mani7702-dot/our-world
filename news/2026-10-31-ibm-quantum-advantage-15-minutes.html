"Can a Quantum Computer Solve a Problem No Regular Computer Ever Could? IBM and UChicago Just Did — in 15 Minutes"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at "18:32" IST
Reading options

"IBM and University of Chicago researchers used a 70-qubit quantum circuit to solve a math problem that would take ordinary supercomputers practically forever — and they found a clever way to check the answer too."

Can a Quantum Computer Solve a Problem No Regular Computer Ever Could? IBM and UChicago Just Did — in 15 Minutes

Imagine a puzzle so tricky that even the world's fastest supercomputer would need longer than the age of the universe to solve it. Now imagine a special kind of computer — one that follows the strange rules of quantum physics — cracking that same puzzle in just 15 minutes.

That is exactly what a team of scientists at IBM and the University of Chicago just accomplished. And here is the really cool part: they figured out a way to be sure the answer is right, even though no ordinary computer could ever check it.

What Is a Quantum Computer, Anyway?

Regular computers — the one on your tablet, the one that helps launch rockets — use bits. Every bit is like a tiny switch that is either on (1) or off (0). It can only be in one state at a time.

But quantum computers use quantum bits, or qubits. Thanks to a weird quantum trick called superposition, a qubit can be both 0 and 1 at the same time — like a spinning coin that is both heads and tails until it lands.

And when qubits are entangled, they can coordinate with each other in ways that let a quantum computer explore many possible answers all at once. For certain types of problems, this makes them unimaginably faster.

The 15-Minute Challenge

The IBM and UChicago team gave their quantum computer a special kind of puzzle called random circuit sampling. The task: ask the computer to generate patterns so complicated that no classical (regular) computer can efficiently copy them.

The quantum computer used 70 logical qubits — that is, qubits protected by error-correction, not just raw hardware qubits. It ran a circuit that was 73 steps deep with 314 special operations mixed in, all in about 15 minutes.

The result was a set of numbers and patterns that, by the laws of mathematics, a regular supercomputer simply cannot reproduce in any reasonable amount of time. That is called quantum advantage — the point where a quantum machine definitively outruns classical computing for a real task.

The Tricky Part: How Do You Check the Answer?

Here is where it gets really clever. Usually, the harder a quantum computer works, the harder it is to verify that it did not just make a mistake. If no regular computer can solve the same problem, then how do you double-check?

The team used something called a spacetime quantum-error-correction code. Think of it like having a team of 76 helpers (physical qubits) working together to represent each of the 70 "important" qubits. If one helper makes a mistake, the others can catch it and fix it.

This error correction made the quantum result 10 times more reliable. And the final answer had a measured accuracy of at least 34.9% — which, in quantum terms, is like hitting a bullseye from a mile away.

Why It Matters for the Future

Today, 15 minutes of quantum work is still a demonstration. But it proves something huge: quantum computers can now solve problems that are literally impossible for regular computers to check — and still be trusted.

That is a huge step toward the day when quantum computers might help design life-saving medicines, discover new materials, or help us understand the deepest secrets of the universe — tasks that would take today's best supercomputers thousands of years.

Try this at home

Build your own "quantum coin flip": Flip a coin and catch it. While it spins through the air, it is kind of like a qubit — it is in a mix of both heads and tails at once. But the moment you look, it becomes one or the other. That is the magic (and mystery) of quantum superposition!

Fun Fact

The quantum computer in this experiment used 76 physical qubits to make just 70 logical qubits (the error-corrected ones). That is because error correction needs extra "helper" qubits to catch and fix mistakes — like having extra proofreaders to check a story!

For grown-ups

"Researchers at IBM and the University of Chicago, led by Prof. Bill Fefferman, published 'Sampling hard circuits with verifiably high fidelity' (arXiv: 2607.25941, 28 July 2026; updated 2 Sept 2026). They encoded 70 logical qubits into 76 physical qubits using spacetime quantum-error-correction codes, ran a depth-73 Clifford circuit with 314 T-gates, and completed the computation in ~15 minutes. The error-corrected logical-qubit approach suppresses gate error rates by 10x after syndrome post-selection, yielding a verified fidelity lower bound of 0.349 at 95% confidence — a demonstration of 'quantum advantage' where the problem is provably beyond the practical reach of classical supercomputers."
