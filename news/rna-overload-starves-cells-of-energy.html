Too Much RNA Can Starve Cells of Energy

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 14:32 IST
Reading options

Scientists discovered that when too much RNA builds up inside a cell, it can damage the tiny energy factories called mitochondria — the same things viruses flood a cell with when they take over.

How a Cell Starves Itself

Every cell in your body is like a tiny city. It needs energy to keep its lights on, move its goods around, and respond to signals — just like a city needs electricity.

The power plants are called mitochondria — little bean-shaped organelles that scientists call the "powerhouse of the cell." They churn out a molecule called ATP, which is the cell's version of battery power. Everything the cell does, from repairing a cut to sending a message to your brain, runs on that ATP currency.

Now here is the strange part: the same molecules that carry your genetic instructions — called RNA — can shut those power plants down if there is too much of them.

The RNA Traffic Jam

Cells constantly make and break down RNA as part of normal life. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries instructions from your DNA to make proteins. Other types of RNA pop up when a virus infects the cell and starts taking over its machinery.

Under normal conditions, the cell has cleanup crews that break down old RNA and keep things tidy. But when a virus multiplies furiously inside a cell, it floods the space with RNA — far more than the cleanup crews can handle.

"The cell's RNA cleanup system normally keeps things balanced," says Dr. Zhilong Yang from Texas A&M University. "But when RNA production runs ahead of breakdown, the excess sits there and starts damaging mitochondria directly."

The Shock: RNA Attacks Energy Factories

Scientists already knew that too much double-stranded RNA (a type that shows up during viral infections) could trigger the immune system to go into overdrive. What surprised the researchers was discovering that ordinary messenger RNA — the kind your own body makes every day — can also harm mitochondria.

Even more surprising: the damage happens without the immune system stepping in.

"When we isolated only the mitochondria and added RNA, we still saw the damage," explains Dr. Djamal Brahim Belhaouari, first author on the study. "That was the moment we realized this was a direct effect."

The leading theory is that excess RNA physically blocks the electrical charges mitochondria need to keep their power-generating machinery running — like pouring sand into a generator.

Why Viruses Face a Problem Too

Viruses are freeloaders. They hijack your cells and use the cell's own power to make more viruses. But producing massive amounts of RNA is a double-edged sword — if the virus floods the cell so much that mitochondria shut down, the cell dies before the virus can finish copying itself.

This explains why poxviruses (like the ones that cause smallpox and mpox) have special RNA cleanup tools built into their own biology. They literally have to mop up their own excess RNA to keep their host cell alive and productive long enough to reproduce.

"It is like a virus managing its own mess so the factory it borrowed stays open," says Yang.

What This Means for You

For most of us, this discovery matters because it shows how delicately balanced our cells are. RNA is essential — without it, no proteins get made and no cells function. But like so many good things in biology, too much of it becomes a problem.

The research may one day help scientists design better mRNA medicines (the same technology behind some vaccines) that deliver the right amount of RNA without overwhelming the cell's energy system. It also gives clues about how viral infections damage cells beyond just the immune response.

Try This at Home

Build a cell city model: Grab a small flashlight (your mitochondrion), a cup of water (the cell), and some glitter (RNA particles). Shine the flashlight through water with just a pinch of glitter — the light passes through clearly. Now add a huge handful of glitter. The light gets blocked, swirling, and dim. That is what happens when too much RNA crowds out the energy flow inside a real cell.

Fun Fact

Mitochondria were once free-living bacteria that merged with ancient single-celled creatures billions of years ago. That is why they still have their own DNA and can reproduce on their own — they are living fossils of an older partnership that made complex life possible!

For grown-ups

The study, published in PNAS by Texas A&M researchers, shows excess RNA from poxvirus infections impairs mitochondria and reduces cellular ATP production. Viruses produce huge quantities of RNA as they multiply, and when the cell cannot break it all down fast enough, the leftover RNA damages mitochondrial membranes and lowers the cell's energy output. The mechanism is direct — not immune-mediated — as shown when isolated mitochondria were damaged by RNA alone in a test tube.
