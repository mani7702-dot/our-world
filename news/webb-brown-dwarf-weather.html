Can a world 20 light-years away have weather?

By Our World Published 19 October 2026 at 17:30 IST
Reading options

NASA's Webb telescope watched a wild world 20 light-years away and found its stormy skies follow surprisingly tidy rules — three repeating weather patterns, not total chaos.

We check the forecast for our own little planet every single day. This month, scientists used one of the most powerful telescopes ever built to take the weather of a world so far away its light left around the time you were born. And they found something surprising: even the wildest weather follows rules.

The world is called SIMP 0136, and it sits about 20 light-years from Earth. That means the light we are catching from it today has been traveling for about twenty years to reach us. The telescope data used in this study was captured back in 2023 — so that light had actually left SIMP 0136 around the year the study's young lead researcher was born. It is a lovely way to feel just how far "far away" is.

What even is a brown dwarf?

SIMP 0136 is not a planet, and it is not a star. It is a brown dwarf — a strange in-between object that is bigger and hotter than a giant planet like Jupiter, but not heavy enough to catch fire and shine the way a star does. Brown dwarfs are one of the universe's great oddities, and scientists are still learning how they behave.

Its sky would be nothing like ours. The atmosphere is packed with enormous, fast-changing cloud systems, and the weather is closer to Jupiter's on a very bad day than anything Earth throws at us. It has even been linked to northern-lights-like effects.

How do you study weather that far away?

Obviously, nobody can drop a weather balloon there. Instead, researchers at Trinity College Dublin used observations from NASA's James Webb Space Telescope, which can detect tiny changes in an object's brightness as it spins.

Their trick was a math technique called Principal Component Analysis, which sorts jumbled data into the few biggest patterns hiding inside it. Think of it like tuning a noisy radio until one clear station comes through.

The surprise: three weather states, not chaos

When the team sorted out the noise, the results were startling. Even though SIMP 0136 looks wildly complicated, almost all of its changing brightness could be explained by just two things: changes in temperature, and the way its clouds are stacked vertically through the atmosphere.

Even more neatly, they found three recurring weather states that rotate in and out of view as the brown dwarf spins. Together they make a shifting patchwork — some regions hotter with thin, wispy clouds, others cooler with thick clouds piled high. As the world turns, these blotches parade past, over and over.

And the rules hold steady over time: the same basic drivers keep shaping the weather across more than a dozen rotations, even as the fine details continually change.

Why this matters

Decoding the weather on a world 20 light-years away sounds like a strange thing to bother with. But it gives scientists a powerful new tool for reading the skies of distant worlds — including planets around other stars. If researchers can find order in something as stormy as SIMP 0136, they can start to ask better questions about atmospheres everywhere.

It is also a lovely thought: somewhere out there, a not-quite-star is spinning through its three moods on repeat, on a schedule no one wrote down and everyone follows.

And there is a hopeful idea hiding in the math. If scientists can find tidy rules behind a sky that looks totally chaotic, maybe the atmospheres of other worlds — even the rocky ones that might, one day, be of interest to us — are more readable than they first appear. The weather report for a brown dwarf may end up teaching us how to read a planet we have never seen.
