"Why Giant Black Holes Can't Hide the Secret of Dark Matter"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 21:18 IST
Reading options

"Scientists have long wondered if 'stupendously large' black holes could make up dark matter. A new study using the cosmic microwave background — light from the very beginning of the universe — shows these giants are too rare to be the invisible substance that holds galaxies together."

1. What Holds the Universe Together When We Can't See It?

Imagine spinning a pizza crust — if it spun too fast, the dough would fly off, wouldn't it? Yet whole galaxies spin at speeds that should tear them apart. Something invisible is holding them together. That invisible something is called dark matter, and scientists have been hunting it for decades.

But what is dark matter made of? Could it be giant black holes hiding in the dark?

2. The Case of the "Stupendously Large" Black Holes

Scientists have wondered if dark matter might be made of extremely massive black holes — so big they'd outweigh entire collections of stars. These theoretical objects, called "stupendously large black holes," could be heavier than 100 billion Suns (and some could even tip the scales at millions of billions of Suns!).

If dark space were packed with these giants, the universe would look different. Each black hole would tug on nearby light and matter in a way that smears the cosmic background glow — the faint afterglow left over from the universe's birth.

3. Reading the Baby Photo of the Universe

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is like a baby photo of the universe, taken when the cosmos was just 380,000 years old. At that time, the universe cooled enough for light to travel freely, and that ancient light has been drifting toward us ever since.

The CMB is almost perfectly uniform — the temperature changes from spot to spot by only about one part in 100,000. But those tiny variations are a treasure map: if dark matter were made of widely scattered black holes, the CMB would show a distinctive "grainy" pattern at small scales. Instead, the light looks smooth — exactly what you'd expect if dark matter is made of ordinary particles, not giant black holes.

4. The Verdict: Not the Dark Matter Hide-and-Seek Champion

After analyzing the CMB, the research team concluded that stupendously large black holes cannot make up a significant portion of dark matter. They're too rare and too spread out to account for the invisible mass that keeps galaxies from flying apart.

But the mystery isn't over! Scientists are now investigating a different kind of black hole — ones as small as asteroids, called primordial black holes. These tiny, dense remnants from the early universe might still be good dark matter candidates.

The search continues, and every new study helps us edge closer to solving one of the biggest mysteries in science.

Try this at home

You can observe the evidence for dark matter yourself! Download a free planetarium app (like Stellarium) and look at a spiral galaxy such as M31 (the Andromeda Galaxy). Notice how the galaxy's outer arms are clearly visible and rotating. In reality, astronomers use instruments to measure how fast stars at the edges of galaxies spin — and they spin far too fast for their own gravity alone. That's dark matter tugging them along. Ask a grown-up to help you explore NASA's visualization of dark matter at https://science.nasa.gov/astrophysics/focus-areas/what-is-dark-energy.

Fun Fact

Dark matter makes up about five-sixths of all matter in the universe — but it's invisible! The remaining one-sixth is the ordinary atoms that make up you, me, your pet, and even the air we breathe. If the entire Milky Way galaxy were a pizza, dark matter would be the invisible cheese holding the whole thing together, while all the visible stars and gas are just the toppings on top.

For grown-ups

"Dark matter is an invisible form of matter that makes up about 85% of all matter in the universe (the remaining 5% is ordinary matter, and 68% is dark energy). It was first proposed when astronomers noticed galaxies spinning fast enough to fly apart — without extra invisible mass to hold them together. Black holes have long been a candidate for dark matter, but the new study investigated 'stupendously large black holes' — hypothetical black holes with masses greater than 100 billion times the Sun (some would be heavier than entire galaxies). Researchers analyzed the cosmic microwave background (CMB), the faint glow left over from 380,000 years after the Big Bang. If countless stupendous black holes peppered the universe, the CMB would look 'grainy' — tiny temperature variations at small scales. Instead, the CMB's temperature varies smoothly at the level expected for particle-based dark matter, not for discrete massive objects. The study team included Christopher Gerlach, Yann Gouttenoire, and Antonio Iovino from the Mainz Institute for Theoretical Physics and NYU Abu Dhabi. Their findings will be published Oct. 26 in Physical Review Letters (DOI: 10.1103/qlf9-thf4). The researchers also note that smaller primordial black holes (asteroid-sized) remain a viable dark matter candidate under investigation."
