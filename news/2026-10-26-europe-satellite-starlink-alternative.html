"Can Europe beat Starlink with its own super constellation?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 18:42 IST IST
Reading options

"Ukraine relies on Elon Musk's Starlink network for battlefield communications, but when the service slows down, whole armies lose contact. Europe is racing to build its own satellite internet system — but its best alternative is still years away."

Can Europe beat Starlink with its own super-constellation?

Why Ukraine Can't Afford to Lose Signal

Picture a soldier in a trench, calling in the location of an incoming drone strike using a tablet no bigger than a paperback. The message zips up to space — to a satellite the size of a laptop tray hovering 550 kilometres overhead — then back down to command, all in less than a second. That connection is Starlink, and for Ukraine's army it is often the difference between life and death.

But Starlink is owned by one company, one man, one country. When that link slows — as it did last week across Kyiv, hobbling air-defence coordination — the question is no longer academic. Can Europe build a satellite internet network good enough to replace it?

The Starlink Monopoly Problem

Starlink is not just the world's biggest satellite constellation — it is the backbone of modern warfare in Ukraine. SpaceX, the company behind it (founded by Elon Musk), has launched over 11,000 satellites since 2019, blanketing almost the entire planet. Roughly 75% of all satellites flying in low Earth orbit today belong to Starlink.

For Ukraine, two services matter:

  1. Starlink (civilian) — used by local governments and ordinary people.
  2. Starshield (military) — a US-government-controlled version used by the armed forces for secure battlefield communications and controlling attack drones.

Last week, Starlink suddenly slowed in Ukraine. For hours, drone operators could barely share video, and air-defence systems struggled to talk to one another. The US Pentagon blamed a technical glitch; Musk blamed Ukraine "violating Starlink's terms of service" by using the network for drone strikes. Either way, the lesson was clear: one provider's decision can freeze an entire battlefield.

Europe's Backup Plans

Europe does not want to admit it, but it has no real substitute yet. There are a few pieces in play:

  • Eutelsat OneWeb — a Franco-British constellation with about 600 satellites. It works, but only covers latitudes up to about 60° north and south — so half of Ukraine can't use it, and it is not approved for military drone warfare.
  • Govsatcom — an EU "sharing" network created in January 2026 that lets member states lend each other satellites. Ukraine is negotiating to join, but the network cannot currently do classified military work.
  • IRIS² — Europe's ambitious "answer" to Starlink. Pronounced "Iris squared", it is a 348-satellite network led by Eutelsat, SES, and Hispasat (the SpaceRISE consortium). The catch? It will not be ready until 2029.

Even European officials admit the gap is wide. One industry executive told the BBC roughly: "If you gave me €8 billion today, I could not have a full constellation working before 2032-2033." Satellites are simply slow to build, launch, and position.

What Happens If the Sky Goes Quiet?

Ukraine's dilemma also reveals a bigger truth about how we stay connected. Most people think the internet flows through satellites — but about 99% of intercontinental data actually travels through undersea fibre-optic cables that snake along the ocean floor. Those cables are now considered strategic military targets: in October 2026, UK authorities reported a sharp rise in suspicious Russian naval activity near key cable landing sites.

When Starlink hiccupped, Ukraine briefly fell back on older systems — slower, more expensive, less reliable. The smart idea — one already used every day on Earth — is to think like a mobile phone: if you lose 5G, you drop to 4G; if that fails, 3G. Redundancy means never having just one path to space.

That idea is finally taking hold. In October 2026, Finnish companies Nokia and ICEYE — yes, the same firm that once supplied Ukraine's satellite internet — announced a partnership with Ukraine's Defence Ministry to build a home-grown satellite communications system.

Try this at home

Grab a ball of string, some tape, and four friends. Each person is a "satellite"; stand in a square and hold the string so the group forms a perimeter. One person in the middle is the "ground station." Have the corner "satellites" toss a soft ball to each other, then pass it to the middle — but the middle person must catch it within two seconds. Try doing this while walking. That's what ground crews face: moving targets, moving data, and only seconds to catch the signal before it is gone.

Fun Fact

Starlink satellites do not actually "hover" over one spot. They dive and climb, adjusting their altitude dozens of times per day to stay correctly spaced. And because they fly in low Earth orbit at about 27,000 km/h (17,000 mph), each satellite completes an orbit every 90 minutes — meaning it sees the same ground station for only a few minutes each day. That is why thousands are needed to give the continuous coverage users expect.

For grown-ups

"Starlink is a satellite internet constellation owned by Elon Musk's SpaceX company, operating in low Earth orbit (LEO) at an altitude of roughly 550 km. As of October 2026 the network comprises approximately 11,150 active and spare satellites, providing broadband service in around 160 countries. Starlink has become critical to the war in Ukraine for two services: civilian Starlink terminals used by local governments and civilians, and Starshield — a US-government-controlled military variant used by Ukraine's armed forces for secure battlefield communications and drone control. The Starling slowdown of October 2026, affecting Kyiv's air-defence coordination, highlighted Ukraine's total dependence on a single U.S. provider whose service terms are set unilaterally. Starlink competes with Eutelsat OneWeb, a Franco-British LEO constellation launched after a 2023 merger, which operates roughly 600 active satellites and covers 60 degrees north to south latitude — less than Starlink's near-global coverage. Europe's next-generation replacement is IRIS² (Iris to the second power), a 348-satellite defence-first secure communications constellation led by Eutelsat, SES, and Hispasat under the SpaceRISE consortium, scheduled to enter service in 2029. A shorter-term fallback is Govsatcom, an EU sharing network created in January 2026 that bundles existing national satellites; Ukraine is negotiating associate membership but the network currently lacks the encryption and tactical-mobility features needed for drone warfare. The fundamental constraint is physical: satellites take years to design, build, test, and launch, and the capital costs run into the billions of euros. Even with €8 billion (approx. $8.6 billion) available immediately, no European operator could have a full alternative constellation in orbit before 2032-2033. Meanwhile the conflict has accelerated a broader shift: in October 2026, Finnish companies Nokia and ICEYE — the latter a former Starlink customer of Ukraine — announced a partnership with Ukraine's Ministry of Defence to develop a sovereign satellite communications capability. Most of the world's intercontinental communications traffic (about 99%) still travels through underwater fibre-optic cables rather than satellites; these seabed cables are now recognised as vulnerable national infrastructure, with UK authorities reporting increased suspicious Russian naval activity near cable landing sites in October 2026. The lesson for Ukraine is not just about one provider's whims but about communications resilience — having multiple, redundant networks so that if one goes down, others can keep armies talking, just as mobile phones fall back from 5G to 4G to 3G.\""
