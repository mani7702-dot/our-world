Why 77 countries are signing up for NASA's Moon agreement

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 21:54 IST
Reading options

Uzbekistan just became the 77th country to join the Artemis Accords — a set of rules that help nations work together in space. What does it mean to explore the Moon and Mars as a team?

Most of us will never leave Earth — but countries around the world are already making rules for how to behave once we do.

The space treaty everyone can agree on

On October 20, 2026, the Republic of Uzbekistan became the 77th country to sign something called the Artemis Accords — a set of simple-but-important rules that help nations explore space together.

The signing happened in Samarkand, Uzbekistan's ancient city of marble towers and blue-tiled mosques, during a visit by U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio. Uzbek Foreign Minister Bakhtiyor Saidov put pen to paper on behalf of his country, agreeing to play by a shared rulebook as humanity reaches back toward the Moon.

Why do we need space rules?

When the first countries started launching rockets in the 1950s, no one really knew what to do up there. Today, dozens of nations, private companies, and even universities send satellites into orbit. The Moon — and someday Mars — could get crowded fast.

The Artemis Accords, drafted by NASA and the U.S. State Department in 2020, lay out eight guiding principles:

  1. Peaceful exploration — space is for everyone, not for weapons.
  2. Transparency — share what you learn.
  3. Helping others in distress — if an astronaut or spacecraft needs aid, you help.
  4. Sharing scientific data — discoveries belong to all of humanity.
  5. Not interfering — your mission shouldn't mess up another's.
  6. Protecting heritage — places like the Apollo landing sites are historical treasures.
  7. Scientific cooperation — work together, not alone.
  8. Sustainable practices — leave space as clean as you found it.

Uzbekistan's leap into space

Uzbekistan may be new to the Accords, but it is not new to the skies. The country already operates a national space program and is working on its first CubeSat — a tiny, shoebox-sized satellite — scheduled for launch in 2028, the same year NASA aims to land humans on the Moon again through the Artemis program.

That timing is no coincidence. Uzbekistan hopes to join a growing club of nations that see space not as a race, but as a shared frontier. The Artemis Accords already count all seven founding members, every space-faring European nation, Japan, Canada, and now Central Asia's newest participant.

What comes next?

The Accords are not a treaty — countries are not forced to sign. But signing sends a message: "I promise to explore the Moon and Mars responsibly, and I want to share what I find."

As more countries join, the Accords grow more useful. When a team of international astronauts lands near the Moon's south pole, they will rely on these same principles to work side by side — just as the 77 signatories now do from Earth.

Try this at home

Draw your own "space rulebook" with friends or family. What rules would you write for visiting another planet? Would you share your discoveries? How would you protect anything special you found? The answers might surprise you.

Fun fact

The Moon is about 4.5 billion years old. The first human-made object to reach the Moon was the Soviet Union's Luna 2 in 1959 — it crashed there on purpose, making the Moon the first celestial body humans have visited. Today, nearly every major spacefaring nation has signed the Artemis Accords to make sure the next visits are peaceful and cooperative.

For grown-ups

On October 20, 2026, Uzbekistan became the 77th signatory of the Artemis Accords during a ceremony in Samarkand, Uzbekistan, witnessed by U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio. The Accords are a non-binding set of principles for civil space exploration established by NASA and the U.S. State Department in 2020 with seven founding nations. Wikipedia confirms that as of October 19, 2026, 77 countries have signed the Accords. The Accords commit signatories to peaceful exploration, transparency, sharing scientific data openly, rendering aid to astronauts in distress, and ensuring space activities do not interfere with those of other nations. Uzbekistan is developing a national space program and aims to launch its first CubeSat by 2028. The Artemis program targets returning humans to the Moon by 2028, with Mars as the long-term goal.
