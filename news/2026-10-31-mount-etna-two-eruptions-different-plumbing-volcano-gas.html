Why Did the Same Volcano Erupt Two Completely Different Ways?

By Our World Published at 00:20 IST
Reading options

Mount Etna in Italy is one of the most studied volcanoes on Earth. So scientists were surprised to learn that two of its eruptions — thousands of years apart — took completely different journeys from deep underground to the surface. One crept upward slowly for weeks. The other burst out in just a few hours.

How can a single volcano behave in two such different ways?

A soda bottle under the ground

Deep beneath a volcano, rock melts into a hot, thick liquid called magma. Trapped inside that magma are dissolved gases — mostly water and carbon dioxide.

"It's like a bottle of soda," said scientist Esteban Gazel of Cornell University. Open the bottle gently and nothing happens. Shake it first, and the bubbles suddenly rush out all at once. "Volcanoes work in a similar way."

For a long time, scientists thought water was the gas that mostly drives explosive eruptions. Then, in 2023, Gazel's team showed that carbon dioxide can trigger them too.

Reading a crystal like a diary

The trick is tiny. As magma cools, it grows crystals, and inside those crystals it traps microscopic bubbles of gas — each one only about 1 to 10 per cent as thick as a human hair.

The team used a technique called Raman spectroscopy, which shines laser light on a sample and reads how the light scatters back. That reveals how much carbon dioxide is packed inside each bubble. More gas means more pressure — and pressure tells the scientists how deep the magma was when the bubble got trapped. It's like finding hidden notes written inside the rock.

Two eruptions, two routes

Comparing the crystals, the team found:

  • The eruption of 122 BC started as magma rose from about 22 km down. But instead of racing up, it paused at just 2 to 5 km below the surface and waited there for several weeks, slowly leaking gas, before finally erupting.
  • The Fall Stratified eruption, nearly 4,000 years ago, did the opposite. Magma shot up from 24 to 30 km deep and erupted within hours — driven by a much bigger load of carbon dioxide.

So the balance between the two gases decides the route: lots of carbon dioxide pushes magma up fast from very deep, while water controls the slower, shallower path.

A giant secret under the mountain

Gazel even noticed something fun. In Greek mythology, Mount Etna was said to be the prison of two defeated giants, Typhon and Enceladus. The 122 BC magma path, long and winding, reminded him of one giant; the shorter one of the other.

"if you look at the plumbing system ... it's like Typhon, because it's elongated and serpentine," he said.

Try this at home

You can make your own mini-eruption — safely. Fill a glass about a third full with fizzy lemonade or soda water. Look closely: those rising bubbles are gas that was dissolved in the liquid. Now stir it gently with a spoon and watch the bubbles fizz out. You just released the gas quickly — the same idea that drives a fast eruption. Try it again with a fresh glass, but this time let it sit quietly for ten minutes first. Which one fizzes more when you stir?

Fun fact: Mount Etna is one of the very few volcanoes on Earth where water and carbon dioxide are both busy at the same time — which is exactly why it was the perfect place to solve this puzzle.
