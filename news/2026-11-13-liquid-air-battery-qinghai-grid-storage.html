What if you could store electricity by turning air into a liquid?

By Our World Published 13 November 2026 at "22:56" IST
Reading options

"In Golmud, China, a giant new 'air battery' now stores spare electricity by chilling air until it turns into a cold liquid, then warms it up again to make power when the grid needs it."

Sunshine and wind do not arrive on a schedule. A windy night can make more electricity than anyone needs, and a still evening can leave a city short. Engineers in China have just switched on a very unusual answer: a machine that turns spare electricity into liquid air, keeps it in a tank, and turns it back into power when the grid asks.

It is called a liquid air energy storage plant, and it sits near the town of Golmud in Qinghai Province. It started feeding electricity into the grid on 30 October 2026.

1. A battery that runs on air

Most big batteries store energy in chemicals. This one stores it in cold.

The plant is built to soak up electricity when there is too much of it — often at midday, when solar farms are working hard — and give it back later. Its numbers are big: 60 megawatts of power, 600 megawatt-hours of energy, and about 10 hours of steady output once it starts. Over a year, its developers expect it to deliver roughly 180 gigawatt-hours, about as much electricity as 30,000 homes use.

2. Squeeze it, chill it, pour it

Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and both turn into liquid when they get cold enough — around −194 °C. That is colder than anywhere on Earth, so the plant has to make that cold itself.

Here is the trick. Electricity runs a set of powerful compressors that squeeze the air to about 70 times normal air pressure. Squeezing a gas heats it up, so the plant catches that heat and keeps it as hot water at up to about 195 °C. The air, now cleaned of water vapour and carbon dioxide so it cannot clog the pipes, is cooled and expanded until it condenses into a pale, boiling-cold liquid, which is stored in insulated tanks.

When power is needed, the liquid air is pumped back up to pressure, warmed with the stored hot water and the outside air, and turned back into a fast-moving gas. That gas spins turbines, and the turbines make electricity. Even the leftover cold is captured so the next round needs less work.

3. Why the tank matters more than the battery

Liquid air has one big advantage: it shrinks. The same amount of air takes up roughly 700 times less space as a liquid than as a gas, so the tanks stay a sensible size. And unlike some storage ideas, it does not need a special cave, a salt cavern or an old gas field to sit in — you can build it almost anywhere.

That matters in places like the Qaidam Basin, where this plant stands: a high, dry, sunny region packed with solar and wind farms that sometimes make more power than the wires can carry. Storing the extra instead of switching it off is exactly what a long-duration store is for.

Try this at home: feel the squeeze

You can feel the two halves of this machine with a bicycle pump.

Pump up a tyre or a ball for a minute, then touch the metal barrel of the pump. It feels warm — that is compression making heat, the same heat the Golmud plant saves in its hot water tank. Now press the valve to let a burst of air out and feel the valve: it goes cold, because expanding air pulls heat away. Squeeze, warm. Release, cold. That is the whole idea, scaled up to the size of a power station.

For grown-ups

"Liquid air energy storage (LAES) is a thermo-mechanical store, not an electrochemical one. On charge, surplus electricity drives a multi-stage centrifugal compressor to roughly 7 MPa; intercooling between stages captures the compression heat as hot water up to about 195 C. The cleaned, cooled air is then expanded and liquefied at about -194 C and held in insulated tanks near atmospheric pressure (liquid air is roughly 1/700 the volume of the same mass of gas, so the tanks stay modest). On discharge the liquid is pumped back to pressure, reheated with the stored heat plus ambient warmth, vapourised and expanded through turbines, with the cold recovered and reused for the next charge cycle. The Golmud plant (China Green Development Investment Group, with the Technical Institute of Physics and Chemistry of the Chinese Academy of Sciences) is rated 60 MW / 600 MWh, giving about 10 hours of continuous output and roughly 180 GWh a year, or the annual electricity of about 30,000 households; construction began in July 2023 and it connected to the grid on 30 October 2026. Round-trip efficiency is well below that of lithium-ion because heat and cold are never perfectly conserved, but the working fluid is free, no lithium or cobalt is needed, siting needs no salt cavern or depleted gas field, and the plant lifetime is measured in decades. It sits in the Qaidam Basin, a solar- and wind-rich region where curtailment is the real problem."
