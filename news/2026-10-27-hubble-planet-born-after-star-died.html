"Could a planet be born from the ashes of a dead star?"

By Our World Published 27 October 2026 at 03:20 IST
Reading options

"Astronomers studying a dead star called a white dwarf think they may have found the first known planet born from the debris of a star's death. If confirmed, this gas-giant world would be a true second-generation planet, formed not from a stellar nursery but from the ashes of a stellar graveyard."

Could a planet be born from the ashes of a dead star?

The star that died — and the planet that may have been born from it

Most planets in our universe form alongside their parent stars, swirling together from a spinning disk of gas and dust like microscopic LEGO bricks clicking into place. But what if a planet could form after a star has already died? Astronomers studying a stellar remnant called a white dwarf think they may have found the first known example of such a second-generation planet.

The star at the center of this mystery is HS 0209+0832, a white dwarf located about 1,650 light-years from Earth. A white dwarf is what remains when a Sun-like star exhausts its nuclear fuel, sheds its outer layers into space, and collapses into an Earth-sized cinder that slowly cools over billions of years. Our own Sun will become a white dwarf in roughly five billion years.

What makes HS 0209+0832 special is a chemical signal discovered by revisiting 27-year-old observations from the Hubble Space Telescope. Astronomer Jamie Williams of the University of Warwick was examining archival Hubble data and found an unexpected abundance of the element niobium — a metal that, until now, had never been confirmed in any white dwarf star.

Why niobium is a cosmic crime-scene clue

Niobium is heavier than iron, and elements heavier than iron cannot be forged inside ordinary stars — they require the extreme conditions inside dying stars. In fact, the only places in the universe where heavy elements like niobium are created are inside the violent deaths of massive stars (supernovae) or the collision of neutron stars. The presence of niobium in a white dwarf's atmosphere is like finding a fingerprint: it can only have come from material expelled during a star's death throes.

"The key insight is that niobium and other elements heavier than iron are not made in the cores of normal stars by fusion," explains theoretical astrophysicist Nicholas Stone of the University of Wisconsin–Madison. "They are forged in the exotic environments inside dying stars. Finding niobium around this white dwarf is a signpost that the star's own 'death' material is present."

The research team believes that when the progenitor star of HS 0209+0832 died and expelled its enriched material, some of that debris eventually came back together — clumping into a gas giant roughly the size of Jupiter.

A planet orbiting a cosmic corpse

NASA's TESS satellite watched the white dwarf for four months and detected repeating dips in brightness. These variations suggest a planet is orbiting HS 0209+0832 at a distance of about 3.7 million miles (6 million kilometers) — much closer than Mercury is to our Sun, but still a respectable distance for a planet orbiting a stellar remnant.

If the candidate world checks out, it would be not just an exoplanet, but a second-generation planet — one that formed from debris left behind by a star that already died. The planet is also losing its atmosphere rapidly, likely because the white dwarf is still extremely hot and its intense radiation is stripping away the gas. Eventually, the white dwarf will cool and the planet may settle into a stable orbit for millions of years.

Try this at home

You can model a dead star's planet-formation process with a simple kitchen experiment:

  1. Fill a shallow bowl with water — this represents the cloud of gas and dust left after a star dies.
  2. Sprinkle a pinch of fine glitter or salt into the center — this represents the heavy elements (like niobium) forged in the star's final moments.
  3. Gently swirl the water with a spoon — watch how the glitter clumps together as currents slow, just like material in space might gather into a planet.
  4. Drop in a small piece of modeling clay and spin it — the clay ball represents the white dwarf (the dead star's core) and the glitter clumps show how new planets can form from the old star's leftovers.

Fun Fact

Our own solar system's planets — Earth, Mars, Jupiter, and the rest — are all first-generation worlds, formed from the same disk of material as the Sun itself about 4.6 billion years ago. A second-generation planet would be a completely new kind of world, born from the ashes of stellar death.
