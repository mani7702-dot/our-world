How many black holes can fit in one ancient galaxy?

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 14:05 IST
Reading options

Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope found three actively-feeding supermassive black holes packed into a single galaxy seen as it was just 1.2 billion years after the Big Bang.

What if you found one black hole in a faraway galaxy? That would already be amazing. Now imagine finding three supermassive black holes — all of them gobbling down material — swimming in the same cosmic neighborhood, at the very dawn of time. That is exactly what an international team of astronomers just did, using the James Webb Space Telescope.

The galaxy is called J0148-4214. Its light needed about 12.5 billion years to reach us, so we are seeing it as it existed when the universe was only around 1.2 billion years old — a toddling infant in cosmic terms. The researchers, led by the Max Planck Institute for Extraterrestrial Physics, measured a redshift of z=5.02, which tells us just how far away and how long ago this galaxy really is.

A cosmic traffic jam of black holes

Two of the three black holes sit near the galaxy's heart, separated by a projected distance of only 620 light-years. For objects that massive, that is practically next door. A third roams much farther out, roughly 5,500 light-years from the center — like a loner who took a very long walk.

Each of these monsters is actively feeding, meaning gas and dust are spiraling toward them, lighting up like beacons we can detect across the universe. Their masses vary wildly: one is roughly 80 million times the mass of our Sun, another is about 600,000 suns, and the third about 2 million suns.

Peeking at black holes with starlight

We cannot see black holes directly (their gravity is so strong that not even light escapes), but the gas around them tells the story. As material spirals in, it moves at extreme speeds and leaves behind fingerprints in the light called "spectral signatures." By splitting that light with JWST's NIRSpec instrument — a technique called integral field spectroscopy — the team could map exactly where the light came from across the galaxy.

It is a bit like hearing three different voices singing in the same room and using subtle shifts in pitch and direction to work out who is standing where, even if you cannot see them.

Why three is more exciting than two

Having three feeding black holes together is extraordinarily rare. Usually when galaxies collide, their central black holes dance toward each other and merge into one. But here, instead of merging, all three are still actively feeding at the same time.

The smaller, 600,000-solar-mass black hole is actually feeding faster than theory says it should — faster than the Eddington limit, which is the speed at which outward pressure balances inward pull. This runaway growth may be a clue to how some black holes became giants so quickly in the early universe.

The two central black holes are expected to crash together in a few hundred million years, creating an even bigger black hole — a fast-track to enormous size. Meanwhile, the lonely third black hole may have been kicked out by gravitational waves, or it may still be drifting inward to join the party.

Try this at home

You can recreate the idea of "mapping invisible sources from their light" with a flashlight and a piece of paper with holes poked in it. Shine the light through onto a wall and try to work out where the holes are from the pattern of light — just like astronomers work out where black holes are from patterns in starlight.

Fun Fact: If you lined up all the mass in these three black holes, it would be about 82.6 million times heavier than our entire Sun. All three exist in the same galaxy, roughly 12.5 billion light-years away, and the light catching them just now left that galaxy when Earth was barely a speck of dust.

for-grownups: The mass ratio between the largest (80 million M☉) and the smallest (2 million M☉) feeding black hole in J0148-4214 is 40:1 — a striking difference packed inside a single galaxy at redshift z=5.02, suggesting that early-Universe black hole assembly was far more chaotic than in modern galaxies.
