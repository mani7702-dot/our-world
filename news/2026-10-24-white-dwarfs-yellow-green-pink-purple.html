"Why White Dwarfs Are Actually Yellow, Green, Pink, and Purple"

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 08:02 IST
Reading options

"White dwarfs are called 'white' for a reason — but new research shows their color can change to yellow, green, pink, or even purple, depending on what's floating in their atmosphere."

Why are some white dwarfs pink, yellow, or even purple?

White dwarfs aren't always white

When you picture a white dwarf, you might imagine a tiny, super-hot star glowing icy blue-white — and usually that is exactly what they look like. White dwarfs are the dense remnants left behind when stars like our Sun run out of fuel and shed their outer layers. They are about as wide as Earth but pack roughly the same mass as the Sun.

But new research says that not all of these stellar leftovers stay white. A team from the University of Warwick has found that some white dwarfs can glow yellow, pink, green, or even purple — a surprise that has astronomers rethinking how these stars work.

What makes a white star turn pink?

It all comes down to what is floating in the star's atmosphere.

For most white dwarfs, their color follows a simple rule: hotter means bluer, cooler means redder. But when certain elements sneak into the atmosphere, they can bend the rules.

The Warwick team studied 151 white dwarfs using computer models and found that 5 of them — about 3.3% — show unexpected colors. Two of them appear yellow, and three appear pink. The culprit? Traces of carbon in their atmospheres push the glow toward pink and purple, while sodium-rich planetary debris creates a greenish tint.

Could other stars surprise us too?

This discovery shows that a star's atmosphere is like a cosmic filter — the elements left behind can change how we see the light from across the universe.

Even cooler objects called brown dwarfs (stars too small to fully ignite) can show strange colors too. Some astronomers have found that they can even appear violet, thanks to sodium, potassium, and molecules like titanium oxide in their atmospheres.

So the next time you look up at a night sky full of points of light, remember: what you see is not always what you get. Stars can be full of surprises — and color surprises at that!

Try this at home

Build a blackbody rainbow! Light a candle in a dark room and hold a piece of white paper a few inches from the flame. Slowly move the paper away from the flame — you will see the paper glow red, then orange, then yellow as if it is getting hotter! This shows how a true "blackbody" should change color with temperature, just like stars.

Astronomers measure this color change using special instruments called spectrometers, which split light into a rainbow to reveal exactly which colors a star is producing. Real white dwarfs sometimes break these rules — and that is how scientists discovered they could glow in surprising shades!

Fun fact: The closest known white dwarf to Earth is Sirius B, part of the famous Sirius star system, sitting just 8.6 light-years away. It is so dense that a single spoonful of its material would weigh as much as a ton on Earth!

For grown-ups: The study was published in RAS Techniques and Instruments (October 2026) led by Dr. Mark Hollands and Dr. Boris Gänsicke at the University of Warwick. They modeled 151 white dwarfs within 20 parsecs (65 light-years) of Earth and found 3.3% deviate from pure blackbody behavior. Carbon-dominated atmospheres produce pink/purple hues (confirming Dufour et al. 2007, ApJ, on SDSS carbon-atmosphere white dwarfs), while sodium-rich planetary debris causes green tints, and hydrogen/helium atmospheres trend cyan/blue in cooler stars.

For grown-ups

New research published in RAS Techniques and Instruments (October 2026) by Dr. Mark Hollands, Dr. Boris Gänsicke, and colleagues at the University of Warwick studied 151 white dwarfs within 20 parsecs (65 light-years) of Earth using computer models. They found that 5 of the 151 — about 3.3% — show colors that deviate from the expected blackbody spectrum: two appear yellow and three appear pink. The key variable is atmospheric composition rather than temperature: carbon in the atmosphere causes pink or purple hues, while sodium-rich planetary debris produces a greenish tint. Cooler white dwarfs with hydrogen and helium atmospheres tend toward cyan or blue. This confirms earlier work by Dufour et al. (2007, Astrophysical Journal) on carbon-dominated atmosphere white dwarfs discovered in the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, where carbon-rich atmospheres were found to alter the star's observed color away from the theoretical blackbody prediction.
