"How did Star Trek creator Gene Roddenberry become the first human to fly to space — after death?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at "10:11 IST" IST
Reading options

"On October 22, 1992, the space shuttle Columbia carried more than cargo to orbit — tucked inside astronaut James Wetherbee's personal kit was a small container of Star Trek creator Gene Roddenberry's ashes. It was humanity's first 'space burial' and a dream Roddenberry never got to fulfill in life."

How did Star Trek creator Gene Roddenberry become the first human to fly to space — after death?

1. A dream deferred

Gene Roddenberry (1921–1991) created Star Trek and spent his life imagining what it would be like to travel among the stars. But despite his cosmic dreams, he never got to leave Earth during his lifetime. He passed away on October 24, 1991 — just a year before his legacy would literally launch into orbit.

2. The first space burial

On October 22, 1992 — almost exactly one year after Roddenberry's death — the space shuttle Columbia blasted off for STS-52, a mission to deploy a satellite and run science experiments. Tucked inside the crew's personal gear was something extraordinary: a tiny container holding a portion of Roddenberry's ashes.

The reason they went to space? Roddenberry's widow, Majel Barrett (who played Lwaxana Troi in Star Trek), arranged for NASA astronaut James Wetherbee — the shuttle's commander — to carry the ashes as a personal item. Wetherbee later said he was "honored to be asked" and was "just glad" he could bring happiness to the family.

NASA kept the memorial flight a secret for nearly two years, not announcing it publicly until 1994. It was humanity's first-ever "space burial" — a fitting tribute to a man who inspired millions to look up at the stars.

3. The mission that carried a dream

The STS-52 mission had its own important work to do. Columbia's primary job was to deploy LAGEOS-2 — a beach ball–sized "spacecraft" covered in tiny mirrors that scientists use to study how Earth's tectonic plates move and how the planet's gravity shifts over time. The crew also tested experiments in weightlessness, from growing protein crystals to studying how flames behave in space.

All the while, somewhere in the cockpit, Roddenberry's ashes orbited Earth at about 17,500 miles per hour — roughly 17,500 miles per hour — making 15 trips around our planet each day.

4. A legacy that keeps flying

The Roddenberry memorial flight opened the door for future space memorials. In 1997, a company called Celestis launched more of Roddenberry's ashes — along with those of Star Trek actor James Doohan (who played Scotty) — aboard a Pegasus rocket from the Canary Islands. Since then, space memorials have carried the remains of notable figures including astronomer Eugene Shoemaker, whose ashes rode to the Moon in 1999.

Today, companies offer space burials on commercial rockets, but back in 1992, it was a quiet, personal gesture — a science fiction writer finally reaching the final frontier he helped make real.

Try this at home

Write your initials — or a tiny note — on a scrap of paper. Fold it up small and tape it inside a model rocket or a toy space shuttle. When you "launch" it (even just by tossing it gently across the room), imagine it orbiting the Earth 15 times a day, just like the ashes of a famous storyteller who dreamed of space adventures for all of us.

Fun Fact

Gene Roddenberry wasn't the only Star Trek connection in space. In September 2026, Star Trek actors celebrated the 50th anniversary of the Space Shuttle Enterprise — the test vehicle that never flew in space but was named after the starship from the show. And on the 60th anniversary of Star Trek, an astronaut aboard the International Space Station gave a special tribute to the franchise, floating a tiny Enterprise model during a live video call.
