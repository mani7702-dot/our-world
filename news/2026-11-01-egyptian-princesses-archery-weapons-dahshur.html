Why Were Ancient Egyptian Princesses Buried With Bows and Arrows?

A 100-year-old puzzle

In the 1890s, archaeologists opened tombs at Dahshur, an ancient Egyptian burial site near the pyramids. Inside, they found something puzzling: royal women buried with bows, arrows, daggers and maces — weapons usually found with men.

For over a century, experts assumed the weapons were just ceremonial gifts, symbols placed in the tomb for the afterlife. The princesses themselves were thought to be figureheads, not fighters.

Now a fresh look at their bones is turning that idea on its head.

What the bones say

The six royal mummies were rediscovered in the Egyptian Museum during a project in 2020. Four of them were daughters and sisters of the pharaoh Amenemhat II. A team led by Dr Zeinab Hashesh studied the skeletons closely.

They found something striking. The bones showed very strong muscle attachments in the shoulders and arms — the kind that build up from years of repetitive, strenuous work, like pulling a bowstring or swinging a heavy weapon.

The princesses also carried healed injuries: cracked ribs and foot fractures that had mended. Princess Itaweret's skeleton, the team says, looks like that of a skilled archer.

"This directly explains the presence of bows, arrows, and maces in the women's tombs," Hashesh said. "These were not just symbolic gifts but tools they actively used."

Not everyone agrees — and that's science

Other researchers have pointed out a fair question: strong muscle marks don't prove exactly which activity built them. The team agrees there are limits — the princesses' skulls were lost over a century ago, and more tests are planned.

This healthy argument is how science works. New evidence arrives, old ideas get tested, and understanding slowly sharpens. What's clear is that these royal women led active, physically demanding lives.

Try this at home

Be a bone detective! Feel your own shoulder and elbow while you gently bend your arm. Muscles attach to bones at bumpy spots called tubercles. The more you use a muscle, the stronger that attachment gets. Athletes — like rowers or archers — build them up over years. That's exactly the clue scientists read on 4,000-year-old skeletons.

Fun Fact: The princesses' tombs held an especially ornate dagger for Princess Ita — a weapon so fine that researchers describe the royal jewellery and objects as "breathtaking in their craftsmanship."

For grown-ups

"A study in Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology (lead author Dr Zeinab Hashesh) re-examined six royal mummies from Dahshur, Egypt, rediscovered in the Egyptian Museum in 2020. Four were daughters and sisters of pharaoh Amenemhat II (Middle Kingdom, c. 1900 BCE): Princesses Ita, Khenmet, Itaweret and Noub-Hotep, plus King Hor. Bones showed unusually robust upper-limb muscle attachments matching repetitive archery and weapon use, and healed fractures — suggesting these royals really trained and hunted rather than owning ceremonial objects. Note: some researchers have questioned how strongly muscle marks alone prove weapon use, so the interpretation is debated. Sources: ScienceDaily (26 Aug 2026), the Frontiers press release, and the paper's DOI record via Crossref; background from Wikipedia and The Conversation."
