Why is the universe's first green star glowing ghostly green — and what its color reveals

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 00:27 IST
Reading options

Astronomers found the first star that would actually look green to human eyes — a dense "white dwarf" glowing green because of sodium in its atmosphere.

Why is the universe's first green star glowing ghostly green?

1. Stars usually come in red, white, and blue

When you look up at the night sky, every star you can see with your eyes is basically white or yellow or red. That is because a star's color is mostly set by how hot it is — much like a campfire log that glows red at its coolest edge and blazes white-hot at its center.

  • Cool stars look red or orange
  • Medium-hot stars (like our Sun) look white or yellow
  • Hot stars look pale blue or blue-white

That is the whole rainbow, right? Well… not quite. Astronomers have now found the first star that would actually look GREEN to human eyes.

2. The green star is a "white dwarf"

The green star is not a normal star — it is what is left behind when a Sun-like star dies. When our Sun runs out of fuel in about five billion years, its core will collapse into an incredibly dense ball called a white dwarf. It will be about the mass of the Sun squeezed into a space the size of Earth.

The green one is named WDJ1330+6435. It does not look green because it is hot or cold — it looks green because of what is floating in its atmosphere.

3. Sodium makes the star glow green

The white dwarf is so dense that its atmosphere is almost like water — millions of times thicker than Earth's air. Floating in that thick soup is sodium — the same stuff in table salt.

Here is the clever part: sodium in a normal, lighter atmosphere absorbs only specific, narrow colors of light. But in the crushing density of a white dwarf, that sodium absorbs a much wider patch of light — eating up most of the yellow, orange, and red wavelengths.

What is left? The light that reflects back is blue and green — and to our eyes, that adds up to a ghostly green color. It is like the star is wearing a sodium-colored filter that blocks everything except green.

4. Not just one — a whole rainbow of oddballs

The team, led by Dr. Mark Hollands from the University of Warwick, scanned thousands of white dwarfs across the sky. They did not find just one weird color — they found whole families of them:

  • Carbon-rich white dwarfs can glow pink or purple
  • Sodium-stained ones glow green
  • Others with leftover bits of asteroids and comets show yellow or cyan tints

Because no star is close enough for us to see its true color directly, the scientists used a trick: they split each star's light into a rainbow (a spectrum) and then ran it through a computer model of how human eyes and brains actually see color.

Try this at home

Grab a prism or a glass of water in a sunny spot. Hold a white piece of paper behind it and watch sunlight split into a rainbow. Now imagine that each color in the rainbow can be "turned down" by something invisible in the air — just like how WDJ1330+6435 loses its yellow and red light, leaving only green. You just recreated a mini version of how astronomers find star colors!

Fun Fact

White dwarfs get their name because the first ones discovered were very hot and looked white or blue-white. Now we know they can be green, pink, and even purple — the name "white dwarf" stuck even though they are anything but just white!

For grown-ups

White dwarf stars are the dense leftover cores of stars like our Sun. A team led by Dr. Mark Hollands at the University of Warwick discovered that white dwarfs can glow in unexpected colors — including green — because trace elements (sodium, carbon, calcium) in their ultra-dense atmospheres absorb specific wavelengths of light. The first genuinely green star identified is WDJ1330+6435, which has accreted sodium-rich rocky material that absorbs yellow, orange, and red light, leaving the star appearing ghostly green. The findings were published in RAS Techniques and Instruments (RASTI). Most stars get their color from temperature alone (red=hot, blue=hotter), but white dwarfs are millions of times denser than the Sun's surface, so chemistry matters as much as heat.
