"How will NASA test the Artemis 3 moon-lander in space?"

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 17:47 IST IST
Reading options

"NASA just laid out a step-by-step plan for the Artemis III moon-lander test flight — a practice run in low-Earth orbit that will bring astronauts one step closer to walking on the Moon's south pole."

How will NASA test the moon lander before astronauts walk on the Moon?

A Practice Run Closer to Home

Before anyone steps onto the Moon again, NASA wants to rehearse the trickiest part of the mission — docking with a lunar lander — while still close to Earth. On October 23, 2026, NASA unveiled its detailed plan for Artemis III, a special test flight that will happen in low-Earth orbit (the region where the International Space Station lives), not on the lunar surface.

Think of it like a full dress rehearsal: astronauts will practice opening hatches, checking life-support systems, and docking with a lander — all in the safety of Earth's neighborhood, so help is never far away.

Three Rockets, One Big Team

The Artemis III test brings together three of the most powerful rockets in the world, all launching from Florida's Space Coast:

  1. New Glenn (by Blue Origin) lifts off first, carrying the Blue Moon lander to orbit.
  2. SLS (NASA's Space Launch System) follows, carrying the Orion capsule with four astronauts: Randy Bresnik, Andre Douglas, Frank Rubio, and Europe's Luca Parmitano.
  3. Starship (by SpaceX) launches last and meets Orion in orbit for a short docking test.

Both Blue Moon and Starship are being tested for the same job — landing astronauts on the Moon. NASA is keeping both options open to make sure at least one works.

Orbit, Dock, Practice, Head Home

After SLS puts Orion into space, the capsule will fly in a stretched "eggbeater" orbit — as close as 29 km (18 miles) to Earth and as far as 1,806 km (1,122 miles) away — before settling into a steady orbit about 555 km (345 miles) up.

There, Orion will dock with Blue Moon for two days: the crew will open the hatch, step inside, test the air systems, and rehearse emergency drills. A couple of days later, Starship arrives for a quick ~2-hour docking demo. Orion's crew will stay safely inside their own capsule during the Starship test.

After about two weeks, Orion will splash down in the Pacific Ocean — a full practice run before the real lunar landing in 2028.

Try this at home

Grab a flashlight and a small ball. Shine the light on the ball to represent Earth, and place a larger ball (or crumpled paper) a few feet away as the Moon. Roll a marble around Earth's orbit — that's Orion. Can you get it to "dock" with the Moon lander? Try adding a second marble that zips in from another angle to represent Starship. Practice makes perfect for astronauts!

Fun Fact

Orion's highly elliptical orbit (the "egg-shaped" path) was chosen on purpose. Flying close to Earth and then far away lets engineers check how the spacecraft handles thermal changes — the scorching heat and chilly cold of different orbits. It's like testing a sleeping bag by spending a night in both a sauna and a freezer!

for-grownups: Every part of this rehearsal — hatch operations, air-system checks, docking, and emergency drills — is designed to reduce risk for the first crewed lunar landing, planned for the Moon's south pole in 2028.
