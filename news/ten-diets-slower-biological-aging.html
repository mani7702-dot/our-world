10 different diets all slow down your body's clock — here's why

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 11:34 IST
Reading options

Scientists studied 10 healthy eating patterns — from Mediterranean to plant-based to DASH — and found they all slow biological aging, measured by DNA methylation clocks.

Your body has two ages: the number of birthdays you have had, and the age your cells are actually acting like.

Scientists call the second one biological age. And a brand-new study found that ten different healthy diets can all turn down your body's internal clock — even though no single diet is a magic bullet.

How do scientists read a cell's age?

You might think age is just a number of birthdays. But every cell also carries a biological age that scientists can measure with something called an epigenetic clock.

Here is the neat part: your DNA sequence is like a cookbook of recipes, but cells add tiny chemical "bookmarks" to the pages. These bookmarks — called DNA methylation — change in predictable patterns as you grow older. So scientists can estimate how "old" a cell is by reading those chemical marks, without needing to change the actual recipes.

The German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) studied about 7,500 people. They looked at three different epigenetic clocks — all based on DNA methylation — and measured each person's biological age. Then they asked: how does what you eat change the numbers?

What ten diets all had in common

The ten eating patterns all came from well-known healthy diets: Mediterranean, Nordic, plant-based, DASH, and others. Each person got a score for how closely they followed each pattern.

Across all three clocks, every single diet was linked to slower biological aging. People who ate more in line with any of the ten patterns had cells that looked younger than their calendar years.

The differences between the diets were modest — none was dramatically better than another. The message is simple: healthy eating matters, and there is more than one way to eat well.

The surprise: smokers benefited most

One unexpected result: the link between healthy eating and slower aging was stronger in smokers than in non-smokers. Smokers who ate healthier saw bigger drops in their biological age.

Scientists think this is because smoking causes so much cellular damage that there is more room for improvement. But this does not mean smoking is safe — the study authors are clear that smoking is very harmful to health.

Why your kitchen choices matter to your cells

Even though the effect size is modest, the study shows something powerful: what you put on your plate leaves measurable marks on your cells. Better eating means your cells' chemical bookmarks stay closer to a younger pattern.

This is one of the cleanest pieces of evidence yet that aging is not a fixed number — it is a process we can gently nudge, diet by diet.

Try this at home

You can see "clocks" all around you! Ask a grown-up to help you time how long a banana takes to turn brown when you leave it on the counter. Then compare it to a banana in a closed paper bag (which ages faster thanks to trapped gas), and a banana in the fridge (which ages differently). You're watching chemical change — one of the same forces that drives cellular aging — just on a banana scale.

Fun fact

Your DNA is about 3 billion "letters" long — and it would stretch about 2 meters in every cell of your body. If you lined up all the DNA from every cell in a human, it would reach to the sun and back dozens of times. Yet tiny chemical bookmarks on that vast string are enough to tell the story of how old your cells really are.

For grown-ups

The short version: DZNE researchers measured biological age in ~7,500 adults using three epigenetic clocks that read DNA methylation patterns — chemical bookmarks on DNA that change predictably as people grow older. Every one of 10 healthy dietary patterns (Mediterranean, Nordic, plant-based, DASH, etc.) was linked to slower biological aging. The differences between diets were modest in absolute terms; the big message is that healthy eating goes hand-in-hand with slower aging. Published in Nature Communications (2026). This is observational research, not a prescription.
