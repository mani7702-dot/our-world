"What Happens When You Shine a UV Light on a Fire Salamander?"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 14:22 IST
Reading options

"Scientists have discovered that Europe's familiar black-and-yellow fire salamanders glow bright green and cyan under ultraviolet light — and their toxic skin secretions keep glowing for more than a day after they leave the body."

A hidden light show in a familiar animal

Some of Europe's most recognizable wild animals are the fire salamander — glossy black skin dotted with bright yellow spots, slow and steady on a forest path. People have been studying them for more than 250 years, writing down what they look like, how they move, and where they live.

So when a scientist shone a special ultraviolet light on one and saw it glow green and cyan, it was a real surprise. A whole new trick, hiding in plain sight on an animal everyone thought they already knew.

The glow that needs a flashlight

Let's start with an important word: biofluorescence. This is not the same as bioluminescence. Fireflies make their own light with a chemical reaction inside their bodies — that is bioluminescence. Biofluorescence is different: the animal cannot glow in the dark on its own. It needs a light shining on it first.

Special molecules in the salamander's skin soak up ultraviolet light — the kind humans cannot see — and then give that energy back as light we can see: bright green and cyan. Turn the UV light off, and the glow stops.

The science team, led by Bernat Burriel-Carranza in Barcelona, found that the glow is strongest along the yellow belly and sides of the salamander's body. That is where the skin glands live — and those glands also produce the salamander's toxic secretions.

Toxic goo that keeps glowing

Here is a remarkable detail: the glowing compounds are not just in the skin. When researchers tested the slippery secretions from the salamander's glands, those secretions glowed too — and they kept glowing for more than 24 hours after they left the animal's body.

That tells us something important: the fluorescent molecules are traveling around inside the salamander's body, not just painted on the surface. The glowing tracks right along the yellow spots — which is no accident, because in a fire salamander, those bright colors always line up with the poison glands.

Why glow at all?

Honestly? Scientists are not sure yet. They have two main guesses:

First guess: a night-time chat. Fire salamanders are active at night and in damp, dark forests. A green glow could help one salamander spot another in the dark — like a tiny headlight on the forest floor. Under a full moon (which has more blue and UV light than daylight), the fluorescence might create little green highlights along their yellow stripes, making them easier to find for friends — or mates.

Second guess: a bigger warning sign. Fire salamanders are genuinely toxic. Their skin makes chemicals called samandarin and samandarone that make predators feel very sick. Their black-and-yellow pattern already warns others to stay away. The glow might be an extra signal: "This one tastes terrible — don't even think about it."

Both ideas are still just that — ideas. Nobody has tested them yet. But being able to say "we don't know yet" is part of how science works.

A reminder about salamanders and fire

The name "fire salamander" has nothing to do with glowing. For centuries, people believed salamanders could walk through flames unharmed — or even that they were born from fire itself. The real reason is much simpler: salamanders like damp, dark places, including piles of old logs. When someone tossed a handful of firewood onto a campfire, out would scramble a startled salamander. It looked like the animal had just walked out of the flames.

Fire salamanders are not fireproof — and they are definitely not safe to pick up. They release toxins through their skin, so the right move with any wild amphibian is always the same: look, do not touch, and wash your hands afterward if you did.

Try this at home

You can see the same magic using kitchen items and a UV blacklight flashlight (ask a grown-up first, and never shine UV light into anyone's eyes — UV is the same kind of light that gives you sunburn):

  1. Pour tonic water into a clear glass in a dark room. Shine the blacklight on it. It glows pale blue — quinine is the glowing ingredient.
  2. Draw a picture with a yellow highlighter, let it dry, then shine the light on it.
  3. Try a banana peel, a leaf, honey, or olive oil. Compare which ones glow and which just sit there.

The tonic water glows because a molecule in it absorbs invisible light and gives back visible light — the very same trick the salamander's skin is doing.

Fun Fact: The European fire salamander has been studied for over 250 years, yet scientists only discovered it glows under UV light in 2026. Even the most familiar animals can still hold surprises when seen with new tools.

For Grown-Ups: The glow is a survival signal — yellow spots that warn predators the salamander is poisonous, now revealed to also glow under moonlight.

For grown-ups

"New research led by Bernat Burriel-Carranza at the Museum of Natural Sciences in Barcelona, with co-authors from the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology (Martin Kaltenpoth, Andrés Brunetti) and the Institute of Evolutionary Biology (Salvador Carranza), discovered that European fire salamanders (Salamandra salamandra) fluoresce green and cyan under ultraviolet light. The fluorescence comes mainly from compounds in their skin glands and the toxic secretions those glands release — secretions that can remain fluorescent for more than 24 hours after leaving the body. The phenomenon works just like biofluorescence in other animals: molecules absorb invisible UV light and re-emit it as visible green/cyan light. This is NOT bioluminescence (the chemical self-light of fireflies); it requires an external light source. The biological purpose is still being studied — leading hypotheses are communication (helping salamanders spot each other at night) and aposematism (making their existing yellow-black warning colors even more visible to predators). Fire salamanders are genuinely toxic: their skin produces steroidal alkaloids like samandarin and samandarone from parotid glands, confirming the long-known connection between their warning colors and chemical defenses (Wikipedia: Fire salamander). The species is classified as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List due to the introduced chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans. The research was published in Royal Society Open Science (DOI 10.1098/rsos.251991) and involved scientists from the Natural Sciences Museum of Barcelona, Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology, and IBE (CSIC-UPF). Facts cross-checked against ScienceDaily and Wikipedia."
