"Can Knitting a Sweater Teach a Fabric to Think? Harvard Scientists Say Yes"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at "18:35" IST
Reading options

"Harvard engineers turned ordinary machine knitting into smart fabrics that snap between shapes, feel movement, and even work like a light switch — all made from yarn."

Can Knitting a Sweater Teach a Fabric to Think? Harvard Scientists Say Yes

A sweater keeps you warm. A pair of gloves keeps your fingers cozy. But what if the same machine that knits a hat could also knit a fabric that changes shape, feels your movement, and even turns a light on and off?

That is exactly what engineers at Harvard University have just done. They turned everyday knitting into a clever new tool.

What Makes These Fabrics So Special?

Ordinary fabric flops around and stays floppy. But the Harvard team found a way to knit fabric that snaps — like a light switch — into more than one shape, and stays there until you snap it again.

Scientists call this multistability. It means a single object can happily sit in several different stable shapes. Think of a snap bracelet or a folding chair that clicks into place.

The trick? They used extra-stretchy yarns and a knitting method called plating, which puts different yarns on each side of the fabric. The dense, thick cloth naturally curls into 3D shapes — just like the bottom edge of a T-shirt that curls up after you cut it.

From Sweaters to Smart Devices

Once the team could make fabric that snaps, they added a secret ingredient: conducting yarn that carries tiny electric signals.

That let them build some amazing things:

  • A soft switch that turns an LED light on and off as the fabric snaps back and forth.
  • A wearable sensor worn over the knee or elbow that counts your steps when it snaps as you walk.
  • A colour-changing lamp shade with three switches, each clicking on a different colour.

The best part: they made these on knitting machines just like the ones in real clothing factories. That means these smart fabrics could be made quickly and cheaply someday.

Why It Matters

This is a peek into the future of "programmable textiles" — clothes that can sense, move, and respond. Imagine a jacket that gives you a gentle squeeze to remind you to sit up straight, or a bandage that changes shape as a wound heals.

Because the fabrics bend and stretch softly, they could be perfect for helping people and robots move comfortably together.

Try this at home

Make your own "snap test": Take a stretchy hairband or an old sock and gently pull it. Notice how it springs back? Real multistable fabrics are built to spring back into two or three different resting shapes — not just one. Materials scientists study these snap-through moves to design new devices.

Fun Fact

The researchers used the same weft knitting method that factories use to make ordinary hats and gloves. So the very machine that made your winter hat could one day make a sensor that counts your steps!

For grown-ups

"Researchers at the Harvard John A. Paulson School of Engineering and Applied Sciences (SEAS), led by recent Ph.D. graduate Kausalya Mahadevan in the lab of Prof. Katia Bertoldi, showed that standard industrial weft knitting can produce multistable mechanical metamaterials from yarn alone. Using elastic yarns and a two-sided technique called plating, the team created dense knits that snap between multiple stable 3D shapes. By embedding conductive yarns, they built soft stretchable switches and a knee/elbow sensor that reads step counts via an Arduino. The work is published in Advanced Functional Materials (2026) and was supported by NSF grant DMR-2011754, ARO MURI W911NF-22-1-0219, and ONR DURIP N00014-19-1-2220."
