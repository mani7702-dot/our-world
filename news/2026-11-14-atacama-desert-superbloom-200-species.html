How does the driest desert on Earth turn into a flower garden?

By Our World Published 14 November 2026 at "02:43" IST
Reading options

"Chile's Atacama Desert is the driest non-polar place on Earth. This spring, after record winter rain, more than 200 kinds of flowers woke up out of the sand - some in patches where nobody has ever seen them bloom."

There is a place in Chile where rain can go years without falling. It is called the Atacama Desert, and it is the driest desert on Earth that is not near the poles. Some weather stations there have recorded almost no rain at all for decades.

This spring, the Atacama is covered in flowers.

More than 200 kinds of native plants are blooming across northern Chile right now - pink, purple, yellow and white - in what scientists are calling the widest and most colourful "flowering desert" in about 30 years. Locals have a name for it: desierto florido, the flowering desert.

1. The rain had to go deep

Flowers in a desert are not a coincidence. They need a very particular kind of rain.

In July and August, unusually heavy rain fell across northern Chile - in some places the most since records began in the 1950s. That water did something ordinary desert drizzle never does: it soaked deep into the ground, past the top layer of sand that dries out in days.

Down there, waiting, were millions of seeds, bulbs and tubers.

2. Seeds that waited for years

Some of these plants have a clever trick. Their seeds stay asleep - dormant - in the dry soil, sometimes for decades, doing nothing at all. No water means no sprouting, which is a good plan in a place where a seedling that sprouts at the wrong time would die.

Then a big rain arrives, the soil stays damp long enough, and the whole buried seed bank switches on at once.

Researchers say some patches are flowering where flowers have never been recorded before - which suggests those bulbs had been asleep since before scientists started studying the phenomenon in detail in 1997.

The flowers you would see there include ananaucas (red and yellow lilies), huillis (small white flowers) and suspiros (lilac and blue). The first pale yellow flowers came in late August; by October the desert was streaked with pink and purple.

3. A whole season, then quiet again

This is not a garden that stays. The plants have two to three months to sprout, flower, make seeds and finish their whole life cycle before the ground dries out again. Then the desert goes back to looking empty.

Officials think the bloom could spread across close to a quarter of the Atacama region - thousands of square kilometres. Part of it is inside Llanos de Challe National Park, which was created partly to protect the areas where flowers come up thickest, and there is a push to make the park bigger to cover ground that bloomed this year.

Scientists are also busy collecting seeds while they can, for a seed bank kept in the desert that stores Chile's plant diversity for the future.

Try this at home: wake up a sleeping seed

You can watch dormancy work with a dried bean from the kitchen.

  1. Put three dry beans in a dry jar and leave them on a shelf for a week. Nothing happens - they are asleep, not dead.
  2. Now put one on a wet paper towel inside a sealed bag, and leave it somewhere warm.
  3. Check it every day for three or four days.

The bean swells and a tiny root pushes out. Water was the whole switch. That is exactly what the Atacama's rain did - only the desert waited years for its turn.

For grown-ups

"The Atacama is a hyperarid desert (mean annual rainfall often under 2 mm) where plant cover depends on episodic rainfall linked to El Nino conditions. Heavy rain in July and August 2026 soaked deeper than usual into the soil, reaching seed and bulb banks that ordinary seasonal drizzle never touches; the resulting 'desierto florido' (flowering desert) is monitored by Chile's forestry agency CONAF and researched by INIA, which is collecting seed from this season's plants. Reported scale: 200+ native species flowering, the widest display in about 30 years and possibly the largest since detailed study began in 1997; officials projected flowering across roughly a quarter of the Atacama region. Two honest caveats: 'largest ever' is still being assessed rather than confirmed, and ecologists worry that more frequent El Nino rain could favour invasive grasses over the native bulbs and bring more trampling from visitors. Bloom cycles like this last only two to three months."
