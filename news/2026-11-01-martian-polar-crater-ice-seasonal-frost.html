"Why Do Frosty Craters on Mars Keep Changing?"

By Our World Published 01 November 2026 at "14:20" IST
Reading options

"On Mars, a scarf of frost appears inside the craters of the north pole each winter and vanishes again in summer. A ten-year photo study just revealed that each crater follows its own private weather."

Look at a photo of Mars's north pole and you will spot something odd. Around the edge of the bright white ice cap sit dozens of small craters — dents punched into the ground by space rocks long ago. And here is the strange part: the stuff lying on the floor of those craters does not stay the same. It seems to appear, then fade away, again and again.

A new study has finally watched that flickering closely. Using a camera in orbit, scientists followed 36 craters on the Martian north pole for almost ten years — about a full decade of Mars time.

A cap that wears a winter coat

Mars has seasons, just like Earth. It also has polar ice caps — giant frozen patches at its poles. The northern cap is made of water ice, though a thin layer of frozen carbon dioxide (the gas in fizzy drinks) settles on it when that pole tilts away from the Sun.

In Martian winter, frost forms on and around the craters. In summer, sunlight turns that frost back into gas — a change called sublimation (say: sub-lih-MAY-shun). Because the frost is bright and the rock beneath is dark, each passing season changes how the crater looks from above.

Photographing the same spots for ten years

The pictures came from HiRISE, a powerful camera aboard NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter. It has been circling Mars since 2006, snapping sharp photos of the surface. The team compared images of the same 36 craters taken across roughly Mars Years 28 to 38 — and 32 of the 36 showed their bright icy patches growing and shrinking with the seasons.

But the craters did not all change together. In any given year, only some of them looked different from the year before. Others stayed almost exactly the same.

Tiny weather, huge planet

That patchiness is the real discovery. The scientists think each crater follows its own local weather — shaped by its shape, depth and the little hills and dips around it — instead of responding to one giant, planet-wide change. On a world with no oceans and air thinner than a whisper, small places can have big effects.

Learning how this top icy layer comes and goes helps scientists read the climate history buried in the polar layers, like pages in a very old book.

Fun fact: When Martian winter arrives, roughly a quarter to a third of Mars's whole atmosphere freezes solid onto the poles. The air itself turns into a kind of snow!

Try this at home

You can copy the Mars craters in your kitchen. Fill a shallow bowl with very cold ice cubes and set it on a table. Let it sit a minute, then wash your hands and quickly look at your fingertips — or breathe gently on a cold window. Do it again later and compare. The misty patch appears, fades and reappears depending on the temperature and dampness around it, just like the frost inside those Martian craters.

For grown-ups

"A study published in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Planets by G. K. Shore, M. E. Landis, V. G. Rangarajan, S. Byrne, C. M. Dundas, S. S. Sutton, A. X. Wilcoski and P. O. Hayne (DOI 10.1029/2026JE009888) used nearly a full Mars decade of NASA Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter HiRISE imagery (Mars Years 28-38, 2006-2025) to track bright floor deposits inside 36 small impact craters on Mars's Northern Residual Ice Cap, the uppermost layer of the North Polar Layered Deposits. 32 of the 36 craters showed changes in their bright deposits, expanding and shrinking with the seasons; seasonal frost on crater exteriors may drive much of the brightness change. The authors report that the changes are localised rather than cap-wide, modulated by local topography, crater geometry and microclimate. Understanding the mass balance of this top layer helps decode the climate record locked in the polar layers. Mars's axial tilt swings far more than Earth's, so its climate shifts are larger; the paper adds that local climates can still produce their own changes. Unlike Earth's water-ice caps, Mars's north polar cap is water ice while its south polar cap includes carbon dioxide (dry) ice; roughly 25-30% of Mars's thin atmosphere freezes onto a pole each winter."
