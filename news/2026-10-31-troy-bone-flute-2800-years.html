What Did the Music of Troy Sound Like? A 2,800-Year-Old Flute Gives Us a Clue

A tiny instrument, a huge find

For about 150 years, archaeologists have been digging at the ancient city of Troy in northwestern Turkey. Over all that time, they have unearthed gold headbands, more than 100 two-handled wine cups, and Bronze Age weapons — but never a single musical instrument. That changed this year. Researchers announced they had found a flute carved from bone, roughly 2,800 years old. It is the first musical instrument ever discovered at Troy.

Why "2,800 years" matters

The flute came from a layer of the site dating to the eighth century BCE — the same era when the poet Homer is thought to have composed The Iliad and The Odyssey, the two epic poems that made Troy famous. Troy's ruins are stacked in nine layers, each from a different period of the city's long life. Scholars once believed the city was nearly empty during this time, but newer research shows people were still living there — and, it now seems, still making music.

What the discovery tells us

Written sources and finds from elsewhere in the region hinted that music was important in Trojan religious ceremonies and daily life, yet no physical proof had ever turned up. Turkey's culture and tourism minister, Mehmet Nuri Ersoy, said the flute gives the first direct archaeological evidence of Troy's musical culture. The excavation's lead archaeologist, Rüstem Aslan, also pointed out a fun coincidence: earlier this year, a composer used a similar ancient instrument to craft the eerie "siren song" heard in a new film version of The Odyssey.

Try this at home

You can make a simple flute from a drinking straw! Flatten one end with your teeth and snip it into a point, then blow gently while you cut small holes along the straw. Each hole changes the note. Your straw flute and the Trojan bone flute both work the same way — a tube of air vibrating at different pitches.

Fun fact

Troy is a real place, not just a legend. Its ruins sit at a site called Hisarlık in modern Turkey and became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1998. People have been studying it for well over a century — and it is still surprising us with new treasures.
