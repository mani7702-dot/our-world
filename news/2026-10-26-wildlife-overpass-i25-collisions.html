How a Giant Bridge Over the Highway Is Letting Colorado's Wildlife Cross Safely

The Bridge That Lets Animals Walk Over Traffic

Imagine trying to walk across a six-lane highway — every 30 seconds a car roars past at 75 miles an hour. Now imagine doing it with a baby in your arms or a whole herd of elk trailing behind you.

That is the everyday challenge wildlife face along Interstate 25 between Denver and Colorado Springs. But since late last year, one giant bridge has given them a safe path over the traffic.

What Is the Greenland Wildlife Overpass?

The Greenland Wildlife Overpass is a 200-foot-wide by 209-foot-long bridge — about the size of three school buses parked end-to-end — that stretches across all six lanes of I-25 near Larkspur, Colorado. It opened in December 2025 and connects 39,000 acres of open habitat on both sides of the highway.

But the bridge is only half the story. To make sure animals actually use it, engineers built five hidden underpass tunnels for smaller creatures like rabbits and frogs, plus miles of sturdy fencing that gently funnels wildlife toward the crossing points — kind of like a hallway that guides them safely over the road.

The whole project cost about $15 million, paid for through a mix of state funds and a driver opt-in fee that started in January 2026.

The Results Are In: 91% Fewer Crashes

Here is where the happy news gets even better:

  1. Before the bridge: Drivers hit wild animals about once a day during migration seasons along the Larkspur–Monument stretch of I-25.
  2. After the bridge: In the first 10 months, there were only two wildlife-vehicle collisions — a drop of 91%.
  3. Travel delays fell by 9%, too, because there are far fewer crash cleanups blocking traffic.

Colorado Parks and Wildlife spokesperson Bridget O'Rourke said the data tells a clear story: "Wildlife overpasses and underpasses and funneling animals to have a safe path across the roads — it's been statistically proven that it's successful."

It's not just the big guys using it. Trail cameras caught pronghorn antelope crossing for the first time in 20 to 30 years, and biologists spotted northern leopard frogs, bunnies, and even tiny critters sneaking through the underpasses.

A Win for Animals AND Drivers

When animals can move freely between the mountains and the meadows, their populations stay healthy. That means strong genes, better food sources, and more room for families to grow. For drivers, fewer crashes mean safer roads, shorter delays, and less worry about hitting a deer or moose at night.

Try this at home

Look for wildlife near your neighborhood roads: stop signs, crosswalks, and railroad tracks can be little "wildlife overpasses" too. Ask a grown-up to help you draw a plan for a tiny wildlife crossing in your backyard — even a piece of plywood over a garden path gives bugs and beetles a dry highway to travel on!

Fun Fact

Banff National Park in Canada has been using wildlife crossings since 1980. Over that time, 24 bridges and tunnels have recorded more than 84,000 animal crossings — that is like the entire school cheering in the gym, all walking (or swimming, or climbing) safely across one highway!

The Greenland Wildlife Overpass was completed in late 2025 in Douglas County, Colorado, at a cost of approximately $15 million. The structure includes a 200 feet × 209 feet (61 m × 64 m) vegetated bridge over six lanes of I-25, five wildlife underpass tunnels, and miles of directional wildlife fencing. Monitoring by Colorado Department of Transportation (CDOT) and Colorado Parks and Wildlife (CPW) showed a 91% reduction in wildlife-vehicle collisions during the first ten months, with only two reported strikes. The project connects 39,000 acres of ponderosa-pine and mixed-conifer habitat critical for elk, mule deer, pronghorn, and black bear migration corridors. Six additional wildlife crossing systems are now in design across Colorado, including one custom-built for bighorn sheep.
