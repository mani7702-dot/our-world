"Could Einstein’s equations explain the universe’s accelerating expansion without dark energy?"

By Our World Published 29 October 2026 at 09:30 IST
Reading options

"Cosmologists add a mysterious 'dark energy' to Einstein's equations to explain why the universe is expanding faster and faster. But a new mathematical study suggests the acceleration might already be hidden inside Einstein's original theory — no extra energy needed."

Could Einstein's equations explain the universe's accelerating expansion without dark energy?

The puzzle of the runaway universe

Picture the universe as a balloon with dots drawn on it. As you blow it up, every dot moves away from every other dot. That's how our cosmos expands. For most of cosmic history, the dots were pulling back together — gravity acted like a gentle brake, slowing the expansion down.

But then, about five billion years ago, something strange happened. The expansion didn't slow. It started speeding up — like the balloon suddenly began inflating faster and faster.

To explain this surprise, scientists invented a mysterious ingredient: dark energy. It's an invisible force that makes up 68% of the universe, but nobody knows what it actually is. Einstein's equations of general relativity are then modified to include it as a "cosmological constant" (Λ), giving us the standard model called ΛCDM.

A wobbly math trick

Here's the thing: to get the universe to accelerate, scientists have to add that cosmological constant by hand. It's like balancing a pencil on its tip to prop open a door — technically possible, but any tiny breeze knocks it over.

Now, a new study from the University of California, Davis argues the whole trick might be unnecessary. The researchers say the standard mathematical solutions used to describe an expanding universe — called Friedmann spacetimes — are actually unstable. Like that pencil, the slightest perturbation makes them collapse or change behavior entirely.

Their calculations suggest that when you let these math solutions be unstable (as real physical systems must be), cosmic acceleration emerges naturally from Einstein's equations alone. No dark energy required.

Why unstable math matters

In physics, an unstable solution is like a ball balanced on a hilltop. It looks fine in theory, but the moment anything disturbs it — a breeze, a vibration — it rolls away and never returns. Physicists generally consider unstable solutions "not physical," because we never observe them in nature.

The UC Davis team applied this idea to the Friedmann model — the math that underpins our entire picture of the cosmos. They found that at both the smallest and largest scales, these solutions are unstable to radial perturbations (disturbances that spread outward from a center, like ripples in a pond).

This means the standard model might be describing the universe in a way that can't actually last. As the researchers put it: the universe should behave like the standard model near a central region, but far from that center, it should naturally show the acceleration we observe — even without adding dark energy.

Try this at home

Take a pencil and try to balance it perfectly on your fingertip, point-up. Notice how it wobbles? That wobble grows and the pencil soon falls over. The math solution for a perfectly balanced pencil is "unstable" — just like the Friedmann spacetime the researchers studied. Small disturbances always grow, and the system moves to something completely different.

Now imagine that wobble IS the accelerating expansion — it was hiding inside the equations all along, waiting for the right moment to emerge.

Fun Fact

Einstein originally introduced the cosmological constant in 1917 to keep the universe static (he thought it wasn't changing). After Edwin Hubble proved the universe was expanding in 1929, Einstein called it his "biggest blunder." Then in 1998, astronomers discovered the expansion was accelerating — and the cosmological constant came roaring back as the explanation for dark energy!

For grown-ups

"A study published in Proceedings of the Royal Society A by researchers at UC Davis examines the stability of Friedmann spacetimes within Einstein's field equations. They argue these solutions are unstable to radial perturbations at large scales, meaning small disturbances grow rather than fade. This instability could naturally produce accelerated expansion without invoking a cosmological constant or dark energy. Wikipedia confirms that the Friedmann equations govern cosmic expansion within general relativity, and the cosmological constant Λ (introduced by Einstein in 1917, revived after the 1998 discovery of acceleration) is the standard explanation for dark energy in the ΛCDM model. The new work suggests the instability of Friedmann spacetimes themselves may generate the observed acceleration directly from Einstein-Euler equations, potentially ruling out ΛCDM as a stable long-term solution."
