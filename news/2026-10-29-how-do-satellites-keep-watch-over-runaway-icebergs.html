How Do Satellites Keep Watch Over Runaway Icebergs?

Imagine a giant block of ice bigger than a city silently breaking free and floating away across the ocean — all captured from space by satellites orbiting high above Earth.

1. What Happened to Iceberg A-81?

In January 2023, a massive iceberg named A-81 broke free from the Brunt Ice Shelf in Antarctica. For an iceberg, that sounds slow — but A-81 is on the run! In just three years, it traveled 1,400 miles (2,300 kilometers) north through the icy waters of the Weddell Sea. That's faster than most icebergs move, which usually drift just a few miles per day. Satellites snapped a photo on October 20, 2026, showing the giant block of ice floating near an underwater shelf off the coast of South Georgia.

2. Why Does an Iceberg "Escape"?

Icebergs break off — or calve — from ice shelves. An ice shelf is like a thick, floating platform of ice that sits on top of the ocean, connected to a glacier flowing down from land. When the ice shelf cracks, huge chunks of ice break away and become free-floating icebergs. The Brunt Ice Shelf in Antarctica has been calving multiple large icebergs one after another, and A-81 is one of the biggest. These giant blocks of freshwater ice then drift with ocean currents, sometimes traveling thousands of miles before they melt away.

3. How Can Satellites See It?

NASA's Terra satellite carries an instrument called MODIS — that's the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer. MODIS takes pictures of Earth every single day, watching the whole planet from space. It can spot icebergs because they show up very differently from the rest of the ocean in satellite images. The bright white ice stands out against the darker water, making it easy to track where each iceberg is going. Scientists use these images to count icebergs, measure their size, and predict where they might float — which helps ships steer clear of danger.

4. Why Should We Care?

Every time a big iceberg breaks off and drifts away, it tells scientists something important about our changing climate. Ice shelves act like giant "plugs" that hold back the flow of land ice into the ocean. When too many icebergs and ice shelves melt, sea levels rise. By watching icebergs from space, scientists can keep track of how fast Antarctica's ice is changing — all without ever leaving their desks. And for ships sailing in the Southern Ocean, knowing where icebergs are means safer journeys for everyone.

Try This At Home

On a clear night, go outside with a flashlight and a dark blanket or tarp. Shine the flashlight through the tarp from one side — you'll see it glows! That's a bit like how satellites use light to "see" icebergs. Try dropping a few ice cubes in a bowl of water and watch how they move as you blow across the surface. Notice how the ice floats higher on top — just like a real iceberg, where most of its mass is hidden underwater. Can you track where each piece drifts?

Fun Fact

The largest iceberg ever recorded was even bigger than the state of Rhode Island! In 1956, the USS Glacier spotted a tabular Antarctic iceberg that measured 335 kilometers long and 97 kilometers wide. And iceberg A-81? At 600 square miles, it's one of the top 10 biggest icebergs ever photographed by satellites.

For Grown-ups

The Space.com article describes NASA's Terra satellite (carrying the MODIS instrument) capturing the Antarctic iceberg A-81 near an underwater shelf off South Georgia on October 20, 2026. The iceberg, measuring 600 square miles (1,550 square kilometers), calved from the Brunt Ice Shelf in January 2023 and traveled 1,400 miles (2,300 kilometers) north through the Weddell Sea — unusually fast for an iceberg. Wikipedia's "Ice shelf" entry confirms that ice shelves are large platforms of glacial ice floating on the ocean, fed by tributary glaciers, and that calving (the breaking away of ice) is a natural process. Wikipedia's "Iceberg" entry confirms that icebergs are pieces of freshwater ice more than 15 meters long that have broken off from glaciers or ice shelves and are floating freely in open water, and that Antarctic ice shelves commonly produce large tabular (table-top) icebergs. All three sources agree on the key facts: ice shelves feed icebergs through calving, Antarctic tabular icebergs can be enormous, and satellites like Terra's MODIS instrument monitor them from space.
