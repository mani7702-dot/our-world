How did Europe's biggest land animal come back from just 54 survivors?

By Our World Published 28 October 2026 at 02:35 IST
Reading options

Hunters wiped out the last wild European bison in 1919. Today there are about 9,500 of them — and scientists in Poland are trying to give them back the open meadows they really came from.

In 1919, the last wild European bison in Poland was killed in a forest called Białowieża, a huge tangled woodland on the border of Poland and Belarus. Soldiers and hungry people had hunted the big animals during and after World War I. Once there were hundreds. Then there were none out in the wild.

The animal has a name Poles love: wisent (say VEE-sent). They call it the "king of the forest."

It should have been the end of the story. It wasn't.

Saving a species from a zoo

A few bison were still alive in zoos and private parks scattered across Europe. Scientists counted them: 54 in the whole world. Those 54 became the animals that a whole species would be rebuilt from.

In 1929, a breeding enclosure opened in Białowieża, and the first animals were brought back into the forest. They had to be fed and watched, like a farm herd. On September 13, 1952, the first pair finally walked out of that enclosure into the wild woods — free for the first time in 33 years.

Today there are about 9,500 wisent in the world, and roughly 900 of them live in Białowieża. Over the past hundred years, herds have been started in Russia, Germany, Ukraine, Romania, Spain and many other countries. In 2020, the animal's official "how worried should we be?" rating was even lowered, from vulnerable to near threatened.

That's what scientists call a conservation success story: an animal brought back from nothing.

But the animals are not quite free yet

Here is the twist. Wisent spent the last few decades living in forests — deep, dark, tree-filled forests. And in those forests, rangers feed them. Someone drives a truck of hay into a clearing, so the bison stay inside the forest instead of wandering into farm fields and eating the crops.

That works. But it makes the king of the forest a bit less... wild, and a bit more like a very large, very shaggy pet cow.

Scientists think the wisent was never really a forest animal at all. Thousands of years ago, it lived out in the open — in wide grassy meadows and dry steppes, where you could see it standing in the sunlight for a long way.

Which is a strange thought. A bison in a dark forest hides. A bison in a meadow doesn't have anywhere to hide — and it doesn't need to.

Putting the meadow back

So researchers like Rafał Kowalczyk, an ecologist at the Polish Academy of Sciences who has studied wisents in Białowieża for more than 20 years, are working on the next chapter: bringing the meadows back.

In a European Union project, farmers near the forest are being offered grants to keep their meadows open — mowed but not ploughed up, not built over. Open meadow is exactly what bison like to eat.

At first hardly anyone signed up. Then the workshops got going, and a couple of dozen farmers joined. Something small and good changed along the way: the farmers stopped chasing the bison away from their land. Now they talk about them as our bison, and stand around watching as the whole meadow turns into a bison crowd, heads down in the grass, everywhere at once.

Rangers are also careful about how much they feed. The wild bison have to be interesting enough to stay inside the forest — because a bison that wanders off can trample a farmer's crops, and a farmer with wrecked crops is a farmer who doesn't want bison nearby.

One big risk of a small family

There's a catch. Remember those 54 survivors? Almost every wisent alive today is descended from an even smaller group — around a dozen animals, and most of their genes trace back to two of them.

When everyone is related to everyone, they all share the same weaknesses. A single virus or parasite could spread through herd after herd and take them down in weeks. Rangers worry about exactly that.

So "back from the brink" isn't the finish line. It's the part of the race where you have to look after the animal even harder.

Try this at home: Pick one simple thing — a small animal, or a big one — and trace its family tree back and back. Keep going until the list stops growing. Notice how few names you really need to be related to every cousin you've ever met. That's how a species of thousands comes down to a handful of ancestors.

For grown-ups

The European bison (Bison bonasus) is the continent's heaviest land animal, and it has recovered from an extreme population bottleneck. After the last wild individuals were killed in the 1920s, the species was kept alive in captivity; today's gene pool derives overwhelmingly from a lowland line traced back to a single founding pair, which makes the species inbred and skews survival among multiple stable subpopulations — measures of viable population matter more than raw headcount for its long-term survival. The Polish herd-management project is trying to shift the ecological baseline from "supplemented forest herd" back toward "grassland-and-steppe species" by restoring open meadows adjacent to the Białowieża Forest, because the species was only adopted into closed woodlands as its natural range diminished. Numbers to know: ~9,500 worldwide, ~900 free-ranging in Białowieża, with the global population growing roughly 15-18% yearly.
