"Why are some monkey brains smooth instead of wrinkly — and what it teaches us about our own?"

Why are some monkey brains smooth instead of wrinkly — and what it teaches us about our own?

Look at a walnut and you are looking at a human brain: all ridges and grooves, folds and wrinkles. But some monkeys have brains that are almost perfectly smooth — like a peeled grape. Why the difference? And what does it tell us about how our own big, wrinkly brains grew?

The smooth monkey puzzle

The common marmoset is a tiny monkey from South America that fits in your hand. But while it is small all over, something surprised scientists: its brain is unusually smooth. Most primates — including humans, apes, and even bigger monkeys — have brains full of folds. The marmoset stands out as an exception.

"The ancestor of all modern primates probably had some folding already," says Dr. Michael Heide from the German Primate Center. "But marmosets lost it along the way."

The cell factories that build brains

Inside every developing brain are special cells called neural progenitor cells — tiny factories that pump out neurons (nerve cells). The more neurons these factories make, the bigger the brain grows, and the more folds appear on the surface.

Scientists grew mini-brains — tiny 3D structures called brain organoids — from both human and marmoset cells. What they found:

  1. Marmoset cells divide more slowly than human cells.
  2. Marmoset cells are simpler — they have fewer branches and extensions.
  3. Marmoset cells have a shorter growth window — they stop multiplying sooner.

All three differences combine to make fewer neurons overall, which means a smaller brain surface and far fewer folds.

What smooth brains teach us

These findings help explain how small changes at the cellular level can lead to big differences in brain size and shape across evolution. Our primate cousins — great apes and humans — evolved ways to keep those cell factories running longer and busier, packing in billions more neurons.

That extra folding lets our brains pack a huge surface area inside a skull that still fits through a mother's birth canal — a trick marmosets simply did not need.

Try this at home

Grab a balloon and a permanent marker. Draw a few dots on the deflated balloon, then inflate it slowly. Watch how the dots spread apart and the surface wrinkles as the balloon stretches. The balloon skin is like brain tissue: when it grows faster than the space around it, it folds. The marmoset's "balloon" simply did not stretch as much.

Fun Fact

A human brain has about 86 billion neurons. A marmoset brain has only around 250 million — about the same number as a mouse, even though marmosets are primates. That's why a grape-sized primate brain can look so smooth.

for-grownups: New research from the German Primate Center (DPZ) used brain organoids — miniature 3D brain structures grown from stem cells — to compare human and common marmoset development. Scientists found three key differences in neural progenitor cells (the stem cells that produce neurons): marmoset cells divide significantly more slowly than human cells, their cell structures are simpler with fewer branching processes, and they have a shorter window during which they can rapidly multiply. Together, these factors produce far fewer neurons, resulting in a smaller cerebral cortex with minimal surface folding. The study was published in Science Advances and builds on established knowledge that gyrification — the formation of brain folds — allows a larger cortical surface area to fit within a limited skull volume.
