"How did a tiny grain of Moon dust become a magnetic time capsule from the Moon's ancient past?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at "01:30 IST" IST
Reading options

"Tiny magnetic grains of iron locked inside Moon dust brought back by China's Chang'e 6 mission may act like a microscopic fossil, preserving clues about the Moon's long-lost magnetic field that vanished billions of years ago."

How did a tiny grain of Moon dust become a magnetic time capsule?

Scientists have found a hidden magnetic fossil inside the Moon dust that China's Chang'e 6 mission brought back from the far side — and it might tell us what the Moon's magnetic field was like billions of years ago.

The Moon's missing shield

The Moon you see in our sky today is quiet and still. But billions of years ago, it was a much more active place. Scientists think the Moon once had its own magnetic field — like a giant invisible blanket wrapping around it, similar to Earth's magnetic shield. This field protected the early Moon and helped shape how it looked.

Then, slowly, the Moon's magnetic field faded away. No one knows exactly when or why. But traces of its old magnetism are still locked inside lunar rocks and soil, like messages from a forgotten past.

China's Chang'e 6 brings back Moon dust

In 2024, China's Chang'e 6 mission made history. It became the first spacecraft ever to collect samples from the Moon's far side — the side that always faces away from Earth. The lander scooped up nearly 2 kilograms of Moon dust and rocks, then rocketed them back to Earth.

Scientists have now studied that precious soil back on Earth. They used super-powerful microscopes and high-tech chemical tools to look inside individual dust grains. And what they found was surprising.

Tiny iron fossils inside impact glass

Buried inside the Chang'e-6 soil were ultra-tiny particles of metallic iron — so small they are measured in billionths of a meter. What makes them special is their crystal shape: they are a form called gamma-Fe (γ-Fe), which is normally only stable at very high temperatures.

These iron grains formed inside impact glass — glassy droplets created when meteorites slammed into the Moon's surface at incredible speed, melting the soil into glass on the spot. As the glass cooled down, the iron got trapped inside, frozen in place like an insect in amber.

A microscopic record of ancient magnetism

Here is the amazing part: these tiny γ-Fe particles can hold onto a magnetic memory. Just like a fridge magnet remembers which way is "north," these iron grains recorded the direction and strength of the Moon's ancient magnetic field when they formed.

Using special imaging techniques, the research team found that the particles keep a consistent magnetic signal — they respond the same way when exposed to an external magnetic field. That stability makes them natural "time capsules" that could help scientists reconstruct what the Moon's magnetic field was like in the distant past.

What this means for Moon mysteries

The Moon's old magnetic field is one of planetary science's biggest puzzles. Why did it turn off? When? How long did it last?

Every tiny grain of iron that holds a magnetic memory is like finding a new page in an old book. With more Chang'e-6 samples to study, scientists hope these magnetic fossils will help them piece together a timeline of the Moon's hidden ancient shield — and maybe, just maybe, learn why a world so similar to Earth in its early days ended up losing its magnetic field while Earth kept its own.

Try this at home

Make your own impact glass! Heat a piece of sugar or honey in a metal spoon over a candle (with adult help and safety gear). When it melts and bubbles, quickly dip a cold metal knife into the melt and pull it out. You will see tiny glassy droplets form — just like the impact glass scientists study on the Moon! Remember: never touch hot metal without protection, and always have an adult nearby.

Fun Fact

The Moon is the only celestial body where humans have brought back signed rocks — samples that were literally labeled with the spot they were collected. Chang'e 6's far-side samples came from the South Pole-Aitken Basin, one of the largest and oldest impact craters in the entire Solar System, dating back roughly 4 billion years.
