How Does a Beetle Hitch a Ride on a Bee? The Rare Insect Back in Wales After 60 Years

Imagine a beetle so shy that it vanished from an entire country for nearly 60 years — and then, when it finally came back, it was found hiding on just one small piece of a windy Welsh headland. That is the true story of the short-necked oil beetle, and this autumn it gave scientists some very good news.

The mystery of the missing beetle

The short-necked oil beetle (its scientific name is Meloe brevicollis) is a chunky, dark beetle that grows up to about 24 millimetres — roughly the length of a paperclip. It used to live along Britain's flower-rich coasts.

But after 1948, something strange happened: nobody in the UK saw one for almost 60 years. Its sandy, flowery seaside homes were disappearing, and the beetle slipped away with them.

It was finally found again — first in Devon in 2006, then on the Scottish island of Coll in 2009. Later, small groups turned up on more Scottish islands: Tiree, Islay, Barra and Uist. Slowly, the beetle was creeping back.

A beetle that catches a bus — made of bee

Here is the cleverest part. A young short-necked oil beetle cannot survive on its own. Its newly hatched babies, called larvae, climb onto flowers and wait. When a solitary bee lands to collect pollen, the tiny beetles grab on and hitch a ride back to the bee's nest. There, they feed on the pollen the bee has stored until they grow into adults.

So the beetle doesn't just need the right spot — it also needs the right bees nearby. That is why scientists had to look after several species at once.

Three patient years of searching

The search took place at Castlemartin Range in Pembrokeshire, the only place in Wales where this beetle is known to live. It is run by the Bumblebee Conservation Trust as part of a bigger project protecting the rare Shrill Carder Bee.

In 2022 and 2025, the team found only dead adults — worrying news. Then, in 2026, after three years of looking, they finally spotted larvae on a small patch of the site. The number and spread of the young beetles told them a bigger family had hatched nearby.

Anna Hobbs of the Bumblebee Conservation Trust called it "very exciting" to find "evidence of its breeding again," and thanked the volunteers for "persevering with us after two years without larval sightings."

Why one small beetle matters

The short-necked oil beetle is "nationally rare" in Great Britain and listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Its return is a sign that careful habitat work — keeping the right mix of bare earth, thick plants for shelter, and lots of flowers — is paying off. Protecting one rare insect means protecting the bees, plants and other creatures that share its home.

Try this at home

Be a pollinator detective! Sit quietly near a patch of flowers on a warm, sunny day and watch which bees visit. Count how many different kinds you can spot in ten minutes. Real scientists do exactly this — called a survey — and patient counting is how they found the beetle again.

Fun Fact

Oil beetles get their name from a secret weapon: when frightened, they squeeze out orange oily droplets from their leg joints. It is a warning that says "don't eat me!" — so leave them be if you ever meet one.

For grown-ups

"The short-necked oil beetle (Meloe brevicollis) was confirmed breeding at Castlemartin Range in Pembrokeshire — its only known site in Wales — after a three-year search reported by Natural Resources Wales and the Bumblebee Conservation Trust on 31 October 2026. Surveys in 2022 and 2025 found only dead adults, raising fears for the species; the 2026 survey found larvae (triungulins) on a small area of the range. The beetle is 'nationally rare' in Great Britain and listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. It disappeared from the UK for almost 60 years after 1948 due to habitat loss, then was rediscovered in Devon in 2006 and on the Isle of Coll, Scotland, in 2009. The work is part of the Shrill Carder Bee project, funded by The National Lottery Heritage Fund and part of Wales's flagship Natur am Byth species recovery programme. Framed for children as a nature-comeback story about teamwork between species and patient fieldwork, not as a habitat-loss alarm. Sources: BBC News (31 Oct 2026); Nation.Cymru (29 Oct 2026); Swansea Bay News; Western Telegraph; Wikipedia ('Meloe brevicollis', 'Meloe')."
