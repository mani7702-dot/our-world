Could You Walk All the Way Around the World? The Man Who Took 28 Years to Get Home

Imagine deciding, as a grown-up, to walk all the way home — and then actually doing it, step by step, across four continents and 28 years. That is the true story of Karl Bushby, a British adventurer who is finally nearing his front door.

One rule, one very long road

On 1 November 1998, Karl left Punta Arenas, Chile — near the southern tip of South America — and started walking north. He had two rules he promised never to break: he would not use any kind of vehicle to move forward, and he would not return to the UK until the whole journey was finished.

His plan? To walk an unbroken path from the bottom of South America all the way home to his childhood city of Hull, in England. He called it the Goliath Expedition. He set off with about $500 in his pocket and no sponsors at all, expecting it to take maybe 12 years.

It took more than double that — about 28 years, and roughly 36,000 miles (58,000 kilometres).

Jungle, ice and a frozen sea

Karl crossed South America, picked his way through the roadless Darién Gap jungle into Central America, and made his way through Mexico into the United States and Canada.

Then came his boldest step. In March 2006, Karl and a French adventurer, Dimitri Kieffer, walked across the frozen Bering Strait — the icy sea that separates Alaska from Russia. It took them 14 days to cross a stretch of ice only about 58 miles (93 km) wide, because they had to find a safe, winding route. He kept going through Siberia, Mongolia, China and Central Asia, and in 2024 he even swam across the Caspian Sea.

What keeps a person going?

The trip was delayed again and again — by visa rules, by a years-long ban from Russia, and by the worldwide pandemic that stopped travel for everyone. But Karl says a little confidence goes a long way. He also kept a promise to himself, and wrote a rule on paper so he could not cheat.

He has one more thing to look forward to: waiting at the end of the road are his son, his father, his brothers — and a 12-year-old granddaughter he has never met.

Try this at home

Grab a world map or a globe. Put your finger on Punta Arenas, Chile, and trace the route north: through the Darién Gap, up through Canada, across the Bering Strait to Russia, then west across Asia and Europe to England. How many countries did your finger cross? That is the same wild path Karl walked — and you can follow it in a single afternoon.

Fun Fact: Karl's "vehicle" was not a car or a bike — it was a heavy handcart he pushed and pulled himself, packed with camping gear and supplies. When it once got a flat tyre, he simply fixed it by the side of the road.

For grown-ups

"Karl Bushby (born 30 March 1969, Hull) set off on 1 November 1998 from Punta Arenas, Chile, at the southern tip of South America, on what he named the Goliath Expedition — an attempt to walk an unbroken path back to his home city of Kingston upon Hull, England, using no motorised transport to advance and never returning to the UK until the walk was complete. The journey covered roughly 36,000 miles (58,000 km) across four continents: South and Central America (including the roadless Darién Gap between Colombia and Panama), North America, the frozen Bering Strait into Russia, then Siberia, Mongolia, China, Central Asia and Europe. In March 2006 he and French adventurer Dimitri Kieffer crossed the 58-mile-wide Bering Strait on foot over 14 days. He began the journey with about $500 and no sponsors. Delays — visa refusals, a five-year Russian entry ban (2013), the Covid pandemic, and funding troubles — stretched the trip from an expected 12 years to about 28. In 2024 he swam the Caspian Sea; in October 2026 he swam the English Channel, reaching UK soil. He is due to arrive at his childhood home in Hull on 2 November 2026. Framed for children as a geography-and-perseverance story about maps, continents, borders and never giving up, not as a tale of danger. Sources: BBC News (31 Oct 2026, by Kevin Shoesmith); Wikipedia ('Karl Bushby'); plus wide outlet coverage (The New York Times, The Guardian, AP News, The Independent, NPR)."
