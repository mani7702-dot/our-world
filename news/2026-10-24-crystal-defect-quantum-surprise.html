How a Tiny Imperfection in a Crystal Revealed a Big Quantum Mystery

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 06:22 IST IST
Reading options

Scientists figured out how a small defect inside a diamond or silicon chip changes its energy when light hits it — a discovery that could lead to better quantum sensors and computers.

1. How a Tiny Imperfection in a Crystal Revealed a Big Quantum Mystery

A speck that thinks like a computer

Deep inside a diamond — the same kind that sparkles in jewelry — there are invisible tiny flaws. These are not mistakes to be ashamed of. In fact, they might be the key to a future where computers are thousands of times more powerful than today's machines.

Each flaw is called a spin defect. It forms when an atom is missing from the crystal, or when a different kind of atom sneaks in. Even though it is just one atom out of billions, this tiny imperfection can hold and process information using the strange rules of quantum mechanics. That makes it a possible building block for quantum computers and ultra-precise quantum sensors.

The light puzzle inside crystals

Here is the problem scientists have been stuck on: when you shine a flashlight on a spin defect, the defect gets excited — its electrons jump to a higher energy level. But the defect soon calms back down. It can do this three ways: spit out a photon, flip its spin, or shake its neighbors by transferring energy into vibrations.

That last option — called internal conversion — is the trickiest to calculate. It requires tracking how excited electrons dance with hundreds or thousands of vibrating atoms at the same time. Even powerful supercomputers struggled to solve it accurately.

A faster way to see inside the quantum world

A team at the University of Chicago found a shortcut. Instead of checking every possible vibration one by one, they used many-body wavefunctions — mathematical tools that describe how all particles interact at once. By combining this with GPU-accelerated simulations, they built a program called WEST that can simulate the entire process much faster.

When they tested their method on nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond, their calculations matched experiments perfectly. In silicon carbide — a crystal used in high-power electronics — they made a surprise discovery: internal conversion was far more important than scientists had assumed.

Try this at home

Take a clear ice cube tray and fill one compartment with water mixed with a drop of food coloring. Freeze it overnight — the coloring is your "imperfection." Shine a flashlight through the ice from different angles. The colored ice scatters light, just like the signals scientists read from real spin defects!

Fun Fact

A single grain of sand contains more atoms than there are grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches — but one missing atom is enough to create a spin defect that could one day power a quantum computer!

For the grown-ups

A team at the University of Chicago Pritzker School of Molecular Engineering developed a new computational method that simulates the full optical cycle of spin defects in crystals like diamond and silicon carbide. Spin defects can act as stable qubits for quantum computers and sensors. When light excites a defect, it returns to its original state by releasing a photon, flipping its spin, or transferring energy into vibrations (internal conversion) — the last of which was extremely difficult to model accurately. Led by Giulia Galli with Stefano Villani and Yu Jin, the team used many-body wavefunctions and GPU-accelerated simulations in the WEST program. Published in Physical Review Letters, their method was tested on nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond and divacancy defects in silicon carbide, revealing that internal conversion has a much greater influence in silicon carbide than previously thought.
