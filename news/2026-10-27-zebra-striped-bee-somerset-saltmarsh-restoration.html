1. A bee with a tuxedo coat in an unexpected place

Imagine seeing a bee that looks like it's wearing a zebra-print jacket — black and yellow stripes running down its fuzzy body. That is the sea aster mining bee, and it usually sticks to the southern and eastern coasts of England. But this October, a naturalist named Nigel Phillips made a surprising discovery: these tiny, elegantly striped bees had arrived at Steart Marshes in Somerset — hundreds of miles outside their known range.

Phillips called it "a huge surprise." And not just because the bees showed up in the wrong neighborhood. The real surprise is why they came here.

2. How saltmarshes become a bee highway

The sea aster mining bee is not just pretty to look at — it is also picky about where it lives. Unlike garden bumblebees that can nest almost anywhere, this little bee depends on two things:

  • Bare, sandy soil for nesting (they dig tiny burrows in the ground, like mining engineers)
  • Sea aster flowers (a pink-and-yellow coastal plant) for food

At Steart Marshes, both ingredients came together thanks to a bold restoration project. Starting in 2014, conservationists tore down old farmland fences and let seawater flow back across the fields. The result? A thriving coastal wetland where saltmarsh grasses, sea aster, and other coastal plants flourished.

When the bees arrived, they found exactly what they needed: warm, sandy soil for nests and plenty of sea aster in bloom. "There were lots of them and several colonial nest areas in the dry sandy ground," Phillips observed.

3. Tiny tenants of the saltmarsh

The sea aster mining bee is what scientists call a solitary bee — meaning females don't live in hives or make honey. Instead, each female digs her own burrow, fills it with a mixture of nectar and pollen, and lays an egg inside. When the egg hatches, the larva has its own private larder waiting.

These bees are also generalists with a specialist diet. They visit many flowers, but they are especially fond of sea aster, a hardy plant that thrives in salty coastal soil. The bee's scientific name, Colletes halophilus, literally means "salt-loving honeycomb bee" — a perfect match for its coastal home.

At Steart Marshes, the site manager Bay Alys Laver said: "People usually associate saltmarshes with huge flocks of wading birds, geese and ducks, but there is so much life here that goes unnoticed."

4. Try this at home

  • Build a bee hotel: Drill small holes in a block of untreated wood or bamboo tubes, and place it in a sunny spot. See if solitary bees move in!
  • Plant a pollinator garden: Scatter wildflower seeds (especially coastal plants like sea thrift or thrift (Armeria maritima)) to attract native bees and butterflies.
  • Make a saltmarsh in a jar: Fill a jar with sand, small pebbles, and a little saltwater. Sprinkle in some wildflower seeds and watch what grows — a mini version of the coastal ecosystem!

5. Fun Fact

Of the 290 invertebrate species found in UK saltmarshes, 148 can only be found in these coastal habitats — that is more than half of them! Saltmarshes are like hidden biodiversity hotspots, packed with rare creatures that exist nowhere else on Earth.

6. For grown-ups

The BBC News article (Oct 27, 2026) by Isabella Beard reports that the sea aster mining bee (Colletes halophilus) was spotted at Steart Marshes in Somerset — a significant range extension from its known southeast England and north-west European coastal distribution. The bee's arrival is directly linked to the saltmarsh restoration project at Steart Marshes, completed in 2014, which transformed former farmland by allowing seawater to flow back and create the coastal wetland habitat that supports the bee's nesting needs (bare sandy soil) and food source (sea aster, Aster tripolium).

Wikipedia's Colletes halophilus article independently confirms: (1) the species is strongly associated with saltmarshes and coastal habitats in southeastern England and northwestern Europe; (2) it relies on sea aster as a food plant and nests in bare sandy soils at the inland edges of saltmarshes; (3) the species is threatened by rising sea levels and human development; and (4) females are larger and brighter than males, with adults 11–14 mm long, featuring a black abdomen with yellowish bands — matching the "zebra-striped" description in the BBC report.

Wikipedia's Andrena article confirms broader mining bee biology: ground-nesting, solitary behavior, females provisioning underground brood cells with nectar and pollen masses, and preference for sandy soils — consistent with the behavioral observations reported by the BBC.
