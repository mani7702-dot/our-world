Why Do Nightjars Give Up Speed for Slow-Motion Hunt?

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 23:45 IST
Reading options

The European nightjar flies thousands of miles each year, yet scientists discovered its wings are built for slow, ninja-like hunting — not for racing through the sky. The tradeoff is written right into how each wingbeat pushes and lifts.

Imagine a bird that flies thousands of miles each year, gliding from Europe down to southern Africa and back again — every single year. You might expect its wings to be perfect, racing-machine wings, right? But scientists just discovered something surprising about the European nightjar: its wings are built more like a ninja's than a sprinter's.

Slow is fast when you're hunting

The European nightjar is a twilight hunter. As dusk falls, it leaves its daytime roost and swoops low over fields, snapping up moths and beetles right out of the air. To catch dinner like that, it needs to fly slowly, stop on a dime, and twist its body in sharp turns.

To study how this works, researchers put nightjars in a wind tunnel and filmed their wingbeats with special cameras. As the birds flew at different speeds, the team traced the invisible swirls of air left behind each wing — the "wake" that reveals exactly how the wings are pushing and lifting.

The tradeoff in every wingbeat

Here is the surprising part. At slow speeds, the nightjar's broad, finger-wide wingtips work beautifully. They let the bird hover a little, twist sharply, and stay in the air while hunting. But when the bird tried to cruise faster — the kind of flying it needs for its long migration — those same wide tips became a problem.

The broad tips created a "double tip vortex" — two swirling whirlpools at each wingtip — that made the air messy and raised the energy cost of lifting the bird's body. In other words, every wingbeat at higher speed was harder work than a typical migrant bird would need.

"It runs counter to the picture of a long-distance migrant as a perfectly optimised flying machine," says biologist Christoffer Johansson at Lund University. "The nightjar's wings look like a careful compromise between two very different jobs."

An upstroke that pushes

The researchers found one more odd trick. Most birds fold their wings close to their bodies on the way up, so the "upstroke" is nearly passive — just letting the wing return. But nightjars can't fold their wings very much, so even on the upstroke they keep pushing air backward, generating thrust.

That active upstroke was thought to belong mostly to bats and hummingbirds, not birds. Finding it in nightjars shows that evolution can arrive at the same clever solution from very different starting points.

Why it matters

This is a small, beautiful lesson about how nature builds bodies. The nightjar does not have wings that are perfect at one thing — they are good at two very different things at once: crossing oceans and catching dinner in the dark. That balance — not pure speed, not pure efficiency, but flexibility — is what evolution often delivers.

Try this at home: flap a pair of kitchen tongs slowly and then fast. At the slow end you can turn and twist easily; at the fast end the loose ends wobble and waste energy — just like the nightjar's broad wings.

Fun Fact

The nightjar's wings stay flexible thanks to a special joint that lets the primary feathers spread wide on the upstroke, turning the whole wingtip into an air-sensing "hand" that feels the air currents while hunting in the dark.

For grown-ups

"The European nightjar (Caprimulgus europaeus) migrates annually between Europe and southern Africa. A wind-tunnel study at Lund University (Johansson LC, Norevik G, Friman S, Hedenström A, PLoS Biol 24(9): e3004023) found broad, slotted wingtips create a 'double tip vortex' that lowers cruise efficiency but aids slow-hovering insect hunting. The bird also generates thrust on the upstroke (usually a bat/hummingbird trait) because it cannot fold its wings much. These results show how evolution balances competing aerodynamic demands when one body part serves multiple costly functions."
