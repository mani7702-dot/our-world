10 surprising true facts from history that sound totally made up

History is stranger than fiction. While the past was really happening, people were inventing things we now think are modern — secret codes, tiny teeth art, and "shows" where babies were the main attraction. Here are ten real things that actually happened, and sound completely made up.

1. Viking warriors filed their front teeth into giggling lines

Some Viking Age warriors — mostly men found in Sweden and Denmark — filed dark horizontal lines across their two front teeth. Archaeologists have found skeletons with exactly these tiny grooves. Nobody knows why for sure: maybe it showed which group you belonged to, showed your rank, or was just decoration. The lines may even have held colored powder so smiles really stood out. Ouch — and no dentist.

Fun fact: The movie The Northman gave its Valkyrie the same black tooth marks. The director picked black because historians still argue what color it was. The idea of tooth-art? Totally real.

2. One woman wrote a five-million-word diary in invisible code

Anne Lister lived in England from 1788 to 1840 (yes, two hundred years ago). She filled 26 big notebooks with about five million words — and roughly one sixth of it was written in her own secret code. She mixed Greek letters with zodiac signs, math symbols, and punctuation to hide private thoughts about her life and relationships. The diaries were so well hidden that people forgot they existed for a long time.

Try this at home: Try writing your name in a code of your own — draw a symbol for each letter. Anne would have loved swapping codes with a friend.

3. 'Amusement park babies' were a real ticketed show

In the early 1900s, a showman named Martin Couney built a glass "Infantorium" filled with tiny babies in warm water tanks. Families could pay to watch the babies, and the money paid for their care. It sounds like a movie plot, but Couney's exhibit ran for decades — first in Coney Island, then at world's fairs. His strange idea helped teach doctors how to care for the tiniest, sickest babies when most hospitals would not even try.

4. Sheep were washed in pee and it was national business

In medieval Scotland and England, wool was so valuable that washing a fleece was a legal duty. The catch? They often used urine to clean the wool, because urine helps remove grease. There was even a special crime for "stealing a handful of wool or mending a leak in a ship's woolHold" — punishable by death. Wool was that serious.

For grownups: Yep, the death penalty for touching someone else's wool. History was intense.

5. Scottish women saved songs for doing the washing

Before music was recorded, Scottish women did a job called waulking — pounding wet wool on a table to shrink it and make it warm and thick. To keep a steady rhythm, they sang waulking songs. The songs could go on as long as the work needed, and the verses built up over time. If someone repeated a verse, it was considered bad luck.

6. A baby was once displayed next to a Ferris wheel for science

At the 1893 Chicago World's Fair, an exhibit let parents see their 'preemie' babies in an incubator — and charge admission. The goal was to show that modern medicine could save the smallest babies. It was part fair spectacle, part science lesson, and part fundraiser that paid for more care. People came, babies got help, and the idea of intensive care for tiny babies was born.

7. Teeth were traded like coins in ancient Mexico

The Maya and later the Aztecs filed their teeth to points and filled the gaps with precious stones. But here is the wild part: archaeologists have found teeth that were pulled from one person's mouth and carefully stored — some even look like they were kept as jewelry. People collected and reused real teeth like we collect coins.

8. A Roman emperor 'married' a sacred stone (and made everyone attend)

In the 3rd century AD, Roman Emperor Elagabalus (say it fast: "el-uh-GAB-ul-us") shocked the empire by insisting his sacred stone statue get married first — then declaring the ceremony binding for everyone else. He drove chariots, wore makeup, and threw lavish parties. The Senate was horrified and wrote it all down. History remembers him as one of Rome's most unusual rulers.

9. Medieval kids were baptized with a whole roasted pig

In parts of medieval Europe, families did not christen babies with just water. Some poured the water over the head of a whole roasted pig. Why? They believed the smell scared away evil spirits, and the pig had to be roasted because... well, no one is quite sure. It was weird either way.

10. Victorian mourners kept their dead loved ones' jewelry — and hair

When someone died, Victorian families often turned a lock of the person's hair into a ring or brooch. These "mourning pieces" were worn every day. Some even wove in curls from several family members. They were expensive, delicate, and meant to keep memories close — like wearing a hug.

Try this at home: Ask a grownup about an older family tradition. You might hear something that sounds too strange to be true.
