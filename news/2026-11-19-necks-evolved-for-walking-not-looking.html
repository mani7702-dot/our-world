Why do animals have necks? The answer surprised scientists

By Our World Published 19 November 2026 at 03:51 IST
Reading options

"A new study of a fish called the sturgeon suggests the first animals on land had stiff necks - and that necks may have evolved for walking, not for looking around."

Watch a dog turn its head when you call its name. Watch a bird swivel to look at a noise in the garden. Their necks make that easy. So it feels obvious that necks first appeared so animals could look around.

A new study says that for the very first animals on land, it was not like that at all. Their necks were stiff. Really stiff.

The first walkers

About 380 million years ago, some fish-like animals began hauling themselves out of the water. Their fins slowly became legs, and their bodies changed to hold themselves up without water to float in.

One famous fossil from that time is Tiktaalik, a fish with arm-like fins. In these animals the head slowly moved away from the shoulders, and a neck appeared. Scientists long assumed that change let them turn their heads to look around on land.

A fish with the old backbone

Bones turn into fossils. Soft parts usually do not. Between the bones of those early animals ran a jelly-filled rod called a notochord, and it almost never survives.

So the team looked for a living animal built the same way. The sturgeon, an ancient fish sometimes called a living fossil, still keeps a notochord like that into adulthood.

The researchers tested how stiff a sturgeon backbone really is, then fed those numbers into computer models of the fossil animals. It is like using a modern bicycle to guess how an old penny-farthing handled.

The surprise

The sturgeon backbone did not feel very stiff. But the model told a different story. Most of the early land animals could bend their necks less than 6 degrees to the side. They could lift their heads less than 3 degrees. They could not twist them at all.

Here is the part that surprised the scientists most. When the head and shoulders moved apart, the neck did not become more bendy. The change happened at the same time, but it did not buy them any extra head movement.

So necks did not first appear for looking around. They appeared alongside another set of changes: the front limbs turning into something that could push the body up and forward.

Walking came first. Looking around came later.

Try this at home

Your neck is a stack of small bones, and you can turn it a long way. Feel it: slowly turn your head left and right, then nod up and down.

Now compare two straws. A bendy straw folds at its ribbed joint. A plain straw barely bends at all. The backbone of an early land animal was closer to the plain straw, with a soft jelly core. Our bendy-straw necks came much later. Ask before raiding the straws.

One more thing

The team points out that their model only looks at the backbone. Muscles, skin and bony plates around it would have made those necks even stiffer. The real animals were probably less flexible than the numbers say.

For grown-ups

A biomechanics study in Nature (14 Oct 2026) measured the material stiffness of the lake sturgeon notochord and applied it to fossil backbone models of stem tetrapods such as Tiktaalik. The model estimates under 6 degrees of lateral head movement, under 3 degrees dorsally, and no head rotation - and mobility did not rise when head and shoulders separated. The authors conclude necks did not evolve for looking around; the separation came with changes that let the front limbs support and move the body. Limitation is backbone-only.
