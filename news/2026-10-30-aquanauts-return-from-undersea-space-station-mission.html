How a 5-Day Underwater 'Space Station' Mission Helps Astronauts Prepare for Real Space

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 22:45 IST
Reading options

"For five days, a team of 'aquanauts' lived in an underwater laboratory 22 feet below the surface — a practice ground for the challenges of real space travel."

Why Do Astronauts Practice Living Underwater?

Imagine spending five days living inside a tiny room at the bottom of the ocean. No quick trip home, no walking outside without a special suit, no easy phone call to a friend — just you, a few crewmates, and the hum of life-support machines keeping you alive.

This isn't submarine duty. It's an astronaut training mission — and it might be one of the closest things we have to living on another world without actually leaving Earth.

Dr. Michaela Musilova and her five-person crew did exactly this in October 2026. They lived inside the Jules' Undersea Lab, bolted to the seafloor 22 feet (7 meters) underwater off Key Largo, Florida. For five days, they couldn't just surface for lunch. They were aquanauts — saturation divers whose bodies had adjusted to life under pressure.

The Ocean Is a Great Practice Room for Space

Living underwater shares surprising similarities with living in space:

  • You're stuck with your crewmates. Just like astronauts on the ISS, you can't just walk away from a problem. Everyone has to work together.
  • You need special suits to go outside. Underwater, that's scuba gear. In space, it's a spacesuit.
  • A mistake can be deadly. A leak, a power failure, or bad air can be fatal in both environments.
  • You feel far from home. Even though Earth is just above, you might as well be on another planet.

During their mission, the crew tested real space challenges: How does living under pressure affect thinking speed and reaction time? Can plants like kale and microgreens grow in a sealed environment? How do you fix equipment when you can't just call a repair shop?

A Mission With a Message

The crew didn't just run experiments — they connected with real people. From their underwater lab, they spoke to a group of teenage space enthusiasts running a simulated space station in Slovakia. Over the water, they shared what they were learning about teamwork, problem-solving, and staying calm when things go wrong.

When their mission ended, Musilova described the feeling of finally breathing normal air again and surfacing — a simple act that felt like coming home.

Try This at Home

Build a mini space station. Take a large clear container (like a fish tank or large jar). Seal it with tape. Put a small plant, a flashlight (your sun), and a tiny toy figure inside. Now imagine you're the commander: How will your astronaut get air, water, and food? What happens if the "hull" gets a small crack?

Real astronauts and aquanauts both have to plan for emergencies where help is hours or days away. The difference is location — one is in space, the other is underwater. But the skill is the same: thinking ahead, staying calm, and trusting your team.

Fun Fact

NASA has been running underwater astronaut missions called NEEMO (NASA Extreme Environment Mission Operations) since the year 2000. Dozens of astronauts have lived in underwater labs for weeks at a time — practicing exactly the kind of teamwork and problem-solving needed for missions to the Moon, Mars, and beyond.

For grown-ups

"This explainer follows Dr. Michaela Musilova's SEA STAR mission — a 5-day saturation dive in Jules' Undersea Lab off Key Largo, Florida (22 ft / 7 m underwater). All five crew members became aquanauts: saturation divers breathing a helium-oxygen mix at ambient pressure so their bodies reach equilibrium with the surrounding water, allowing extended stays without daily decompression. Wikipedia confirms saturation diving is a technique where divers live under pressure for days to weeks, surfacing once at the end; NASA has run dozens of NEEMO (NASA Extreme Environment Mission Operations) analog missions since 2000 to study human factors in isolated, high-risk environments. The SEA STAR mission tested cognitive performance under pressure, microgreens growth (lettuce/kale) at 1 atm pressure differential, neutral-buoyancy 'spacewalks' using scuba gear, and student-built environmental sensors. Musilova is an astrobiologist and analog-astronaut veteran who has led multiple NEEMO-style missions. The undersea lab environment mirrors key ISS challenges: confinement, equipment failure, team dynamics, limited communication delays, and the psychological weight of being unable to simply 'go home' — all valuable rehearsal for real spaceflight. Sources: Space.com (Michaela Musilova, Oct 30 2026); Wikipedia Satellite articles: 'Saturation diving', 'Underwater exploration', 'NASA_NEEMO'."
