How can a dying coral come back just by touching a healthy one? A touch for 5 days is enough

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 14:30 IST
Reading options

"Bleached corals — ghostly white and close to dying — can fully recover in just seven weeks after touching a healthy, genetically identical coral for as little as five days. Stanford scientists found that brief contact is enough to pass beneficial algae back into the damaged coral and restart its recovery."

How can a dying coral come back just by touching a healthy one? A touch for 5 days is enough

Imagine a coral that looks dead — pale as a ghost, hardly clinging on after a marine heat wave. Now imagine it fully bouncing back, not in months, but in seven weeks, after touching a healthy neighbor for no more than a single workweek. That is exactly what Stanford scientists have just shown.

The problem: corals bleach when oceans warm

Corals are animals, not plants — but they borrow color and most of their food from tiny green algae that live inside their bodies. When the sea gets too warm, corals kick those algae out. The coral turns stark white (that is the "bleaching"), loses its main energy source, and slowly starves, becoming open to disease and harmful algae. Some reefs bounce back in months or years; many never fully recover.

The surprise: five days of contact is enough

For the first time, researchers showed that severely bleached corals can make a complete comeback after touching a healthy, genetically identical coral — and they only need about five days of contact to get the recovery started.

In the lab, the team placed 10 pairs of coral fragments side by side. In each pair one fragment was healthy and the other was bleached and near death. Where the fragments touched, the white coral began to color in again. After seven weeks every touched coral had recovered. The fragments that were kept a few millimeters apart? They died.

How the touch works

Because the fragments came from the same parent colony, they are genetic clones. Like identical twins, they recognize each other and fuse at the contact point, opening tiny channels between their tissues. Through those channels the healthy algae — and the energy-rich molecules they make — flow into the bleached partner. Inside the rescued coral those algae then start dividing furiously, roughly doubling every four days, which is about ten times faster than they grow in the wild. That burst of new algae gives the coral back its color and its energy supply, fast.

Why reefs could use this

Today, a bleached coral is often written off as a lost cause. These findings suggest a hopeful new path: if scientists can get beneficial algae back into damaged corals quickly enough, reefs might survive the increasingly frequent marine heat waves driven by climate change.

For now the method only works in the lab, because each bleached coral needs a nearby healthy clone. The Stanford team's next goal is to deliver the helpful algae directly, so a whole reef could recover after a heat wave — not just paired fragments in an aquarium.

Try this at home

You can't keep corals at home, but you can see the same "recovery by contact" idea in a jar of pond water: fill two clear cups with the same pond water, put a healthy, green blade of grass in one for a day, then move it to the second cup — both cups of water green up as algae spread. It is a tiny demonstration of how something good can pass from a healthy source to something struggling, just by being close.

Fun fact

Coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor, yet an estimated 25% of all marine life depends on reefs for food, shelter, or breeding — including thousands of fish that end up on our plates.

For grown-ups

Corals are animals that live in a partnership with tiny algae (Symbiodiniaceae) living inside their tissues; the algae give corals most of their energy and color. When ocean water gets too warm, corals expel the algae and "bleach" — turning stark white and starving. Lead author Danny Collins (Stanford Doerr School of Sustainability, PhD student) and senior author Fiorenza Micheli tested 10 pairs of coral fragments from the same clone, one healthy and one bleached. Fragments that physically touched for as little as five days recovered completely in seven weeks; fragments kept a few millimeters apart died. Using microscopy the team saw that genetically identical fragments fuse their tissues at contact points, forming channels that let healthy algae (and the nutrients they produce) pass into the bleached partner; the algae then divide ~2x every 4 days — about 10x faster than in wild corals. Published in Science Advances on October 14, 2026. The technique works in the lab, not yet on reefs: every bleached coral needs a healthy genetic twin nearby, which is impractical across a whole reef. The team is now testing whether beneficial algae can be delivered directly to damaged corals after bleaching, so reefs could recover after marine heat waves without requiring paired clones.
