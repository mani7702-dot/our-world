Why is a dead star flashing radio waves every 8 minutes?

By Our World Published 16 November 2026 at "09:20" IST
Reading options

"Astronomers have traced a mysterious radio pulse that repeats every eight minutes to a dead star spinning in orbit around a star much like our own Sun - the first system of its kind ever found."

Picture a lighthouse the size of a small planet, spinning all the way around once every eight minutes. Now put it 5,000 light-years away and wrap it in a beam of radio waves instead of light. Every time the beam sweeps past Earth, our telescopes catch a blip.

That is close to what astronomers have just found: a dead star, spinning fast, flashing radio waves at us on a steady eight-minute beat.

The best part? It has a neighbour - a star almost the same as our Sun.

1. What the telescopes caught

The signal has a name: GPM J071507-114153. That is a lot of numbers, so think of it as a radio address.

Astronomers first spotted it in December 2024 using the Murchison Widefield Array, a set of radio antennas in the outback of Western Australia. The array sweeps across the Milky Way, listening for faint radio crackle from space.

Then they kept listening. Between December 2024 and September 2025 they recorded 214 separate pulses from the same spot in the sky, each one about eight minutes after the last.

That pattern - on for a few hours, silent for about a day, then on again - puts it in a group astronomers call long-period transients, or LPTs. The first one was found in 2021. Only about a dozen are known.

2. Two stars, not one

Radio telescopes are good at hearing a signal, but not always at saying exactly where it comes from. So the team used the MeerKAT telescope in South Africa to pin the source down to a tiny patch of sky with just one faint star in it.

Then came the surprise. Using the giant Keck telescope in Hawaii, they split that star's light into a spectrum - a fingerprint that reveals what the star is made of and how hot it is.

It was not the small, cool red dwarf that astronomers expect in these systems. It was a G-type star, the same family as the Sun - almost the same mass, and almost the same surface temperature.

Its partner is the invisible one. The pulses wobble slightly in timing as the pair swing around each other, and the visible star wobbles too. Together those measurements show the two orbit every 32 hours or so, and that the hidden object weighs about two-thirds as much as the Sun.

That is too light to be a neutron star. The answer that fits is a white dwarf: the hot, crushed core left behind after a Sun-like star runs out of fuel, squeezed down to about the size of Earth but still with more mass than the Sun.

3. A storm on a distant star

Here is the bonus nobody ordered. Because the radio beam from the white dwarf has to travel through the space around its companion, it acts like a torch shining through smoke. When the beam passed behind a big loop of hot gas above the companion's surface - most likely a stellar prominence - the signal changed.

The team also picked up flares from the Sun-like star, the same kind of magnetic storms that cause auroras on Earth.

"It's amazing that by using radio wavelengths we can detect the storms on a star over 5,000 light-years away," said co-author Natasha Hurley-Walker.

4. What we still do not know

Nobody has seen this white dwarf directly. Everything above comes from timing and light - careful, patient measurement, not a photograph.

Astronomers also still argue about what powers LPTs in general. Is the beam coming from the white dwarf itself, or from a patch of space just above it? This system is the first where the pulses are clean enough to show the dead star moving along its orbit, which is a big step toward an answer.

And no - it is not aliens. It is a star, doing star things, extremely loudly.

Try this at home: build a lighthouse star

You can model the whole discovery with a torch and a friend.

  1. Stand still and sweep a torch across your friend's face once every few seconds, turning your whole body as you go.
  2. Have your friend walk backwards, a few steps at a time. Ask: when does the flash get harder to see?
  3. Now try sweeping the beam past a curtain or a plant between you. Notice how the flash changes when something gets in the way - that is exactly how the scientists spotted the gas loop around the far star.

For grown-ups

"McSweeney et al., 'A long-period transient with a Sun-like companion', Nature Astronomy, published 12 November 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41550-026-03016-z. The source GPM J071507-114153 was detected in an MWA survey of the southern Milky Way starting 4 December 2024; 214 pulses were recorded through September 2025 with a period of about 7.9 minutes, and the system appeared as roughly 6-hour on-phases separated by about 26-hour off-phases. MeerKAT localised the source to a single faint star, and Keck spectroscopy identified that star as a G-type main-sequence star of almost the same mass and temperature as the Sun - not the red dwarf usually seen in long-period transient (LPT) systems. Pulse arrival-time variations plus the companion's radial-velocity wobble give an orbital period of about 32 hours and a companion mass near two-thirds of a solar mass, too low for a neutron star, so the pulsing object is inferred to be a rapidly rotating, highly magnetic white dwarf. This is the first LPT in which the pulses clearly track the compact object's orbital motion, and the first white dwarf pulsar found orbiting a Sun-like star. The team also detected radio emission crossing a magnetic structure - most likely a stellar prominence - around the companion, and recorded flares from it. Around a dozen LPTs are known since the class was identified in 2021. Roughly 100 hours of data were processed at the Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre. This is indirect evidence from timing and spectroscopy, not a direct image of the white dwarf."
