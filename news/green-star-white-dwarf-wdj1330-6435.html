Why Is That Star Green? Astronomers Find the First Genuinely Green Star

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 22:20 IST
Reading options

Most stars are red, white, or blue, but a new study found a dying star that would actually look green to human eyes — and explained why some white dwarfs break the rule.

If someone told you they saw a green star, you might think they were mixing up planets and stars. But now astronomers really have found a star that is genuinely green — and it took a surprise discovery about dead stars to explain how.

The color rule for stars

Stars are cosmic light bulbs: their color comes mostly from how hot they are. Cooler stars glow orange-red, the Sun looks white, and the hottest stars blaze pale blue — just like an iron bar that glows red, then orange, then white as it heats up. There is even a name for a "perfect" radiator: a blackbody, and most stars follow the rule closely.

White dwarf stars — the dense, Earth-sized leftovers left behind when Sun-like stars run out of fuel — were thought to obey the same rule. They got their name because the earliest examples found were hot and shone white or pale blue.

The green exception

Now a team led by Dr Mark Hollands at the University of Warwick studied thousands of white dwarfs and found something odd. One star in particular, called WDJ1330+6435, would actually appear green to the human eye — the first star ever found that does. Some other white dwarfs turned out to glow pink, purple, yellow, or cyan instead of the expected orange, white, or blue.

The twist is density. The atmospheres of the coolest white dwarfs are incredibly packed — roughly the density of water and millions of times denser than the Sun's surface. Under those extreme conditions, even tiny amounts of elements like carbon, calcium, and sodium interact with the surrounding helium plasma and absorb light across a much wider range of colors. That shifts the star's color away from the standard rainbow.

How they saw the color

No white dwarf is close enough to see its color directly with our eyes. So the astronomers used starlight that telescopes already collect every day: each star's spectrum, the rainbow-like fingerprint made when its light is split apart by wavelength. By running those spectra through a model of human color vision, they calculated exactly what color each star would look like to someone watching it through a telescope.

Why it matters

The finding is a reminder that a star's spectrum can tell us not just its temperature and chemistry, but its true visual color too. Carbon-rich white dwarfs tend to look pink or purple, while those loaded with dust from shattered asteroids and comets lean yellow or cyan. And the green one? It had swallowed rocky planetary debris that dumped sodium into its atmosphere, and the dense gas filtered out just the right wavelengths to leave a ghostly green glow.

None of this fixes the real problem for reefs or planets — these stars are already dead, just glowing embers. But it opens a new way to read the night sky: color, for once, is telling us more than we expected.

Try this at home: on a clear night, find the brightest "star" that looks reddish and the brightest one that looks bluish. Those are red supergiants and hot blue stars — but a real green star is hiding in the data, not in the eyepiece.

For the grown-ups

Lead author Dr Mark Hollands (Warwick Prize Fellow, Astronomy & Astrophysics Group, University of Warwick) and colleagues analyzed spectra of white dwarf stars using a model of human color vision to recover their perceived colors. The green appearance of WDJ1330+6435 arises from sodium absorption in its ultra-dense (∼1 g/cm³) helium-dominated atmosphere, which removes much of the yellow-red light and leaves a green hue. The method is computational (the star is 275 light-years away and far too faint to resolve by eye); the team verified the same color-prediction pipeline reproduces known photometric colors for stars observed by Hubble. Full results appear in a peer-reviewed study referenced by the University of Warwick press release and ScienceDaily.
