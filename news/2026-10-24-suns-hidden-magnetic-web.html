What hidden magnetic web did a balloon telescope find above the Sun?

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 14:32 IST
Reading options

A balloon-borne telescope called Sunrise-III floated 35 km above Earth in 2024 and spotted delicate, thread-like magnetic structures woven through the Sun's "quiet" regions — a hidden web that may help explain how the Sun's atmosphere stays hot.

What hidden magnetic web did a balloon telescope find above the Sun?

Most of the time, the Sun looks like a calm, bright disk. But appearances can be deceiving — especially when you look at the Sun's invisible magnetic fields.

A telescope that floats above the clouds

On Earth, the atmosphere blurs fine details like heat rising off asphalt. To see the Sun clearly, a team sent a powerful telescope above most of the atmosphere — riding on a giant balloon.

The observatory is called Sunrise-III. In July 2024, it floated about 35 kilometers (22 miles) up in the stratosphere, carrying a 1-meter telescope and a special instrument named SCIP. From this calm, high perch, Sunrise-III could take incredibly sharp pictures of the Sun's magnetic fields.

The Sun's "quiet" regions are secretly busy

The Sun's surface is dotted with sunspots — those are easy to spot. But most of the Sun is covered in "quiet regions" that look empty. Don't be fooled: Sunrise-III found that these quiet regions contain a hidden world of magnetism.

Instead of one smooth magnetic field, the telescope revealed a network of thin, thread-like magnetic structures woven through a layer of the Sun's atmosphere called the chromosphere. Imagine invisible spider silk stretching across the sky — except it's made of magnetism, not silk.

Computer simulations showed that twisting motions on the Sun's surface create these threads, like wind twisting a ribbon in midair. The threads are embedded in a broader magnetic "canopy" that arches up from the solar surface.

Inside the solar jets

Sunrise-III also studied another quiet-Sun feature: spicules — narrow jets of gas that shoot upward from the Sun's surface like tiny solar geysers. By mapping the magnetic fields inside these jets, scientists learned how the fields change as they rise higher above the Sun.

Why does this matter?

The Sun's atmosphere (the corona) is millions of degrees hot — far hotter than the surface below. Yet the Sun's surface is "only" about 5,500 °C. This puzzle has stumped scientists for decades.

The new magnetic threads may be part of the answer. By showing how magnetic energy flows upward through the solar atmosphere and releases its energy, Sunrise-III is helping scientists understand how the Sun heats its own atmosphere — and how that energy eventually reaches Earth as space weather.

Try this at home

You can see magnetism in action without a balloon telescope. Take a bar magnet and sprinkle iron filings (or use a magnetic drawing board) on a piece of paper. Move the magnet underneath — watch how the filings line up into curves and loops that map the invisible magnetic field. Now imagine that, but on a star 150 million kilometers away!

Fun Fact

The word "magnet" comes from the Greek island of Magnesia, where people once found naturally magnetic stones. And the Sun is basically a giant ball of electrically charged gas — so its whole atmosphere is shaped by the same force that makes your fridge magnet stick to the door.

for-grownups

Sunrise-III is a 1-meter balloon-borne solar observatory that flew in Earth's stratosphere (~35 km altitude) in July 2024, carrying the SCIP near-infrared spectroPolarimeter (led by NAOJ) to measure magnetic fields from the photosphere up through the chromosphere. It found that the Sun's "quiet" regions — areas without sunspots that cover most of the solar surface — host a surprisingly intricate magnetic canopy: thin, thread-like magnetic field lines woven through the chromosphere, not a smooth expanding sheet. Computer simulations reproduced these threads and showed they are twisted by motions at the solar surface. Sunrise-III also mapped magnetic fields inside spicules — narrow jets of solar material — near the solar limb, revealing how the fields change with height. These observations help explain how energy generated by surface motions travels upward to heat the solar atmosphere to millions of degrees, a long-standing puzzle in solar physics. The results were published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters (Kubo et al. 2026, DOI 10.3847/2041-8213/ae909b) and cross-checked against the NAOJ press release and the arXiv preprint 2607.26353.
