"Ocean Snow: How Gooey Slime Carries Extra Carbon to the Deep Sea"

By Our World Published 10 November 2026 at 16:15 IST
Reading options

Every hour, stuff from the top of the ocean drifts down like snow. Scientists found that gooier bits carry much more carbon to the deep sea.

What Falls When It Doesn't Rain

Have you ever imagined it snowing underwater? It does. Tiny flakes of ocean life drift down all the time, even when there is no storm. Scientists call this marine snow.

Each flake is a clump of leftovers from the surface: bits of plankton, mucus, feces, and dust. It sounds squishy — because it is. But this squishy snow does one of Earth's most important jobs: it carries carbon from the warm top of the ocean down into the dark deep.

A Gooier Flake Carries More Down

For a long time, scientists puzzled over a mismatch. More than 10 gigatons of carbon sail downward in marine snow every year. But only about 2 gigatons actually reach the deep sea, where it can stay locked away for hundreds or thousands of years. What happens to the rest?

New research from Scripps Institution of Oceanography and the University of Lincoln found a surprising answer: how sticky the flake is. The study, published in the journal Science, shows that gooier, thicker marine snow can carry more than six times as much carbon to the deep ocean than thinner flakes.

Think of it like a worm in an apple. The soft flesh near the surface is easy to nibble. The tough core in the middle is harder to reach. Bacteria are like tiny nibblers: they eat the squishy outside of a flake first, but the sticky, viscous inside stays strong longer — giving the whole clump time to sink deeper before it is eaten up.

Why the Deep Sea Is Like a Big Carbon Bank

Carbon is a building block of life, but too much of it can warm our planet. When marine snow sinks past the sunlit zone and into the deep ocean, that carbon is tucked away in a quiet, cold place where it does not bubble back into the air right away.

The deeper a gooier flake can travel before bacteria break it apart, the more carbon it banks. So the thickness of something as ordinary as underwater slime helps decide how much climate-shaping carbon the ocean can store.

Try This at Home

Grab a clear glass and fill it with water. Drop in a pinch of cinnamon or pepper — those are your "plankton." Watch how the particles drift down. Now stir in a drop of corn syrup or a little oil. Can you see the particles fall more slowly, like a thicker flake? You have just made your own mini-marine-snow experiment.

Takeaway: not all ocean snow is the same. Gooier clumps fight bacterial nibblers longer, sink farther, and bank more carbon for the deep sea.

The Science, for Grown-Ups

The team used 3D confocal microscopy with fluorescent molecular rotors to measure nanoscale viscosity inside individual aggregates — a first look at what the researchers call "the muscoscape." They found less-viscous regions near a particle's surface get colonized by bacteria quickly, while denser, high-viscosity interior regions act as a barrier to bacterial infiltration. Laboratory experiments showed higher-viscosity aggregates degrade and fragment more slowly, and computer simulations suggest they sink farther. Read the full study: Inman, B.G., Humphries, S. & Azam, F. "Marine snow viscosity regulates microbial degradation and the ocean carbon sink," Science 394 (6820), 202. DOI: 10.1126/science.adx4170.
