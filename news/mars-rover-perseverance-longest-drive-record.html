How far can a robot drive on Mars? Perseverance just broke the record

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 22:55 IST
Reading options

NASA's Perseverance rover drove 45.22 km (28.10 miles) on Mars, becoming the farthest vehicle ever to roll on another world. Its engineering twin on Earth even got a Guinness World Records plaque.

The rover that keeps going farther

Mars is a cold, dusty, rocky planet about 225 million kilometers (140 million miles) away from Earth. It is not the easiest place to drive — the gravity is only 38 percent of ours, the ground is uneven and rocky, and thick dust storms can last for weeks.

Yet a robot there is setting records.

Since landing in Jezero Crater in February 2021, NASA's Perseverance rover has been rolling across the Martian surface, taking photos, drilling rocks, and searching for signs that Mars once had life. And now it has passed a major milestone.

A new record written in the red dust

On Sol 2006 — that is October 11, 2026, in Martian mission time — Perseverance's odometer ticked past 45.22 kilometers (28.10 miles). That makes it the farthest any vehicle has ever driven on another world.

For comparison, Perseverance's older sibling rover, Curiosity, has driven about 36 kilometers (22 miles) so far on Mars. And before that, the record belonged to the Mars Exploration Rovers Spirit and Opportunity.

So how did Perseverance cover more ground than any machine before it?

Smarter driving on a stranger planet

Perseverance did not just drive in a straight line. It spent months carefully navigating boulder fields, climbing steep slopes, stopping to study interesting rocks along the way.

The team on Earth taught the rover new tricks:

  • AutoNav — Perseverance can now drive on its own, using cameras to spot hazards and plan a safe path without waiting for commands from Earth. That means it can cover more ground in less time.
  • GBLC — A system that lets the rover know exactly where it is on Mars using photos taken from space, like GPS but built for a planet without satellites.
  • Stronger wheels — Engineers doubled the motor power in the wheels and raised the tilt limit to 24 degrees, so the rover can climb steeper hills.
  • Sharper brakes — Updated software helps Perseverance stop safely on loose sand or rocky ground.

The plaque that rode shotgun

Perseverance's engineering twin — a nearly identical rover that lives in a clean room on Earth — received an official Guinness World Records plaque for the achievement. It sits in NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory as a reminder that sometimes, the best way to break records is to keep moving.

And the journey is far from over. Perseverance is heading toward an ancient river delta in Jezero Crater, where scientists hope it will find signs of long-gone lakes and maybe, just maybe, the building blocks of life.

Try this at home

Build a Mars rover test track. Use a toy car or a small robot and drive it across different surfaces — sand, gravel, grass, and a smooth floor. Time how far it can go on each. On paper, mark how many "sols" (Mars days — about 24 hours and 39 minutes) your rover survives. Can you make it navigate an obstacle without touching the sides?

Fun fact

Perseverance carries a tiny sphere called the MOXIE experiment that turns Mars' carbon dioxide atmosphere into oxygen — the same gas we breathe. So while humans may not be on Mars yet, the rover is already making the air ready for them.

For grown-ups

The short version for curious grown-ups: on Sol 2006 (Oct 11, 2026), Perseverance's odometer hit 45.22 km (28.10 miles) — the farthest any vehicle has driven on another celestial body, surpassing its predecessor Curiosity. The rover's engineering twin on Earth received a Guinness World Records plaque for the milestone. Perseverance has been navigating Mars since landing in Jezero Crater in February 2021, and its team has pushed driving limits by raising the tilt tolerance to 24 degrees, reducing undercarriage clearance to 20 cm, and adding onboard Global Localization (GBLC) so the rover can autonomously know where it is without GPS. The full traverse is tracked via the mission's odometry system, cross-checked against orbital imagery from the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter. Source: NASA Science Blog (Vandi Verma, JPL); concept-level confirmation via Wikipedia Mars 2020 article.
