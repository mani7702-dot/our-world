Which period-drama details look too strange to be true — but really happened?

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 16:47 IST
Reading options

Period dramas on TV and film show tiny teacups, frequent fainting, and people who never smile — but what looks like Hollywood exaggeration is often real history. Here are 10 everyday details from costume dramas that historians confirm really happened.

Which period-drama details look too strange to be true — but really happened?

When you watch a period drama, it can all feel a little too perfect — tiny teacups, powdered wigs, people fainting at dramatic news. But what if the filmmakers got more right than they knew?

1. Teacups were tiny — really tiny

Modern mugs hold 12–16 ounces, but in Georgian and Regency England, teacups held just 4–6 ounces. Tea was expensive and a sign of wealth, so a small cup was both practical and polite. Those delicate little cups you see actors sipping from? They are accurate.

2. Wigs were dusted with real starch

Powdered wigs weren't just for style — the white powder was often starch, made from wheat or rice. Wig wearers applied it to give their hairpieces a pale, powdery finish. The practice was so common that "to powder one's wig" was a regular daily ritual.

3. Fainting was a real medical concern

Women in the 18th and 19th centuries fainted frequently — and not just for drama. Tight corsets compressed the ribcage, limiting lung capacity and blood flow. Sudden standing, strong smells, or emotional shock could easily trigger a faint. Doctors of the time called it "syncope."

4. Coaches really did bounce like wild ride

Traveling by horse-drawn stagecoach was jarring. Without shock absorbers, passengers were thrown around for hours. The word "bump" in old English literally comes from the sound of coaches hitting bumps in the road. Many a scene in Pride and Prejudice or Sense and Sensibility captures this perfectly.

5. Brown teeth were normal

Unlike the pristine white smiles in modern films, most people in the 18th century had brown, yellow, or missing teeth. Sugar was expensive and dental care was dangerous. Toothlessness was so common that "to have one's toothache" was a standard expression for pain.

6. People really wrote with quill pens

Every letter, novel, and legal document was handwritten with a goose-quill pen. Writers had to dip the pen in ink after every few words and sharpen the quill regularly. That's why historical letters have those distinctive spidery, inconsistent lines.

7. Candles needed constant attention

Before electricity, every room was lit by candles. But candles don't just burn — they drip, smoke, and need trimming. Every few hours, someone had to snip the wick with scissors to keep the flame steady. No wonder characters in period dramas are always tending fires and trimming wicks.

8. Perfume masked real odors

Bathrooms were primitive, streets were paved with dirt and animal waste, and people rarely bathed for days at a time. Heavy perfume, scented powders, and pomanders (bunches of herbs) were carried to mask unpleasant smells. The coquettish spritzing of perfume in dramas is historically spot-on.

9. Gloves were worn indoors

In the 18th century, gloves weren't just for outdoors — they were a status symbol worn at meals and in drawing rooms. Taking off a glove to shake hands (or a glove scene!) signified intimacy and trust.

10. Mirrors were expensive and rare

Most homes didn't have mirrors like we do today. A large, clear mirror cost a fortune and was a luxury item. That's why characters in period dramas often admire themselves in polished silver or reflective water — because most people simply didn't have mirrors.

Try this at home

Ask a grown-up to help you find a real quill pen (or make one from a craft stick). Dip it in ink and try writing your name — notice how hard it is to keep the lines even! Or compare a modern teacup to a tiny espresso cup: can you drink tea from something that small?

Fun Fact

The phrase "keeping a stiff upper lip" — often associated with British period dramas — was popularized by Victorian-era novels and etiquette guides. It didn't mean to suppress emotion but rather to control one's posture and expression in public, as showing too much feeling was considered uncouth.
