"How Does a Rock in Space Get a Name — and Who Decides?"

By Our World Published 01 November 2026 at 12:20 IST
Reading options

"A Slovenian scientist working in Northern Ireland just had a distant space rock named after him. The asteroid (20560) Nežič is 10.9 km wide and circles the Sun in the asteroid belt. Here is how a lump of rock millions of kilometres away ends up carrying a person's name."

How Does a Rock in Space Get a Name — and Who Decides?

A dot with a name

Imagine finding a tiny new dot in the night sky — a rock so far away it looks like a single speck. How would you even name it? That question just became real for Dr Rok Nežič, a scientist at the Armagh Observatory and Planetarium in Northern Ireland. An asteroid discovered in New Mexico back in 1999 has now been officially named Nežič in his honour, nearly three decades later.

Its full name is (20560) Nežič. That number is the key to the whole system.

The number comes first, the name comes later

When astronomers spot a brand-new asteroid, they do not stop to think of a nice name. There are far too many space rocks for that — more than a million have been catalogued, and new ones appear almost every night. So every object first gets a plain, ordered number based on when it was discovered. Number one is Ceres, found in 1801. The higher the number, the later it was found.

Only after scientists have tracked an asteroid long enough to be sure of its path around the Sun — its orbit — can it earn the "upgrade" of a real name. Nežič's rock, 10.9 kilometres across, sits in the asteroid belt, a wide ring of rocky leftovers circling the Sun between Mars and Jupiter. It was spotted on 9 September 1999 by an Earth-based sky survey called LINEAR, working from Socorro, New Mexico.

Who gets to choose?

The naming of small worlds is managed by the International Astronomical Union (IAU), the same worldwide body that names craters and moons. The rule is simple but strict: a name cannot be bought, and you cannot simply name an asteroid after yourself. Someone else has to nominate you. In Nežič's case, two people put his name forward, and the IAU approved it.

Names usually honour scientists, explorers, artists or places — people and things that have added something to human knowledge. Nežič studies dusty tails around comets using a technique called polarimetry, which reads how light waves twist when they bounce off tiny dust grains. A colleague at the same observatory, Shane Kelly, was honoured in the same round. His asteroid is even bigger — 15.6 kilometres wide — because, as Nežič explains, the largest asteroids were found first and named long ago, so newly named ones are often smaller.

As Nežič put it, having an asteroid carry your name is "a dream come true." He joked that he wishes he could visit it some day. For now, (20560) Nežič is safely cruising the asteroid belt, going around the Sun — just like we are.

Try this at home

You can build your own "asteroid belt" and practise naming it:

  1. Sprinkle a handful of dried beans or pebbles on a dark sheet of paper — these are your asteroids.
  2. Shine a torch from one side. Notice how each pebble casts a shadow. Astronomers spot asteroids the same way: as moving dots that block or reflect light.
  3. Write a number next to each pebble in the order you "discover" them — 1, 2, 3.
  4. Now pick your favourite and give it a name. Explain why it deserves one. Could it be a scientist, a grandparent, or your pet?

Fun Fact

An asteroid's official name always starts with its number in brackets, like a house number on a street. And some asteroids are named after surprising things — there is one honouring the composer Beethoven, and others named for famous artists, writers and even whole countries. There are rules, but the sky is full of imagination.

For grown-ups

"Dr Rok Nežič is a Slovenian astronomer and tours/outreach officer at Armagh Observatory and Planetarium (AOP) in Northern Ireland, where he arrived in 2015 to complete a doctorate in space and climate physics. The asteroid officially designated (20560) Nežič = 1999 RX118 was discovered at Socorro, New Mexico, on 9 September 1999 by the LINEAR survey, and appears in the Minor Planet Center's official record with the citation noting his cometary-dust polarimetry research. A colleague, Shane Kelly, also had an asteroid named: 15.6 km versus Nežič's 10.9 km. Small-body naming is governed by the International Astronomical Union (IAU); discoverers may propose names once an object's orbit is well enough determined, and a nomination must be put forward by others (two people nominated Nežič).
