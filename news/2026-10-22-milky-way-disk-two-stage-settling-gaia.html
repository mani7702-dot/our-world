How did our Milky Way's two disk layers form — and when did the thin one appear?

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 22:41 IST
Reading options

New Gaia satellite data reveals our Milky Way's disk flattened in two steps — a fast squeeze about 10.8 billion years ago then a slow calm that built the home of our Solar System.

A galaxy built in two layers

Look up on a very dark night and you can see a glowing band arching across the sky — the Milky Way, our home galaxy. From space, it would not look like one flat pancake. It is shaped like a stack of two: a thin disk where our Sun lives, and a thicker, puffier disk above and below it. New data from the Gaia space telescope is finally revealing how these two layers formed.

Born puffed up

When the Milky Way was brand new, gas inside it was hot and stormy, swirling like a shaken snow globe. Stars forming from that messy gas ended up on puffy, spread-out orbits — the ancestors of today's thick disk. Those old stars still cruise hundreds of light-years above and below the galaxy's mid-plane.

Then, about 10.8 billion years ago, something changed. The gas began to calm, cooling and sinking into a thinner layer. New stars formed in that calm disk, circling in tight, orderly paths — the birth of the thin disk where our Sun would one day take shape.

Watching the change

The European Space Agency's Gaia telescope maps over a billion stars by measuring their positions, colours, and motions with incredible precision. Scientists fed its latest data (DR3) into models that weigh up star ages and how rich they are in heavy elements.

The result shows a sharp break about 10.85 billion years ago. Before then, the galaxy's thickness dropped fast — from over 4,100 light-years to about 1,600 light-years in just 2 billion years. After the break, thinning slowed to a gentle trickle, settling to about 400 light-years near the Sun today.

Why two disks?

Think of it like a musical chord: the low note (thick disk) played first, loudly and messily. The higher notes (thin disk) joined later, calm and steady, settling right where the low note still lingers. Encounters with gas clouds, spiral arms, and the galactic bar gently shake older stars out of the thin plane, keeping the thick disk alive.

Every star in the thin disk — including our Sun, 4.6 billion years old — formed from gas that settled into that calm, flat layer after the great squeeze. So when you see the Milky Way arching overhead, you are really looking at a frozen family photo: old thick-disk stars at the edges, and a bright band of younger suns where new stars still form.

Try this at home

Make a galaxy in a jar! Fill a clear jar halfway with water, add a drop of milk, and swirl it. The cloudy swirl is like the young, turbulent Milky Way. Let it sit — the milk settles into a thin layer at the bottom. Tip the jar and swirl gently again, and watch how some cloud stays suspended. That is the thick disk remembering the galaxy's messy youth.

The Milky Way's thin disk near the Sun is only about 300–400 light-years thick, while the thick disk towers about three times higher above the mid-plane.

For grown-ups

A study in The Astrophysical Journal Letters led by David Mirabal (Instituto de Astrofisica de Canarias) used Gaia Data Release 3 colour-magnitude diagram fitting to trace vertical scale heights (hZ). Before ~10.85 Gyr ago, hZ dropped steeply from ~1.26 kpc to ~0.5 kpc in about 2 billion years as the gas cooled and the thick disk shrank. After that break, thinning became gradual (hZ ~0.1 kpc for the youngest populations). The older stars kept their thick-disk orbits, leaving a fossil record above and below the thin disk where our Sun now resides. Gaia DR3 was released in June 2022.
