"What happens when a mushroom makes you see tiny humans walking on your dinner plate?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 02:32 IST IST
Reading options

"In parts of China and the Philippines, people eat a special wild mushroom called Lanmaoa asiatica for its savory flavor — but if it isn't cooked long enough, they start seeing dozens of tiny human figures climbing on their food, walking around furniture, and even swimming in their soup. Scientists have finally identified the mushroom responsible and discovered it does NOT contain any known psychedelic compounds like psilocybin — the chemical that causes its strange effects is a complete mystery."

What happens when a mushroom makes you see tiny humans walking on your dinner plate?

A mushroom that brings tiny people to life

Imagine sitting down to eat your favorite meal, and as you lift your spoon, you see dozens of tiny people climbing over your soup, running around your table, and even swimming in your bowl. They look real — solid, three-dimensional, dressed in bright clothes — but they are only as big as your thumb.

Sounds like a fairy tale, right? But for people in parts of China and the Philippines, this isn't a story. It happens after they eat a wild mushroom called Lanmaoa asiatica, also known as "Jian shou qing" in China and "Sedesdem" in the Philippines.

The mushroom mystery that puzzled scientists for 90 years

The strange story started way back in 1934, when researchers first heard reports from Papua New Guinea of people seeing tiny humans after eating wild mushrooms. They called it "mushroom madness." For decades, scientists thought these visions might just be cultural beliefs — stories passed down that influenced what people thought they saw.

But then, during the 1990s, more reports came in from Yunnan Province in China. People described the same tiny figures: elves, clowns, and little human-like characters marching around their homes. The clues all pointed to one mushroom — the tasty, blue-staining bolete that locals love to eat.

When scientists tested it, they were stunned. There was no psilocybin (the chemical in magic mushrooms) and no other known psychedelic compound. The mushroom had been examined even by Albert Hofmann, the famous Swiss scientist who discovered LSD and psilocybin — and he couldn't find the active ingredient either!

DNA cracks the case

Finally, a team of researchers from the University of Utah — PhD student Colin Domnauer and mycologist Bryn Dentinger — set out to solve the mystery. They collected mushroom specimens from China and the Philippines and used DNA sequencing to identify them.

Their conclusion: every mushroom causing these "tiny human" visions was the same species — Lanmaoa asiatica. These mushrooms are relatives of boletes, the same group that includes porcini and other beloved edible fungi. They grow in the wild, form friendships with pine tree roots underground, and taste delicious and savory (that's the "umami" flavor).

But here is the twist: no one has ever found the actual chemical that makes people see tiny humans. It is a completely new kind of hallucinogen that scientists have never encountered before!

What the tiny people actually look like

The "tiny humans" are not just random shapes. People describe them as being between 3 and 30 centimeters tall — about the length of your hand to your forearm. They look like elves or fairy-tale characters, often wearing brightly colored clothes. They walk around realistically, climbing over furniture, going through doorways, and even tumbling off tables.

One person described seeing tiny humans leap from their spoon into their soup, where they began to swim — then when they took another spoonful, the tiny figures were still visible inside their mouth!

Unlike many hallucinations that people know are "make-believe," these visions feel startlingly real. However, most people stay aware that they are intoxicated and understand the visions are not real.

Why the mushroom is safe — when cooked right

In Yunnan, L. asiatica is a popular food served in restaurants, especially during mushroom season from June to August. Locals even have a rule: restaurants set 15-minute timers and warn diners, "Don't eat it until the timer goes off, or you might see little people!"

The cooking process breaks down the mysterious compound, making the mushroom perfectly safe and delicious when properly prepared. But if undercooked, the delayed hallucinations can last 1 to 7 days — sometimes even weeks!

What this teaches us about our brains

The mushroom is teaching scientists something fascinating about how our brains create reality. These tiny human visions are not random — they follow rules. The figures walk around obstacles, interact with real objects, and behave as if they have minds of their own.

Similar "tiny person" hallucinations also happen in other conditions: when people withdraw from alcohol, when they have certain eye diseases, or as part of aging. This suggests that our brains may be naturally wired to "fill in" tiny figures when something goes slightly wrong in how we process what we see.

By figuring out the mushroom's unknown chemical, scientists hope to learn more about how our brains create the world we see around us every day.

Try this at home

Draw a tiny person on a piece of paper — maybe 2 centimeters tall, wearing a bright hat. Now draw the same scene again, but this time add a second tiny person who is climbing on a book, and a third one swimming in a teacup. Our brains love to create stories about small figures and impossible places — just like the visions caused by this mysterious mushroom!

Fun Fact

The mushroom's name Lanmaoa asiatica means it is found mainly in Asia. Local restaurants are so serious about keeping people safe that some restaurants in Yunnan ban alcoholic drinks along with undercooked mushrooms, and local governments send out public alerts during mushroom season to warn about possible poisonings!

The ScienceDaily article reports on a study led by Colin Domnauer and Bryn Dentinger at the University of Utah and the Natural History Museum of Utah, published in Mycologia (June 2026, DOI: 10.1080/00275514.2026.2670968). Using DNA sequencing of 53 mushroom specimens from natural habitats and fungarium collections worldwide, the researchers confirmed that Lanmaoa asiatica is the sole species in its genus known to produce psychoactive effects. Chemical and genomic analyses ruled out psilocybin, psilocin, and muscimol — none of the known hallucinogenic compounds. The mushroom is a bolete (family Boletaceae) forming ectomycorrhizal relationships with Pinus yunnanensis (Yunnan pine). Wikipedia's article on Lanmaoa asiatica confirms these details and notes that Lilliputian hallucinations (seeing tiny autonomous human figures) also arise in neurological conditions including Charles Bonset syndrome, alcohol withdrawal, and macular degeneration. The delayed onset (12–24 hours) and multi-day duration of the mushroom's effects suggest the unknown compound may act as a prodrug or through the gut-brain axis.
