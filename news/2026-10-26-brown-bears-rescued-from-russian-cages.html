How were two brown bears saved from tiny cages? A 5-year rescue across three countries

A cage too small to stand

Imagine being locked up in a box so cramped you can barely move. For most of their lives, two brown bears named Bu and Tyapa knew nothing but tiny cages in Russia. One bear — Bu — had been forced to perform tricks in a circus. The other — Tyapa — lived in a cage just 2.5 meters by 2.5 meters, too low to stand upright, with no roof to protect him from winter snow or summer sun. He had no water bowl and had to drink from a hose.

But today, these two bears roam freely in a lush woodland in Ireland, exploring trees, splashing in streams, and discovering what it means to truly live like a bear.

A rescue delayed by war

In 2021, Killian McLaughlin, founder of Wild Ireland sanctuary in County Donegal, heard about Bu and Tyapa and knew he had to help. But the rescue turned out to be one of the most complicated animal operations ever attempted.

First, Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022 — the planned route through Ukraine became impossible. Then, in February 2026, escalating conflict involving Iran closed airspace across the Middle East, scaring off the alternative route.

Despite two major disruptions, more than 100 people refused to give up. Veterinarians, animal transport specialists, lawyers, cargo teams, and sanctuary volunteers worked across three countries — Russia, the United Arab Emirates, and Ireland — to make the journey happen.

Finally home in Ireland

In late October 2025, rescuers drove 36 hours to the remote Russian region of Dagestan to reach the bears. They had to cut Bu and Tyapa out of their rusted steel cages, sedate them gently, and place them in secure transport crates. After veterinary care and quarantine, the journey began in earnest.

The bears traveled from Moscow via Abu Dhabi to Dublin, finally arriving at Wild Ireland on August 3, 2026 — nearly five years to the day after the rescue began.

Since arriving, both bears have made a remarkable recovery. They have gained weight, their coats are thick and healthy, and follow-up blood tests show their kidney function — damaged by years of dehydration — has returned to normal. At Wild Ireland, they now explore a rich habitat of woodland, streams, and sheltered areas where they can retreat for privacy if they choose.

How does a young bear learn to be a bear?

It turns out that being a bear is a full-time job — and these two missed their crash course in cub-hood. At the sanctuary, the bear care team hides food around the habitat to encourage natural foraging behavior. By searching for berries, insects, and fish-like treats, Bu and Tyapa are relearning how to act like bears — sniffing, pawing, and exploring every corner just as their wild cousins do.

Their keeper, Chloe McElhatton, studied zoology at Queen's University Belfast before joining the team. She says, "We've done a lot of work hiding food around the bear habitats, encouraging them to search and forage for it themselves. This promotes their natural behaviors and helps them become more comfortable in that new open space."

Try this at home

Make a mini bear habitat in a shoebox. Crumple brown paper to make trees. Use a small bowl of water for a "stream." Hide a few treats (like raisins or nuts) in different spots — under the paper "bushes," behind the "rocks" (pebbles), and near the water. Now pretend you are a bear searching for food. Which sense do you use first — smell, sight, or touch? Bears have an incredible sense of smell (7 times stronger than a bloodhound!), so they can sniff out food from miles away. After your bear finds all the treats, talk about how lucky they are to live free — unlike circus bears or animals kept in tiny cages.

Fun Fact

Brown bears are surprisingly fast runners — they can sprint up to 30 miles per hour (48 km/h), faster than Olympic sprinters! But despite their speed, they prefer to eat plants — up to 90% of their diet is vegetation like berries, grasses, and roots. They are truly gentle giants: despite weighing as much as a small car, a wild brown bear would much rather run away from you than come anywhere near.

For grown-ups

"Brown bears (Ursus arctos) are large omnivorous mammals native to Eurasia and North America. They are classified as 'Least Concern' by the IUCN Red List with an estimated 200,000-300,000 individuals worldwide, though many local populations are endangered. The brown bear's nearest relative is the American black bear. Brown bears can live up to 25-30 years in the wild and weigh anywhere from 220 to 1,500 pounds depending on the subspecies and habitat. They are skilled swimmers and can run up to 30 mph — faster than humans. The rescue of Bu and Tyapa involved over 100 people including veterinarians, animal transport specialists, lawyers, and cargo teams across Russia, the UAE, and Ireland. The operation was delayed twice: first by Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, then by Iran- linked airspace closures in February 2026. The bears traveled from Moscow to Dublin via Abu Dhabi, arriving on August 3, 2026. Wild Ireland, founded in 2019 in Burnfoot, County Donegal, is a sanctuary that provides refuge to rescued, exploited, and hunted animals including brown bears, grey wolves, Eurasian lynx, and wild boar in a Celtic rainforest setting."
