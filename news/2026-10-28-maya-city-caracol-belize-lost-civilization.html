"How archaeologists revealed Caracol — the Maya's biggest city, hidden under jungle for 1,000 years"

By Our World Published 28 October 2026 at "14:07 IST" IST
Reading options

"Deep in the Belizean jungle, the ancient Maya city of Caracol sprawls across 200 square kilometres — bigger than Belize City. For centuries it was invisible beneath the trees. Now, laser scanners, buried tombs, and a lost death mask are rewriting everything we thought we knew about this powerful ancient civilisation."

How did Caracol become the Maya's largest city — and why was it buried under jungle for 1,000 years?

1. A city hiding in plain sight

Deep in the forests of Belize, behind a winding dirt road that inspired its name — "Caracol" means "snail" in Spanish — sits one of the ancient world's greatest cities. If you can believe it, this Maya metropolis was so well-hidden under jungle growth that a logger named Rosa Mai stumbled upon it by accident in 1937, while searching for mahogany trees.

For nearly a thousand years, Caracol had been swallowed by the rainforest. When archaeologist A.H. Anderson finally reached the ruins the following year, he found 11 carved stone monuments poking through the green. Today, thanks to laser-scanning technology, we know Caracol covered 200 square kilometres — that's bigger than Belize City, and it supported a population of over 100,000 people at its peak.

2. Lasers that see through trees

For decades, archaeologists could only explore small sections of Caracol on foot, hacking through vines and scrambling over fallen logs. Then, in 2009, they tried something revolutionary: LiDAR — short for "light detection and ranging."

They flew an aircraft equipped with powerful lasers over the entire site. The lasers sliced through the tree canopy, bounce after bounce, mapping every hidden temple, causeway, and rooftop beneath the jungle. Suddenly, the full scale of Caracol was revealed: 36,000 structures, 11 new roads, and thousands of residential groups that had been invisible for centuries. It was like discovering an entire city in your backyard that you never knew was there.

3. The war that made Caracol a superpower

Caracol wasn't always a powerhouse. For years, it was a minor player — a client state of the more famous city of Tikal. But in AD 562, something dramatic happened. Caracol's ruler, Lord Yajaw Te' K'inich II, led a "star war" — a ritual battle meant to capture an enemy king — and defeated Tikal. Just like that, the underdog became the giant.

Over the next century, under the brilliant rule of K'an II, the city built roads, grew its population, and became the dominant force in the Maya world. The irony? Tikal would later copy Caracol's architectural style — a sign that the tables had turned.

4. A king's mask found in the dark

In 2025, archaeologists made one of the most stunning discoveries in recent Maya history. Deep inside a tomb in the South Acropolis, they found the intact burial of Te K'ab Chaak, the mythical founder of Caracol's dynasty, who ruled around AD 350.

The tomb held breathtaking treasures: a jade mosaic death mask, elaborate jadeite ear flares, spiny oyster shells from the Pacific coast (over 200 miles away), and obsidian blades from central Mexico. The fact that these luxury goods were in the tomb of the city's very first ruler told archaeologists something extraordinary — Caracol was trading with distant civilisations 600 years earlier than they had previously believed.

5. Why did it burn?

At the height of its power around AD 800, Caracol was the most advanced city the Maya world had ever seen. Its residents enjoyed public markets, stone-built reservoirs, and a society where even ordinary families had access to luxury goods like jade and obsidian.

But then, in AD 895, disaster struck. The city caught fire — and never recovered. Archaeologists found burnt structures and charred remains of elite goods. Within decades, the jungle reclaimed the streets. Unlike other Maya cities that declined slowly, Caracol's collapse was sudden and total. The jungle kept its secrets safe for another thousand years.

Try this at home

Build a mini LiDAR! You don't need an airplane — you can simulate how archaeologists "see" through obstacles at home. Tape a small object (like a toy figurine) under a colander or mesh strainer. Hold a flashlight at an angle so the light bounces off the mesh and casts a shadow of the hidden object onto a wall. Move the flashlight around — notice how the shadow shifts and reveals the object's shape even though you never lift the colander. This is similar to how LiDAR lasers bounce off tree leaves to map what's hiding underneath!

Fun Fact

The tomb of Te K'ab Chaak contained 97 shards of jade and 26 pieces of shell — all carefully arranged to form a magnificent mosaic death mask. Archaeologists had to painstakingly piece the mask back together, like the world's most valuable jigsaw puzzle, using over 100 fragments!

for-grownups

Caracol's "star wars" were real — the Maya carved them into stone. A star war (or "k'atun war") was a ritual battle where warriors captured an enemy ruler, a practice linked to Venus, which the Maya sometimes called the "heavenly star." The captured ruler would be sacrificed or ritually killed, and his city forced into submission. In AD 562, Caracol's victory over Tikal triggered a 120-year decline for Tikal — the captured Tikal lord's name disappears from monuments, and the city stopped building new temples. Meanwhile, Caracol entered its golden age. The "symbolic egalitarianism" system the Chases discovered meant that while the city was still ruled by divine kings, common people had access to luxury goods through market trade — a uniquely egalitarian twist on Maya society that challenges the old stereotype of Maya as solely temple-building theocrats.

For grown-ups

"Caracol is a UNESCO-recognised ancient Maya city in western Belize. At its peak (AD 650–800), it covered approximately 200 square kilometres (77 square miles) and supported an estimated 100,000–115,000 people — far larger than Belize City today. The site was lost to the jungle for a millennium after abandonment around AD 900, until a logger named Rosa Mai rediscovered it in 1937. Archaeologist A.H. Anderson named it 'Caracol' (Spanish for 'snail') after the spiral-shaped access road and snail shells littering the site. Led by Drs. Arlen and Diane Chase since 1985, excavations used LiDAR (light detection and ranging) in 2009 to reveal the city's full scale: 36,000 structures, 11 previously-unknown causeways, and thousands of residential groups. The city's power stemmed from 'star wars' — ritual warfare that captured enemy rulers. In AD 562, Caracol defeated Tikal (its former overlord) in the first recorded Maya star war. Its most famous ruler, K'an II (AD 618–658), expanded the causeway system and ushered in a golden age. A 2025 excavation uncovered the tomb of Te K'ab Chaak (dated to around AD 350), the dynasty's founder, containing a jade mosaic death mask, jadeite ear flares, spiny oyster shells from the Pacific coast, and obsidian from central Mexico — proving early trade connections with Teotihuacan. The city abruptly burned and was abandoned around AD 895, ending the Maya Classic period in the region."
