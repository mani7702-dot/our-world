"Why Is Half of Mars Mysteriously Hot Inside?"

By Our World Published 01 November 2026 at "08:01" IST
Reading options

"Mars already looks like two different worlds on its surface — smooth plains on top, cratered highlands below. Now scientists have discovered that the split runs deep, with one half of the planet's insides hundreds of degrees hotter than the other."

Mars has two faces. Look at a picture of the Red Planet and you can see them: the northern half is mostly smooth, low, dusty plains, while the southern half is packed with tall mountains and ancient, battered craters. Scientists have wondered about that split for years. Now a new study has found that the difference does not stop at the surface — it goes all the way down.

A planet that is warmer on one side

Researchers measured Mars's insides and found that the rock deep beneath the southern hemisphere is roughly 200 to 400 degrees Celsius hotter than the rock beneath the north. That is a huge gap. It means part of the southern interior may even be partly molten, like slush rather than solid stone.

So how do you take the temperature of something you cannot open? You use gravity.

Weighing a planet from space

The team studied how the gravity around Mars pulls on spacecraft. Because Mars travels a slightly stretched-out orbit and spins on a tilted axis, the Sun tugs on it a little differently through the seasons. Those tiny tugs make the planet's gravity wobble, and satellites feel every wobble as a small change in speed.

By tracking those speed changes over many years, the scientists built a 3-D model of what is hidden inside. They combined data from three missions — Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Odyssey and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter — using a clever method nicknamed "tidal tomography."

Why the hidden heat matters

The hot zone helps explain other Martian puzzles. Old iron-rich rocks in the south carry a strange magnetic signature, and NASA's InSight lander found that quake waves lose energy faster in the south — both make sense if the region is hotter. A warmer interior could also tell us more about how Mars formed the basins that may once have held water.

Scientists still disagree about why Mars turned out so uneven inside. Perhaps a giant impact warmed one side, perhaps the southern mantle once churned like a slow-boiling pot, or perhaps thick rocky layers simply trapped heat like a blanket.

Fun fact: We have never dug more than a few metres into Mars — yet a study like this lets us "feel" thousands of kilometres down, just by watching how spacecraft speed up and slow down.

Try this at home! You can test the idea without a spaceship. Wrap a small mystery object inside a sock and ask a friend to guess what is inside — then try guessing again by gently squeezing and tapping it. The outside feels the same, but the way it pushes back gives clues about what hides within. That is what gravity does for Mars: it lets us guess what is inside from a machine far away.

For grown-ups

"A study published 27 August 2026 in Nature reports a thermal anomaly beneath Mars's crustal dichotomy. Lead author Alexander Berne (Caltech PhD '26, now University of Arizona) and co-author Amirhossein Bagheri (Caltech, former InSight team member) used 'tidal tomography': analysing decades of tiny velocity changes in Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Odyssey and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter as the Sun's pull on Mars varies across its elliptical, tilted orbit, then inverting those gravity signatures into a 3-D model of the interior. The southern interior is inferred to be ~200-400 °C warmer than the north and possibly partially molten. The result could help explain the north-south magnetic contrast in crustal minerals, InSight's finding that seismic waves attenuate faster in the south, and how basins that may have held water formed. Competing hypotheses for the cause: heat released from the north by a giant impact, past convection in the southern mantle, or thick southern structures trapping heat. Funded by NASA; co-authors from Brown, Arizona, NASA Goddard, Academia Sinica, Rome, Delft, UCLA, UT Austin, CU Boulder and UC Santa Cruz. DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10893-x."
