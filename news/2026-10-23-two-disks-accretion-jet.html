How does a black hole's dinner disk power its cosmic fire hoses?

By Our World Published 23 October 2026 at 19:45 IST
Reading options

Supermassive black holes launch twin jets of energy, but their outer disks shouldn't be able to generate the strong magnetic fields those jets need. New X-ray and radio data show the inner disk swaps into a hotter, thicker form that does the job.

Two disks, one black hole

At the heart of almost every galaxy sits a supermassive black hole — a gravitational trap millions to billions of times heavier than our Sun. When such a black hole is actively feeding, it is rarely quiet. Streams of gas spiral inward, forming a dazzling, superheated accretion disk, and from the black hole's poles it fires twin jets of ionized matter that can blast across hundreds of thousands of light-years. The puzzle is: how does the dinner disk power these cosmic fire hoses?

The magnetic puzzle

Most astronomers think jets are launched by the Blandford-Znajek process: a spinning black hole hands over its rotational energy through strong, organized magnetic fields that loop through the disk and out along the rotation axis. That works beautifully for "hot, thick" disks. But some of the brightest, most actively feeding black holes are surrounded by cold, thin disks — and thin disks struggle to hold the large-scale magnetic fields the process needs. That should mean no strong jets... yet those same systems light up the sky with them. A real contradiction.

Peering at 3C 120

To crack the puzzle, an international team turned the new XRISM X-ray telescope and several radio observatories at 3C 120, an active galaxy about 420 million light-years away whose central black hole is gulping gas and shooting a powerful jet. XRISM spotted a tell-tale iron emission line whose shape was stretched and skewed by extreme gravity. Working that signature through Einstein's equations told the scientists exactly how close the inner disk reaches to the black hole.

The answer: the cold, thin disk is truncated at roughly 20 times the black hole's radius, and inside that boundary the disk transforms into a hot, thick flow — the very structure Blandford-Znajek needs to fire up a jet. So the disk is not truly "one disk" but two in one: an outer thin layer and a hotter inner engine, side by side, letting one black hole both feast and fire its jets at the same time.

The big picture

This switch in the disk's shape appears to be a universal rule, not a one-off. By showing exactly how matter, magnetic fields, and black-hole spin join forces at the very edge of the event horizon, the result gives researchers a clear, repeatable way to watch jets being born. Each new jet we understand helps explain how black holes shape the evolution of their host galaxies — and why the universe looks the way it does today.

Try this at home

Model a black hole jet with a balloon and two straws. Poke the straws straight through the center of a slightly inflated balloon (the straws are the rotation axis). When you let go of the balloon mouth, the rubber neck constricts the airflow and the escaping air (the "jet") shoots out both ends in opposite, straight streams — just like a black hole's polar jets. The balloon skin is the disk, and the two streams show how jets always emerge along the spin axis.

Fun Fact

If the thin disk around a feeding supermassive black hole stretched out flat, it would be so vast that from Earth we would see it edge-on as a razor-sharp ring — only about 20 micrograms of gas per square meter, yet hot enough to outshine an entire galaxy.

For the grown-ups

Astrophysicists link relativistic jets to the Blandford-Znajek mechanism, which extracts spin energy from a black hole via large-scale poloidal magnetic fields. The puzzle: cold, geometrically thin accretion disks cannot sustain such fields, yet high-accretion-rate systems still launch powerful jets. Using XRISM X-ray spectroscopy and the Global Millimeter VLBI Array (GMVA), VLBA, and the East Asian VLBI Network, researchers imaged the broad-line radio galaxy 3C 120 (~420 million light-years away; black hole mass ~6.3 × 10⁷ M☉, z = 0.033). XRISM revealed a relativistically broadened iron Kα line, showing the cold thin disk is truncated at ~20 Rg, inside which a hot, geometrically thick flow takes over — providing the magnetic scaffolding for Blandford-Znajek jet launching. Co-lead authors Hirofumi Noda (Tohoku University) and Kazuhiro Hada (Nagoya City University). Published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, "Disk Truncation Triggers Relativistic Jet Launching of a Highly Accreting Supermassive Black Hole."

For grown-ups

Astrophysicists have long linked relativistic jets to the Blandford-Znajek mechanism, which extracts a spinning black hole's rotational energy via large-scale poloidal magnetic fields. But cold, geometrically thin accretion disks cannot sustain such fields, creating a paradox at high accretion rates. Using XRISM X-ray spectroscopy and the Global Millimeter VLBI Array (GMVA) plus VLBA and the East Asian VLBI Network, researchers measured a relativistically broadened iron K-alpha emission line in the broad-line radio galaxy 3C 120 (distance ~420 million light-years, z=0.033, central black hole mass ~6.3 x 10^7 solar masses). Relativistic broadening of the line shows the cold thin disk is truncated at roughly 20 gravitational radii (Rg), where the inner region is replaced by a hot, geometrically thick flow that can sustain the magnetic fields required for jet launching via Blandford-Znajek. Co-lead authors: Hirofumi Noda (Tohoku University) and Kazuhiro Hada (Nagoya City University). Published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, "Disk Truncation Triggers Relativistic Jet Launching of a Highly Accreting Supermassive Black Hole."
