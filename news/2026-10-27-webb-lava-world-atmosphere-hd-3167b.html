"Can a World of Melted Rock Really Keep an Atmosphere?"

By Our World Published at 12:15 IST IST
Reading options

"James Webb Space Telescope studies a super-Earth exoplanet where the ground is forever molten, yet it holds onto an atmosphere — revealing secrets about how rocky worlds, including baby Earth, keep or lose their air."

Can a World of Melted Rock Really Keep an Atmosphere?

Imagine a planet so close to its star that the ground boils — rivers of lava flow under a sky glowing red-hot. This isn't a scene from a fantasy movie; it's a real type of world called a lava planet, and one of them, 154 light-years away, just surprised astronomers by keeping an atmosphere despite the scorching heat.

What Is a Lava World?

Lava worlds are rocky planets that orbit so close to their stars that their star-facing sides are constantly covered in molten rock. Think of it like this: if Earth were moved much closer to the Sun, our oceans would boil away and the ground would melt into oceans of lava. That's the everyday reality for these worlds!

The planet HD 3167 b is one such lava world. It's a "super-Earth" — bigger than our planet but still rocky — and it circles its star so fast that one full trip takes just one Earth day. Its surface temperature reaches over 1,700°C (3,100°F), hot enough to vaporize most rocks.

How Do We Spot an Atmosphere on a Faraway World?

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) uses a clever trick called the secondary eclipse method. When the planet passes behind its star (from our viewpoint), it disappears from sight. By measuring how much the total brightness drops, scientists can tell how much light the planet itself is glowing with in the infrared.

Here's the key insight: if a planet has no atmosphere, its star-facing side should be as hot as absolutely possible — like a bare rock baking in an oven. But if it does have an atmosphere, that air acts like a blanket, spreading heat around. The day side cools down, and the planet glows less brightly.

HD 3167 b was cooler than the theoretical maximum, which told the team that an atmosphere is likely wrapping around it — even though the planet is being blasted with intense stellar radiation every single hour of every single day.

Why Does This Matter?

At first glance, a world of lava might seem boring for the search for life. It's far too hot for plants, animals, or even microbes. But HD 3167 b teaches us something precious: how rocky planets hold onto (or lose) their atmospheres.

Scientists think that early Earth may have looked a lot like this — a molten ball of rock covered in a global ocean of magma, before things cooled down enough for our planet to grow the oceans and skies we know today. By studying HD 3167 b, we're peeking back in time at our own planet's dramatic babyhood.

The team is now studying nine more ultra-hot lava worlds to find out whether there's a "tipping point" temperature where planets either keep or lose their atmospheres. Each discovery helps us understand not just exotic worlds far away, but our own cosmic story closer to home.

Try This at Home

Look up at the night sky on a clear evening. Every star you see is like a distant sun, and many of them have planets orbiting around them. Somewhere out there, a world is completing a full orbit before you finish reading this article — and its nightside is just as scorching as its day side. Can you imagine a day that lasts exactly as long as one full spin of our planet?

Fun Fact

HD 3167 b wasn't always known by that name. Astronomers first spotted it with NASA's Kepler Space Telescope (during its extended K2 mission) as K2-96 b. Its official designation "HD 3167 b" comes from the star catalog where its host star was first recorded — a reminder that every numbered star in the sky has a story waiting to be discovered.

for-grown-ups: HD 3167 b is a rocky super-Earth (1.6 R⊕, ~4.8 M⊕) in the constellation Pisces, orbiting HD 3167 every 0.96 days at just 0.018 AU — a classic "lava world" or ultra-short-period planet. JWST secondary-eclipse observations in the mid-infrared showed the planet's dayside to be cooler than the maximum theoretical temperature for an airless body, implying the presence of an atmosphere that redistributes heat (Coy et al. 2026, ApJL, doi:10.3847/2041-8213/ae7f23). This makes HD 3167 b the coolest known lava world with evidence for an atmosphere — a crucial data point in the search for a critical temperature transition between airless and atmosphere-bearing rocky worlds. Wikipedia's HD 3167 b article confirms the orbital parameters, the 2016 discovery (Vanderburg et al.), and the July 2026 JWST study. The Lava planet Wikipedia article provides broader context: such worlds orbit extremely close to their stars, are tidally locked, and may include Io-like "super-Io" planets with continuous volcanism. The study was led by Brandon Park Coy (UChicago) in Megan Weiner Mansfield's program surveying 10 ultra-hot worlds. The "cosmic sandbar" model (Nguyen 2026) proposes that outgassing from magma oceans continuously regulates these atmospheres.
