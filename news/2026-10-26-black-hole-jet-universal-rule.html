"When Do Black Holes Fire Giant Space Jets — and Why at the 2% Point?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 12:13 IST
Reading options

"Astronomers found a surprising 2% rule: whether it's a small black hole or a supermassive giant, jets fire when feeding drops to the same critical threshold — a rule that works across the entire universe."

1. When do black holes fire giant space jets — and why does the 2% point matter?

How did a 2% rule connect tiny and titanic black holes?

Not all of the time. A black hole that just finished gobbling a star might sit quietly for months or even years — and then, suddenly, it fires a jet. Why the long wait?

The surprising answer: the jet appears when the black hole's feeding slows to about 2% of its maximum safe rate. And that 2% is the same for both tiny stellar black holes and the galaxy-smashing giants millions of times heavier.

2. What happens when a star gets too close to a black hole?

A star torn apart in a cosmic tug-of-war

Imagine a star wandering a little too close to a supermassive black hole — millions or billions of times the Sun's mass. The gravity on the near side of the star pulls much harder than on the far side. That difference is called the tidal force, and it stretches the star into a long, thin noodle before ripping it apart in an event called a tidal disruption event.

The shredded star doesn't vanish all at once. Some of it falls toward the black hole, forming a swirling accretion disk that glows with light. The rest loops back out — and in some cases, the black hole fires a relativistic jet: a narrow beam of particles shooting outward at nearly the speed of light.

Those jets can carry enormous energy across vast distances, shaping entire galaxies in the process.

3. Why do some jets fire immediately — and others years late?

The 2% feeding rule that connects all black holes

Scientists noticed something puzzling: sometimes a tidal disruption event produces a jet almost right away. Other times, the black hole stays quiet for months or years before suddenly bursting a jet into space.

Dr. Adelle Goodwin and her team tracked 20 of these stellar deaths. They discovered that the delayed jets all appear when the black hole's feeding rate drops to roughly 2% of the Eddington limit.

The Eddington limit is a fundamental boundary: it is the point where radiation pressure pushing outward balances gravity pulling inward. Beyond it, a black hole would blow itself apart; below it, everything settles calmly.

The real "wow" moment: the same 2% rule was already known for stellar-mass black holes — the smaller kind found in our own Milky Way. That means black holes that differ in size by a factor of a million follow the same universal rule.

A rule written in the sky

Because radio telescopes are expensive and competition for observing time is fierce, knowing when a delayed jet is likely to appear helps astronomers aim their giant dishes at the right moment.

For curious young stargazers: the next time you hear "black hole," remember that the same physics governs a black hole 10 times the Sun's mass and one millions of times heavier. The universe, it seems, speaks the same language at every scale.

Fun Fact: The key insight for this discovery came not from a telescope, but from a conversation over drinks at a scientific conference in Madrid — sometimes the best breakthroughs happen when scientists talk to each other.

Try this at home: Draw two circles of different sizes (one small, one huge). Around each, draw arrows spiraling inward toward the center. Now imagine the big one is a supermassive black hole and the small one is a stellar black hole. Both follow the same feeding rule — the 2% point — even though one is millions of times bigger!

For grown-ups

"A team led by Dr. Adelle Goodwin at Curtin University's International Centre of Radio Astronomy Research (ICRAR) studied tidal disruption events (TDEs) — rare moments when a star wanders too close to a supermassive black hole and gets torn apart. Using observations from telescopes across Australia, the US, India, South Africa, and space, they tracked 20 tidal disruption events and found that delayed radio jets appear when the black hole's feeding rate drops to approximately 2% of the Eddington limit. The Eddington limit is the theoretical balance point where outward radiation pressure counters inward gravitational pull. Remarkably, this same 2% threshold was already known to govern jet formation in much smaller stellar-mass black holes in our own Milky Way. The discovery, published in Nature Astronomy, suggests that black hole physics operates by the same rules regardless of mass. Wikipedia's 'Tidal disruption event' article confirms that stars passing close to supermassive black holes undergo spaghettification and form accretion disks that emit radiation, with relativistic jets produced in a fraction of cases. Wikipedia's 'Eddington luminosity' article confirms the Eddington limit as the balance between radiation pressure and gravity."
