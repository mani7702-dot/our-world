"What New Things Will NASA's UVEX Telescope See in Ultraviolet Light?"

What New Things Will NASA's UVEX Telescope See in Ultraviolet Light?

Seeing the invisible rainbow

When you look up at the night sky, you can only see a tiny slice of what is really there. Human eyes detect visible light — the colours of the rainbow — but space is full of other kinds of light, too. One of those is ultraviolet (UV) light, which sits just beyond the violet end of what we can see. It is invisible to us, but it reveals some of the most dramatic events in the universe.

Right now, there is almost no UV telescope working. The old one — called GALEX — stopped operating over a decade ago. But NASA has announced plans for a new, more powerful UV telescope called UVEX (the UltraViolet Explorer). It should launch sometime around 2030 or 2031.

Ten times the sensitivity

UVEX will be built to survey the entire sky in UV light — mapping it in a way that has never been done before. It will be up to 100 times more sensitive than GALEX, meaning it can spot objects that are 100 times dimmer. Think of it like upgrading from a small flashlight to a powerful spotlight that can light up the darkest corners of the universe.

One of the big things UVEX will study is low-mass galaxies — tiny, faint galaxies made of old, simple stars. These little galaxies are like living fossils from the early universe, and studying them in UV light will tell us how galaxies formed and changed over billions of years.

Cosmic fireworks and gravitational waves

The universe is full of fireworks — but many of them only show themselves in ultraviolet. Flares from nearby stars, stellar mergers, and tidal disruption events (when a star gets torn apart by a black hole) all light up in UV. UVEX will watch for these explosions over and over, like a cosmic lighthouse that never stops sweeping the sky.

UVEX will also be ready to drop everything and point at the spot where gravitational waves are detected. These ripples in spacetime are caused when two black holes or neutron stars crash together. By the time UVEX sees the aftermath, it can help scientists understand what happens when these violent collisions unfold.

Try this at home

Grab a blacklight (UV light) and check out some everyday items. White paper, teeth, and even some flowers glow brightly under UV! Ask an adult to help you safely test this. Notice how the invisible light reveals patterns you never saw before — just like UVEX will do for the whole universe.

Fun Fact

The word "ultraviolet" means "beyond violet." It was discovered in 1801 by German physicist Johann Wilhelm Ritter, who noticed that dark paper near a violet spectral line turned darker — proving there was light beyond what the eye can see.
