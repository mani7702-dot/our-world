"Why does Antarctica have a new channel named after a reporter?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 22:53 IST
Reading options

"A stretch of icy water in Antarctica is now called the Amos Channel, after BBC science reporter Jonathan Amos. The waterway only appeared when a chunk of ice connecting two islands broke apart."

Why does Antarctica have a new channel named after a reporter?

Have you ever wondered how places get their names? Mountains, rivers, islands — somebody has to decide what they're called. In Antarctica, that's a very serious job, and it just gave one person a very special honour.

A brand-new channel appears

In the west of Antarctica, two islands — Rothschild Island and Alexander Island — used to be joined by a thick bridge of ice. When that ice broke apart, a stretch of seawater opened up between them for the first time. That's a brand-new geographic feature. And every new feature on Earth needs a name.

Why a reporter?

The committee that names Antarctic places picked a very unexpected name: the Amos Channel. It's named after Jonathan Amos, a science reporter who spent 38 years at BBC News telling the world about Antarctica.

Amos reported on colossal icebergs, penguin colonies, and even the discovery of explorer Ernest Shackleton's lost ship, the Endurance. But here's the funny part: he has never actually visited the continent himself — he wrote about it all from his desk!

"An extraordinary accolade"

When Amos heard the news, he said it was "absolutely incredible — what an extraordinary accolade". He admitted that having a place on the map is "quite the incentive to go and visit" — even after covering Antarctica for nearly four decades.

The British Antarctic Survey said the honour fits his "outstanding contribution to the promotion of Antarctic research". From now on, researchers and map-makers in that part of the continent will use the name Amos Channel on all their charts.

Try this at home

Look at a map or an atlas and find a place near you with an unusual name — a river, a mountain, or a street. Do a quick search to find out who or what it was named after. Sometimes the story behind a name is stranger (or funnier) than you'd expect!

for-grownups

Antarctic place-names are decided by official committees under strict rules. The UK Antarctic Place Names Committee recommended this name, which applies to sites in the British Antarctic Territory as well as South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands. The Amos Channel only became a real, stable feature when the ice bridge connecting Rothschild and Alexander Islands disintegrated, letting seawater flow between them. Jonathan Amos reported on Antarctic science for 38 years at BBC News but has never travelled there himself.

For grown-ups

"Antarctic place-names are decided by official naming committees under strict rules. The Amos Channel was recommended by the UK Antarctic Place Names Committee, which oversees names for sites in the British Antarctic Territory as well as South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands. Geographic features can generally only be named once they are confirmed to exist — this channel became a real, stable feature when the ice bridge connecting Rothschild Island and Alexander Island disintegrated, letting seawater flow between them. Jonathan Amos spent 38 years at BBC News (joining in 1986) covering polar science, including colossal icebergs, penguin colonies, and the discovery of Ernest Shackleton's lost ship Endurance; he has never personally travelled to Antarctica. Named in recognition of his 'exceptional and influential role in bringing Antarctic research to the forefront of public understanding.'"
