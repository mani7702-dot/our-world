"Who Was the First Homo erectus? A 2-Million-Year-Old Skull Hints at a Mystery"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at "11:47" IST
Reading options

"A 2-million-year-old adult skull from South Africa's Cradle of Humankind rewrites when our human ancestor Homo erectus first appeared — and reveals how it lived alongside a very different rival."

Who Was the First Homo erectus? A 2-Million-Year-Old Skull Hints at a Mystery

1. A skull that rewrote the timeline

Two million years ago, in what is now South Africa, a young visitor to our evolutionary family tree took its first breath. We know this thanks to a tiny but mighty fossil — an adult Homo erectus skull fragment found in a place called the Cradle of Humankind, a UNESCO World Heritage site famous for hiding some of the world's oldest human relatives under layers of rock and time.

This new fossil is a game-changer: it makes Homo erectus — the first human ancestor to walk fully upright and leave Africa — about 200,000 years older than scientists previously knew. Before this discovery, the oldest adult Homo erectus remains came from a dusty site in Georgia, dating back around 1.8 million years. Now, the South African skull pushes our family's story deeper into the past.

2. Two cousins sharing one neighbourhood

Here's the twist: Homo erectus wasn't alone. Another human relative, Paranthropus robustus — a heavily built cousin with a tiny brain but enormous teeth — also lived in the same valley at the same time.

It was like a real-life tale of two strategies. Homo erectus was growing a bigger brain and smaller molars, probably because its group was learning to eat more varied foods, including meat. Meanwhile, Paranthropus kept a brain no bigger than a chimpanzee's but evolved teeth and jaw muscles so powerful they worked like a built-in mortar and pestle for grinding tough plants.

3. Who was winning? A 10-to-1 surprise

When archaeologists dug through the dirt of Drimolen Cave, something astonishing emerged: for every single Homo erectus fossil, there were more than ten Paranthropus fossils. That means our own ancestor was the lonely underdog — the long-shot — while its bulky-toothed cousin was the neighbourhood heavyweight champion.

Yet history took a very different path. While Paranthropus thrived at the time, it was the slender-brained Homo erectus lineage that eventually led to us. Sometimes, being rare doesn't mean you're doomed — it just means you were carrying the future in your tiny human-sized skull.

4. Catching evolution in slow motion

The Drimolen fossils don't just tell us when our ancestors appeared — they show us how they changed. By comparing Paranthropus teeth from 2 million years ago with those from 1.8 million years ago, researchers caught evolution happening before their eyes: the molars grew steadily larger, and so did the muscles that crushed food.

It's one of the clearest snapshots of evolution in action that scientists have ever found — proof that the human story is written one small tooth, one fossil, one mystery at a time.

Try this at home

Build a fossil detective kit! You don't need a cave — just a tray, some sand or flour, and small toys. Bury a few items (a penny, a tiny toy dinosaur, a button) at different depths. Then use a kitchen sieve or colander to "excavate" them layer by layer, brushing gently with a soft paintbrush. As you uncover each object, ask: which layer was it in? What does that tell you about whether it's "older" or "younger" than the others? Real archaeologists do the same thing — and the Drimolen team found their skull fragment buried in ancient sediment that told them it was 2 million years old.

Fun Fact

The adult skull found at Drimolen was given the code name DNH 127. Before it was discovered, the only other adult Homo erectus skull from the site was a juvenile named DNH 134 — meaning scientists had to guess what the grown-up version looked like. Now, thanks to DNH 127, they can see that the adult had a braincase that was rounder than the child's, confirming the youngster would have grown up to look just like its kind.

For grown-ups

"Drimolen Main Quarry in South Africa's Cradle of Humankind has yielded an adult Homo erectus fossil (DNH 127) dated to about 2 million years ago, roughly 200,000 years older than the earliest known adult H. erectus remains from Dmanisi, Georgia. The study, published in Annals of Human Biology by researchers from La Trobe University and international colleagues, assigned 18 newly described fossils to both Homo erectus and Paranthropus robustus. The adult H. erectus skull fragment joins the original juvenile specimen DNH 134, confirming that our genus Homo first appeared in southern Africa around 2 million years ago. While H. erectus was developing a larger brain and smaller molar teeth — possibly linked to a shift toward eating more meat — P. robustus retained a chimp-sized brain but evolved enormous molars suited to grinding tough vegetation, functioning like a living mortar and pestle. The two species shared the same landscape, yet P. robustus outnumbered H. erectus at Drimolen by more than 10 to one, making our ancestor the underdog at the time. The fossils also document gradual evolutionary change in P. robustus over a 200,000-year window, with teeth and chewing muscles growing progressively larger. The P. robustus population from Drimolen (2 million years ago) was distinct enough from the later Swartkrans samples (1.8 million years ago) to earn the separate subspecies designation P. robustus ukusa. This research adds a crucial early chapter to the human family tree, showing that the story of our origins in Africa was crowded with competing experiments in how to be human."
