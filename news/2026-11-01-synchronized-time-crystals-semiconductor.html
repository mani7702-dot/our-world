"Why Do Separate 'Time Crystals' Suddenly Tick Together?"

By Our World Published 01 November 2026 at 12:30 IST
Reading options

Imagine two pendulum clocks hanging on the same wall. If you start them swinging at slightly different speeds, something magical happens: within a few minutes, they end up ticking in perfect step. A scientist named Christiaan Huygens noticed this back in 1665, and it has puzzled people ever since.

Now, more than 350 years later, physicists have found a tiny, high-tech version of the very same trick — hiding inside a piece of crystal.

What is a time crystal?

A normal crystal, like a diamond or a snowflake, has a pattern that repeats in space. A time crystal is stranger: its pattern repeats in time. Its insides keep ticking back and forth in a steady rhythm, all on their own, without anyone pushing it again and again.

In January 2024, scientists at TU Dortmund University in Germany built a continuous time crystal inside a semiconductor — a material used in computer chips. Its rhythm stayed steady for hours.

Many crystals, one heartbeat

In their new study, the same team made something even more surprising. They found that many time crystals can appear inside a single piece of material — and when they shine a wide laser beam across them, the separate crystals lock together and start ticking at the same speed.

The trick works through the movement of tiny spinning particles called electron spins drifting between regions. The crystals even synchronized when they were 40 micrometres apart — more than a thousand times wider than a single crystal! Move them farther apart, though, and the beat breaks: each one ticks on its own again.

Why it matters

Being able to make tiny oscillators work together could one day help build new kinds of spin-based technology — devices that use the spin of particles instead of electric currents. The team says this is a first step toward controllable networks of these tiny ticking clocks.

Fun Fact

The word "crystal" usually makes us think of something sparkly and frozen. A time crystal isn't frozen at all — it is a crystal that moves, repeating its pattern in time rather than in space. It is a brand-new kind of order that scientists only started creating in the 2010s!

Try this at home

Hang two spoons from a single piece of string or a thin ruler, so they dangle side by side. Give one a gentle push and watch what happens over the next minute. Do they start swaying together? That shared wobble is the same idea as Huygens' clocks — and the same idea the scientists used to get their tiny time crystals ticking in unison.

For grown-ups

"Physicists at TU Dortmund University (Prof. Alex Greilich and colleagues) report in Nature Communications (DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-75714-1, 22 July 2026) that multiple continuous time crystals (CTCs) can form within a single gallium-arsenide semiconductor lightly doped with indium and silicon, and synchronize their coupled electron–nuclear spin oscillations. At temperatures near −270 °C, each localized electron interacts with roughly one million nuclear spins; a pump laser aligns electron spins, which transfer polarization to the nuclei, and feedback sustains the oscillation. Broad-beam illumination couples spatially separated CTCs through the diffusion of spin-polarized electrons, locking them to a common frequency even when they are up to 40 µm apart — more than a thousand times the size of a single oscillator. The effect is analogous to Christiaan Huygens' 1665 observation of two pendulum clocks synchronizing through a shared support. The work points toward controllable networks of spin oscillators for future spin-based technologies. Source: ScienceDaily / EurekAlert / Nature Communications; corroborated by Phys.org and SciTechDaily."
