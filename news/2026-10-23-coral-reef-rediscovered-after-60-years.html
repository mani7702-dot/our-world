"A coral reef hidden for 60 years was thought dead — so what did scientists find when they looked again?"

A coral reef hidden for 60 years was thought dead — so what did scientists find when they looked again?

A reef written off as dead

Back in the 1960s, scientists surveyed a deep-water area off the coast of Benin, in the Gulf of Guinea. They were looking for productive fishing grounds, but what they found on the seafloor looked like a coral reef that had given up its living.

After their survey, they wrote it off — probably dead.

More than 60 years later, a team led by Gérard Zinzindohoué from the Institut de Recherches Halieutiques et Océanologiques du Bénin wondered: what if they were wrong?

Hunting ghosts with sonar

The team returned with modern tools that did not exist in the 1960s — high-resolution sonar and a Deep Sea Camera System. They scanned 11.5 kilometers of seafloor, searching for the old reef's signature.

"It was not an easy field campaign," Zinzindohoué recalled. "Long days at sea, technical problems, and a lot of uncertainty."

Then the sonar showed something that looked like a reef. And when they lowered the camera?

"There it was — real, in front of us."

A hidden world of corals and fish

The cameras revealed eight different types of coral clinging to rocky patches more than 50 meters below the surface. Around them swam eight species of fish, including golden African snappers, blackbar soldierfish, and three-banded butterflyfish.

Unlike shallow coral reefs that form one big wall, this reef is made of separate coral patches scattered across the seabed — what scientists call a mesophotic coral ecosystem. These "low-light" reefs live where sunlight is dim, far below the usual sunlit zones where most reefs grow.

"This is the first confirmed record of a living mesophotic coral ecosystem on the Gulf of Guinea continental shelf," Zinzindohoué said. "If we were able to find this reef again after 60 years, how many others are still undocumented?"

Try this at home

Make a "deep reef" in a jar. Fill a clear jar most of the way with water, then add a few drops of blue food coloring. Drop in small pom-poms or paper bits to represent coral colonies. Shine a flashlight from the side (not straight down) to simulate how little light reaches the deep sea. Notice how the "corals" cluster in certain spots — just like the patchy reefs scientists found off Benin!

Fun Fact

Mesophotic reefs live between 30 and 150 meters deep. In some places like American Samoa, they contain up to 80% of all coral species — even though most people have never heard of them!
