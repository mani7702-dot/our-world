How NASA's new helicopter blades could fly 25x farther on Mars

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 22:14 IST
Reading options

NASA's Ingenuity helicopter proved flight on Mars was possible. Now a new blade design could let the next generation of Mars helicopters fly 18.7 kilometers in a single trip — 25 times farther than Ingenuity ever managed.

Most airplanes need thick wings and powerful engines. But on Mars, the rules are completely different — and NASA is redesigning helicopter blades from scratch.

The helicopter that flew on another planet

In April 2021, a tiny helicopter named Ingenuity made history as the first aircraft to achieve powered, controlled flight on another world. It was never supposed to last that long — designed for just five test flights — but it ended up flying 72 times over nearly three years, covering more than 17 kilometers of Martian sky before its mission ended in 2024.

Each of Ingenuity's flights was short: at most about seven-tenths of a kilometer. That is roughly the length of a city park — a modest distance even for a toy drone on Earth, let alone a helicopter in an atmosphere 100 times thinner than ours.

Why Mars makes flying so hard

Flying on Mars is like trying to hover inside a balloon that has been nearly emptied of air. The atmosphere is so thin that rotor blades have to spin incredibly fast — about 240 revolutions per minute — just to generate enough lift. On Earth, a typical helicopter rotor spins at around 100–150 RPM and has plenty of air to push against.

That thin air also means helicopters can only carry tiny science instruments, and they can only travel short distances before running low on battery power. Ingenuity managed just 10 to 15 minutes of flight time per charge — barely enough to scout ahead for the Perseverance rover.

The blade that could go 25x farther

Now, NASA engineers think they can change that. A research team at NASA's Ames Research Center — working with Imperial College London and Nagoya University — has designed a new rotor-blade shape called roamx-0201.

Instead of the rounded, bulbous cross-section used on Ingenuity, the new blade is thin and sharp — more like a knife blade than a traditional airfoil. The shape was picked by computer simulations tuned specifically to Martian conditions, then tested in a wind tunnel back on Earth.

The trick is what happens at the edge of the blade. As air flows over a rotor, it creates a thin layer called the boundary layer. The new airfoil is designed so that turbulence in this layer actually helps generate lift — rather than hurting performance like it does on Earth-style blades. The thinner profile also delays a problem called drag divergence, where rotor tips approach the speed of sound (which is lower on Mars) and suddenly lose lift.

In simulations, the roamx-0201 blade lets a Mars helicopter fly as far as 18.7 kilometers in a single trip — more than 25 times Ingenuity's best — while carrying scientific instruments.

The next generation: SkyFall

That longer range matters because NASA's next Mars helicopter mission, SkyFall (launching in 2028), plans to send a trio of next-generation helicopters to scout the Red Planet's surface. With longer legs on each flight, these aircraft could cover far more ground, carry heavier scientific payloads, and act as true aerial scouts for future human explorers.

It is a fitting tribute to Ingenuity — the scrappy little helicopter that proved flight on Mars was possible and now inspires the designs that will make it routine.

Try this at home: Hold a piece of paper horizontally and blow over the top. The paper lifts — that is Bernoulli's principle, the same force helicopter blades use to create lift. Now imagine trying the same thing in a room that has almost no air — that is what flying on Mars is like.

Fun fact: Mars' speed of sound is about 10 times slower than on Earth (roughly 260 m/s vs. 340 m/s), which is why drag divergence hits Mars helicopters so much earlier. The thin atmosphere actually helps delay it too — but only if the blades are shaped right.
