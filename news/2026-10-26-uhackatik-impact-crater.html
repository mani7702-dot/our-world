"How did an amateur astronomer's camping trip uncover a 390-million-year-old impact crater in Quebec?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 22:48 IST
Reading options

"An amateur astronomer searching satellite maps for a camping spot in Quebec noticed a suspiciously round circle on the landscape. What he found was Uhackatik — a 390-million-year-old impact crater larger than 25 kilometres across, confirmed by mysterious shatter cones that only form when a space rock slams into Earth."

How did an amateur astronomer's camping trip uncover a 390-million-year-old impact crater in Quebec?

A circle in the satellite view

In 2024, Joël Lapointe was planning a camping trip to Quebec's remote Côte-Nord region when he spotted something odd on online satellite maps. Near Lake Marsal, the landscape formed a near-perfect circle — too round to be ordinary. He wondered: could this be a meteorite impact crater?

Most people would have kept that curiosity to themselves. But Lapointe reached out to scientists, and his hunch started a real investigation.

A hard trek into the wilderness

Planetary geologist Gordon Osinski from Western University organized an expedition in October 2025. Getting there was an adventure all on its own. The team flew in by floatplane, but the aircraft could not land all the way at the shore — they had to wade through 50 metres of water before reaching land.

Once on shore, the landscape was brutal: rugged, swampy, full of insects, and tangled in "deep, twisty, gnarly vegetation." Even a geologist with years of fieldwork across six continents said it ranked among the most difficult places he had ever worked.

But something promising was already there. The rocks around the circle were breccia — broken, jumbled stone that earlier geologists had interpreted as evidence of a volcanic pipe.

The shatter cones that told the real story

On the expedition's second day, the team found something decisive: shatter cones.

Shatter cones are distinctive curved grooves in rock that form only under the crushing pressure of a hypervelocity impact. They look a bit like the ridges on a seashell, and they fan out in rows from the impact point. According to Wikipedia, they are "the only unequivocal evidence of an impact event that you can see in the field with the naked eye."

With shatter cones in hand, the mystery was solved. This was not a volcano — it was an impact crater, 25 kilometres (16 miles) across, formed about 390 million years ago. The crater has a raised rim, a central uplift, and towering cliffs showing columnar jointing — all hallmarks of a complex impact structure.

The researchers named it Uhackatik, following consultation with the local Innu Council of Ekuanitshit.

Why most craters are hidden

Earth is older and more active than the Moon or Mars, so most of our impact scars have been erased. Rain, wind, ice, and the slow crawl of tectonic plates gradually wear them down over millions of years. That is why scientists have confirmed only about 200 impact structures on Earth — and why each new discovery is so exciting.

A comparable impact happening today would cause "regional devastation on a scale that would wipe out major cities and have global climate impacts." Fortunately for hikers, the Uhackatik impact happened 390 million years ago — long before humans walked the Earth.

Try this at home

Fill a shallow baking pan with flour. Sprinkle a thin layer of cocoa powder on top. Now drop a marble from about 30 centimetres above the surface. Watch the cocoa dust explode outward and form a perfect circle with a raised rim — just like a tiny impact crater! Try different marble sizes and drop heights to see how the crater shape changes.

Fun Fact

Uhackatik is the largest impact crater discovered since 2018, when scientists found the 31-kilometre-wide Hiawatha structure in Greenland. It ranks among the 11 known impact structures in Quebec alone.

for-grownups: The Uhackatik impact structure (release ID 260921081101) was discovered by amateur astronomer Joël Lapointe in 2024 while he was planning a camping trip near Lake Marsal, Quebec. A field expedition led by Gordon Osinski (Western University) in October 2025 confirmed the impact origin through the discovery of shock metamorphism features — specifically shatter cones — in breccia outcrops. The crater is ~25 km in diameter and dates to the Middle Devonian period (~390 million years ago), based on radiometric analysis of impact melt and surrounding rock units. This places it roughly 100 million years before the end-Ordovician impact spike documented in the literature. With its discovery, Uhackatik becomes the largest confirmed impact structure since the Hiawatha crater was identified in 2018 (31 km diameter). Impact craters on Earth are rare and well-preserved examples are scarce due to erosion, tectonics, and volcanism — only ~200 confirmed structures exist globally. Shatter cones form under shock pressures of 2–30 GPa and are the definitive field indicator of hypervelocity impact.
