Will a Leap Hour Replace the Leap Second?

Question: What happens when the clocks that run our world drift away from the turning of the Earth?

At the end of October 2026, the leap second — that extra tick added every few years to keep clocks in step with the sky — officially became history. Someday, we may need to add a whole leap hour to catch up.

Why We Count Seconds Differently Than Sunrises

There are two ways to tell time, and they slowly disagree.

The first way is atomic time, the kind inside every phone and computer. It is built on the super-steady heartbeat of caesium atoms — exactly 9,192,631,770 wiggles of a caesium 133 atom make one second. These clocks are so perfect they would not lose a single second in 15 billion years. That is older than our whole universe!

The second way is tied to our spinning Earth. One full spin equals one day — the Sun rising and setting. But Earth is not a perfect clock. Big earthquakes, melting ice sheets, and the Moon's gentle tug all gently slow our spin, adding about 2.3 milliseconds of drift per day every hundred years.

For decades, experts added a leap second whenever the two clocks drifted too far apart. Since 1972, they inserted 27 leap seconds — the last one on 31 December 2016. The next leap second will never come.

A Vote in Versailles

In early October 2026, delegates at the 28th General Conference on Weights and Measures in Versailles, France, voted to end the leap-second practice. The new rule lets atomic time and Earth-time drift apart by up to a full hour (3,600 seconds) instead of forcing a sync every few years. The change becomes official on 20 May 2027.

Why bother? Leap seconds cause real headaches for computers. A single extra second once tripped up LinkedIn, Reddit, and even Qantas airline systems. By letting time drift naturally, engineers remove that surprise entirely.

— "With the new arrangement, time will be strictly uniform, so the time of an event can be reliably predicted years in advance," explains space tracker David Herald. "The issue of the Earth's irregular rotation is dealt with where it belongs: the Earth itself."

Looking Up at a Drifting Sky

For most of us, nothing changes overnight. The shift is tiny — so small that over a whole human lifetime it adds up to less than a minute. The Sun will still cross the sky at roughly noon.

But for astronomers and space travelers, the change matters. Long trips to the Moon and Mars already run on their own clocks. NASA's NICER instrument aboard the International Space Station watches pulsing neutron stars that could one day guide spacecraft deep into the solar system — where Earth's wobbles matter far less than they once did.

Try This at Home

Grab a stopwatch or phone timer. Count how many times you can blink in exactly ten seconds. Now imagine a clock so steady it would take longer than the age of everything to gain ten seconds. That is the kind of heartbeat keeping time for our world.

Fun fact: before atomic clocks, the second was based on the Earth's spin. The first "atomic" second in 1967 had to be exactly 9,192,631,770 wiggles of caesium — a number chosen so the new second matched the old one almost perfectly.

for-grownups: Atomic time (TAI/TT) already differs from Earth-based UTC by 37 seconds thanks to past leap seconds. The 2026 CGPM resolution lets that gap grow up to one hour. Earth orientation parameters (EOP) will still be published for astronomy and satellite work — the sky will never lose track of where it is, even if our clocks do.
