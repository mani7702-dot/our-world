"How does growing plants in space help us care for Earth?"

By Our World Published 25 October 2026 at 21:40 IST
Reading options

"NASA astronaut Luke Delaney is busy tending to plants aboard the International Space Station, testing how microgravity changes the way life grows — and what that tells us about tending our own planet from up above."

What does a space botanist do on the ISS?

Imagine tending a garden while floating two hundred miles above Earth, where every seed, leaf, and drop of water drifts as if underwater. That is exactly what NASA astronaut Luke Delaney does aboard the International Space Station, and he is not alone — every day in orbit, plants are helping scientists unlock secrets that reach all the way back down to our blue planet.

Why grow plants where there is no up or down?

On Earth, roots always grow downward and stems reach upward. That invisible direction is called gravitropism — a built-in GPS that tells plants which way is down. In space, that GPS stops working, so plants must figure out a new way to grow.

NASA scientists have been asking: without gravity's pull, how do plants decide which way is up? By studying petri dishes and growth modules in the ISS laboratory, researchers watch how plants re-orient themselves, how their cells change shape, and how their genes switch on and off. It turns out gravity is not just about falling — it sends signals that plants use to build themselves properly.

The ISS as a floating laboratory

Aboard the station, plants grow inside special pillows of soil under LED lights, with gentle pumps and cameras recording every inch of growth. Since the ISS began hosting plant experiments, crews have grown lettuce, radishes, zinnias, and even moss that survived the vacuum of space itself. Each experiment teaches us how life adapts — knowledge that could help astronauts grow fresh food on a three-year trip to Mars.

But the gifts keep coming back to Earth. As plants grow without gravity's tug, scientists learn how to make crops stronger, water them more efficiently, and protect them from stress — improvements we can bring home to farms facing drought and extreme weather. The space garden is, in a sense, Earth's garden teaching us new tricks.

Try this at home

Plant a seed in a clear cup with a damp paper towel and watch how the root always bends downward — that tiny tug is Earth's gravity guiding it. Try it upside down: tape a sprouting seed to the inside of a jar lid and hang it so it grows in a circle. Notice how it still finds its way. Look at the night sky and find the ISS — on a clear evening you can see a slow, steady "star" gliding across, carrying its floating garden high above.

Fun Fact

The ISS travels at about 28,000 kilometers per hour, which means it orbits Earth 15 and a half times every single day — so those plants complete a sunrise and sunset every 90 minutes, living through far more days in a week than we do on Earth!

For grown-ups

"NASA astronaut Luke Delaney, a member of the SpaceX Crew-13 mission launched to the International Space Station (ISS), is conducting botanical research using petri dishes and plant-growth experiment modules inside the station's Destiny laboratory module. The experiments investigate the genetic responses of plants grown in microgravity compared with Earth-grown controls, building on decades of ISS plant research. Since 2014, NASA's Veggie (Vegetable Production System) facility and the Advanced Plant Habitat have enabled hundreds of plant growth studies in orbit, including lettuce, radishes, and zinnias. Growing plants in space addresses two critical needs for long-duration missions: producing fresh food for astronauts and maintaining atmospheric air quality (plants absorb CO2 and release oxygen), while also revealing how gravity shapes root development, gene expression, and plant structure. The research directly supports NASA's Artemis program goals of sustainable lunar habitation and eventual human missions to Mars, where growing food will be essential. The ISS itself is an international collaboration involving NASA, Roscosmos, ESA, JAXA, and CSA, orbiting 418 km above Earth at roughly 28,000 km/h."
