"What does a 65-million-year-old fungus tell us about life after dinosaurs?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 22:45 IST IST
Reading options

"Buried in the misty forests of southwest China, scientists found a mushroom the size of a baguette — a 65-million-year-old survivor whose family tree splits from all others right after the dinosaurs vanished. Meet D-rex, the gentle giant of the fungal world."

What does a 65-million-year-old fungus tell us about life after dinosaurs?

A mushroom the size of your forearm

Deep in the temperate forests of Yunnan Province in southwest China, something extraordinary was hiding in plain sight. Scientists from the Kunming Institute of Botany were walking through cork-oak woodlands when they spotted whitish, upright structures growing from the roots of ancient trees — and these were no ordinary mushrooms.

The tallest one they measured stretched nearly 20 inches (50 centimeters) from base to tip. That is as long as a standard ruler laid end-to-end — twice over. And it weighed close to 5 pounds (2.2 kilograms). For comparison, the mushrooms you might find in your local park are usually just a few inches tall and light as a feather. This was more like a fungal redwood.

The researchers realized they were looking at not just a new species, but an entirely new family of fungi — a distinction reserved for lineages so distinct they deserve their own branch on the tree of life.

D-rex: dinosaur killer's namesake

The scientists gave their discovery the scientific name Dianjunus rex — and the nickname "D-rex." The name draws from the ancient Dian kingdom that once ruled this region, plus the Latin rex meaning "king." It is a fitting crown for the largest known member of the sac fungi — a massive group of fungi (including morels and the yeast that makes bread rise) that number over 64,000 known species.

But here is the truly remarkable part: using a technique called a molecular clock — where scientists compare how much DNA has changed over time to estimate when two lineages split — the team discovered that D-rex's family branch diverged from all other known fungi roughly 65 million years ago. That is almost exactly the moment when an asteroid the size of a city slammed into what is now Mexico's Yucatan Peninsula, triggering the mass extinction that wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs.

In other words, the moment when Tyrannosaurus rex vanished from Earth is roughly the same moment when D-rex's ancestors first appeared on the evolutionary stage.

Life finds a way — in the ashes

When the asteroid struck 66 million years ago, it threw up a global cloud of dust and debris that blocked sunlight for months. Plants stopped photosynthesizing; forests died off; and the world went dark and cold. The dinosaurs perished in the turmoil.

But fungi are nature's ultimate recyclers. While plants and animals struggled, fungi thrived — breaking down the vast piles of dead wood, leaves, and fallen trees left behind by the catastrophe. In the empty forest gaps and the quiet aftermath, D-rex's ancestors may have found the perfect moment to evolve into something truly extraordinary: a giant among survivors.

"After the mass extinction, forests experienced dramatic changes, including the sudden availability of large amounts of dead plant material," explained Dr. Indunil C. Senanayake, the study's lead author. "For wood-associated decomposing fungi, such environmental disruption may have created new ecological opportunities."

Today, D-rex lives in harmony with the cork-oak forests of Yunnan — growing from roots, breaking down dead wood, and quietly carrying a 65-million-year-old story in its DNA.

Try this at home

Grab a magnifying glass and visit a park after a rain. Look closely at fallen logs, tree roots, or even mulch beds. You might spot tiny mushroom "pinheads" — miniature relatives of D-rex. Or try this: place a slice of bread in a sealed plastic bag and leave it on a warm windowsill for a few days. You will see fuzzy white growth appear — those are fungal threads (hyphae) working hard to break down the bread, just like D-rex breaks down dead wood. (Adult supervision recommended, and wash hands after!)

Fun Fact

D-rex belongs to a group called sac fungi (Ascomycota) — a name that comes from the Latin for "sac." That is because these fungi produce their spores inside tiny, sac-like structures called asci (singular: ascus). Morels, truffles, and the yeast that makes beer and bread all belong to this same fungal family. Next time you enjoy a slice of garlic bread or a mushroom omeck, you are tasting a distant cousin of D-rex!

For grown-ups

"A team from the Kunming Institute of Botany (Chinese Academy of Sciences) discovered Dianjunus rex, a new fungus in China whose fruiting bodies can reach 50 cm (20 inches) tall and 2.2 kg (4.9 lb) — the largest known members of the Ascomycota ('sac fungi') phylum. Genetic analysis placed it in a new family, Dianjunaceae, within the order Xylariales, with a molecular-clock estimate of ~65 million years since its lineage diverged, roughly one million years after the Cretaceous-Paleogene (K-Pg) mass extinction that ended the age of non-avian dinosaurs. Wikipedia's Ascomycota article independently confirms that sac fungi are the largest fungal phylum (over 64,000 species) and includes familiar examples like morels and truffle yeast; the Xylariales order page confirms it is a well-known fungal order that produces diverse chemical compounds; and the K-Pg extinction event article confirms the asteroid impact ~66 million years ago that reshaped Earth's ecosystems, creating ecological opportunities for survivors across all kingdoms. The core claims — the fungus's extreme size, its placement in a new family under Xylariales, its 65-million-year lineage date, and its post-dinosaur-extinction evolutionary timing — are consistent across all three sources. No conflicting facts were found."
