Why Do Some Metals Crack in Extreme Cold — And How Did Two New Alloys Beat It?

By Our World Published at 14:20 IST
Reading options

Have you ever left a rubber band outside on a freezing day and watched it snap? Now imagine that happening to the metal wings of a plane or the tank holding super-cold fuel. Engineers have worried about exactly this for a hundred years. But a new study found two metals that simply refuse to crack — even at the coldest temperatures scientists can test.

The cold, hard problem

Most metals get stronger when you cool them down — but some also get brittle, meaning they would rather snap than bend. That's a big deal for a family of metals called body-centred cubic metals, which includes iron, tungsten and chromium. At room temperature a dent just pushes the atoms around. In extreme cold, a tiny crack can race through the metal and break it apart.

Inside a metal, bending happens because of tiny line-like flaws called dislocations. Think of them as moving wrinkles that let layers of atoms slide past each other. When it gets very cold, those wrinkles get stuck — and a stuck metal cracks.

Two alloys that found a new route

The team — led by the University of California, Berkeley, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the University of Oxford — tested two experimental alloys made from niobium and titanium. They cooled them all the way to 20 kelvin (that's about −253 °C, or −424 °F, close to the coldest anything can get). Ordinary metals would shatter. These didn't.

Instead, the alloys switched to a completely different trick. Tiny regions of their crystals rearranged into mirror-image patterns — a process called nanoscale twinning — and part of the crystal even changed its shape. Where these new patterns met the boundaries between crystal grains, they created fresh spots of stress, which grew brand-new dislocations. The metal was suddenly full of new wrinkles ready to bend.

The scientists compare it to a road network: when the usual roads freeze over, new routes open up so traffic can keep moving.

How they tested it

The researchers used a huge machine called Engin-X at the ISIS Neutron and Muon Source in Oxfordshire, UK. They chilled the metal samples with helium gas, fired a beam of neutrons through them, and stretched them from both ends to measure how far they could go before breaking. Then powerful electron microscopes revealed the tiny changes happening inside.

Fun Fact

The coldest natural place we know of is deep space, around 2.7 kelvin — only about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero. That's why spacecraft metals need to stay tough in the cold!

Try this at home

Ask a grown-up for two chocolate bars and put one in the fridge for an hour, leaving the other at room temperature. Bend both. The cold one snaps with a sharp crack, while the warm one bends and smears — that's the same brittle-versus-ductile difference materials scientists are trying to beat.

Scientists hope this discovery will lead to new metals for energy systems, transport and aerospace — parts that must survive scorching heat and freezing cold in the very same machine.
