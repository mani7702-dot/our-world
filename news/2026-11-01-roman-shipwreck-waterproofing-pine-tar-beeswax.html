"How Did Ancient Roman Sailors Keep Their Ships Waterproof?"

By Our World Published 2026-11-01T09:40:00+05:30 at 09:40 IST
Reading options

Imagine sailing across a cold, salty sea in a wooden boat, with waves slapping the hull all day. Water, salt, tiny microbes, and wood-eating worms all attack the wood. So how did ancient Roman shipbuilders keep a wooden ship floating for years and years? A new study of a 2,200-year-old shipwreck just revealed their clever recipe.

A Ship That Refused to Give Up Its Secrets

The wreck is called Ilovik-Paržine 1. It sank about 2,200 years ago near the coast of what is now Croatia. Divers found it in 2016 lying under just 4 metres (13 feet) of water. It had cracked apart and slid under rocks and mud, so it blended into the seafloor and stayed hidden. That same burial is exactly what saved it: without oxygen, the microbes and shipworms that normally eat old wood could not destroy it.

The Secret Coating: Pine Tar and Beeswax

For as long as people have built boats, they have painted the hull with sticky coatings to keep the water out. Scientists in France and Croatia studied 10 samples of the coating from the wreck. Using a technique called mass spectrometry, which can spot unknown substances inside a mixture, they found that nearly every sample was made of pine tar, also called pitch, a thick dark resin boiled out of pine wood.

One sample held a special blend: pine tar mixed with beeswax. Greek shipbuilders knew this mixture as zopissa. Adding wax made the coating bend instead of cracking, and it was easier to paint on while hot.

Pollen: A Tiny Time Capsule

Pine tar is so sticky that it traps pollen from nearby plants and holds it for thousands of years. By studying that trapped pollen, the team could tell where different layers of coating were made. They found pollen from holly oak, pine, olive, hazel, alder, ash, and even fir and beech trees from nearby mountains.

The samples also showed the ship was recoated four to five times, with three separate batches at the bow. That means the same ship was repaired again and again in different ports across the Mediterranean. Earlier clues suggest it was built in Brundisium, today the city of Brindisi in Italy.

Try This at Home

Ask a grown-up to help you test waterproofing. Coat one piece of cardboard with a little cooking oil or beeswax (with supervision) and leave another piece plain. Sprinkle water on both and see which one beads up and repels the water. That is the same idea the Romans used, just with pine pitch!

Fun Fact

The Roman writer Pliny the Elder, writing about 2,000 years ago, actually described zopissa as pitch that was scraped off old ships, mixed with salt water and wax, and reused. Ancient sailors were recycling long before recycling was a word.

This piece covers a 2026 study in the journal Frontiers in Materials. Researchers analysed 10 coating samples from the Ilovik-Paržine 1 shipwreck (found in 2016 off Croatia, about 2,200 years old) using mass spectrometry and pollen analysis. They found hull coatings made of pine tar (pitch) and a pine-tar-and-beeswax blend the Greeks called zopissa, plus pollen that revealed where repairs likely happened, from Italy to the north-eastern Adriatic.
