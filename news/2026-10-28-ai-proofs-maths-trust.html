"Can AI Solve Maths Problems Faster Than Humans — and Should We Trust It?"

By Our World Published 28 October 2026 at 20:30 IST IST
Reading options

"Artificial intelligence can now solve famous maths problems in record time, but mathematicians worry that speed without understanding could shake the very foundations of scientific trust."

Can AI Solve Maths Problems Faster Than Humans — and Should We Trust It?

Imagine asking a computer to solve a puzzle that has stumped the world's best mathematicians for decades — and it comes back with the answer in record time. That is becoming reality as artificial intelligence steps into the world of mathematics. But just because a machine finds the answer fast, does that mean we should celebrate? Or should we pause and ask: what did the AI actually do?

When machines beat the clock

In recent months, AI systems have started solving famous maths problems that took humans years to even understand. One example grabbed headlines when OpenAI claimed its AI agents had worked out a solution to the Navier–Stokes equations — a set of rules that describe how fluids like water and air move. Solving this problem is worth a US$1 million prize, and OpenAI said its AI cracked it in just 88 hours.

That sounds amazing. After all, if machines can do the maths, what else can they do? But here is where things get interesting. Two mathematicians — Tristan Buckmaster from New York University and Levent Alpöge from Anthropic — raised an important question: where did the AI learn this solution from? They worry that AI tools might pick up ideas from other researchers' unpublished work without giving credit — a bit like copying homework, even if no one meant to.

A growing worry: proof indigestion

Fields Medallist Terence Tao — one of the world's greatest living mathematicians — recently wrote in Nature magazine that we may be entering a new era he calls "Maths 2.0". In this world, AI can create hundreds of new proofs, but human mathematicians might struggle to understand even a fraction of them. He calls this problem "proof indigestion" — when results pile up faster than anyone can check.

Think of it like being given a finished puzzle with 1,000 pieces but no picture on the box. You know the pieces fit, but you are not sure what the final image should look like. That is how some mathematicians feel today. AI produces answers quickly, but the reasoning behind them can be so tangled that even experts need months to work through it.

But AI is not all bad news. Dr Oliver Vince from Basecamp Research uses AI to study DNA — the building blocks of life. Just two small boxes of genetic material may hold more information than all the text on the internet, and AI helps scientists read and understand it. When used carefully, AI can be a brilliant lab partner.

Trust in the balance

Here is the big worry: if AI keeps solving problems faster than humans can understand, how do we know which results are correct? Scientists rely on peer review — when other experts read and check each other's work. But if every paper comes with a 1,000-page proof that nobody can digest, the whole system starts to break down.

Worse still, AI sometimes makes up facts — just like a chatbot that confidently describes a movie that does not exist. If this happens in science, researchers might chase leads based on fake results, wasting time and money. That could mean fewer real discoveries — the kind that lead to new medicines, cleaner energy, or better technology.

Try this at home

Want to test your own problem-solving skills? Try a puzzle: draw a shape that can cover a tennis ball without overlapping or gaps. Now ask an adult whether they think a computer could solve it faster — and whether that would matter. The answer might surprise you!

Fun Fact

The most famous maths problem ever is probably Fermat's Last Theorem. For over 350 years, nobody could prove it — until mathematician Andrew Wiles finally did in 1994. His proof was so long and complex that it took him seven years to write, and the first version had to be fixed!
