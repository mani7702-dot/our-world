"This 'New' Frog Was Hiding in Plain Sight for 100 Years"

This 'New' Frog Was Hiding in Plain Sight for 100 Years

A century of mistaken identity

Deep in the rainforests of the Ecuadorian Chocó — one of the wettest places on Earth — there is a small brown frog whose call sounds like a tiny click. For more than 100 years, scientists thought this frog was something it was not.

The frog, now officially named Pristimantis milpe (the Milpe Robber Frog), was hiding in plain sight. It's common, widespread, and easy to spot at night — but nobody realized it was its own species until 2026.

The detective work at the Natural History Museum

The mystery was solved by a team led by Dr. Santiago Ron from the Pontificia Universidad Católica del Ecuador. To figure out what the frog really was, he traveled to London to study the Natural History Museum — home to over 80 million specimens.

There, among the 19th-century frog skins pinned and preserved decades ago, he found the original "type" specimens — the very frogs that scientists had used to describe this group. By comparing those historical specimens with living frogs in Ecuador today, the team realized something surprising:

The old specimens had been mistakenly linked to a different species.

"It's amazing that such a common frog went unnamed for so long," says Dr. Ron. "It just shows how much we still have to learn about the life around us."

Where does the Milpe Robber Frog live?

The Milpe Robber Frog lives in the Chocó forests of western Ecuador and southern Colombia — part of the Tumbes-Chocó-Magdalena region. This lush, rainy strip along the Pacific coast stretches about 1,500 kilometers (930 miles) from Panama to Peru and is one of the most biodiverse places on the planet.

The frog is found from sea level to 1,200 meters (3,940 feet) up the mountainsides, across more than 67,000 square kilometers of forest. Males call loudly from leaves on shrubs and bromeliads, several meters above the forest floor.

How to tell a male from a female

The Milpe Robber Frog has a clever disguise trick: males and females look different!

  • Males tend to have more uniform coloring
  • Females have striking orange, light blue, or black spots around their groin and thighs — like nature's own secret code

This kind of color difference between the sexes is common in the Pristimantis family, which includes over 626 described species — making it the frog genus with the most species on Earth.

Try this at home

  • Go on a night walk: If you ever visit a rainforest in Ecuador or Colombia, grab a flashlight after dark. Frogs are easiest to spot when they're calling! Listen for clicks and chirps coming from the trees.
  • Visit a museum: Natural history museums are full of undiscovered secrets. Those 19th-century specimens at the Natural History Museum in London helped scientists today solve a 100-year-old mystery!
  • Build a bug hotel: Frogs love eating insects, so creating a small shelter in your garden helps local frogs and toads keep pest populations balanced.

Fun Fact

The Pristimantis genus gets its name from Pristimantis, a figure from ancient Greek history and myth — a minor king and companion of Alexander the Great! Today, over 600 living frog species carry this name, making it the most diverse genus of frog — and the most diverse genus of any vertebrate — on the entire planet.

for-grownups: Why museum collections matter

This discovery shows that natural history collections are still irreplaceable for modern science. The 19th-century specimens at the Natural History Museum in London (NHM) — collected before DNA testing, before modern taxonomy software, before satellite maps — are still the gold standard for identifying species today.

When researchers combine century-old specimens with modern field observations and genetic data, they can resolve classification mysteries that puzzled scientists for decades. The Milpe Robber Frog's story reminds us that biodiversity discovery is far from over — even in the 21st century, common animals can still surprise us.

For grown-ups

"Pristimantis is the most species-rich vertebrate genus on Earth, with over 626 described frog species as of 2026. These 'robber frogs' (family Strabomantidae) are found from southern California to northern Argentina. Pristimantis milpe, the new Milpe Robber Frog, was discovered when researchers re-examined 19th-century type specimens at the Natural History Museum in London. The species had been misidentified as P. subsigillatus for over a century. The discovery shows that even common, widespread species can go unrecognized, highlighting the importance of natural history collections. The frog lives in the Tumbes-Chocó-Magdalena biodiversity hotspot — a 1,500-km-long Pacific coastal region from Panama to Peru that is one of the world's wettest and most biodiverse areas."
