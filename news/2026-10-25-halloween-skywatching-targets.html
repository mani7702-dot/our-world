"What are the five spooky Halloween skywatching targets this October?"

By Our World Published 25 October 2026 at 21:02 IST
Reading options

"Ready for a spooky stargazing adventure? Five Halloween skywatching targets — the Pleiades, the Demon Star, the Sea Monster, lunar spiderwebs, and Halloween fireballs — are glowing in October's night sky."

1 — What five spooky skywatching targets can you see this Halloween?

As October's nights grow longer, the stars seem to tell ghost stories. Five Halloween-themed skywatching targets are glowing in the October sky right now, and you can see them with nothing more than your eyes or a pair of binoculars. From a "demon star" that blinks like Medusa's eye to fireballs that streak across the darkness like shooting ghosts, these spooky sights are perfect for a Halloween night outdoors. Here is your guide to five things that go bump in the night sky.

2 — Why does the Pleiades star cluster feel so spooky?

Look east after midnight and you will spot a tiny dipper of six or seven bright stars. This is the Pleiades — an open star cluster of more than 1,000 stars that sits about 444 light-years away. Ancient Celtic druids held their spookiest celebration, Samhain, when the Pleiades climbed highest at midnight. They believed the veil between the living and the dead grew thin at this time, letting spirits wander the earth. With binoculars you can sweep up dozens of the cluster's hidden stars — proof that even in daylight, the night keeps its secrets.

3 — What makes the "Demon Star" Algol blink like an eye?

High in the eastern sky hangs Algol, the "Demon Star" in the constellation Perseus. To ancient sky-watchers, Algol was the eye of Medusa — the Gorgon whose gaze turned monsters to stone. Every 2.87 days Algol dims to about one-third of its normal brightness as an unseen companion star slides in front of it, creating a gentle eclipse that looks like the star winking at us. Wikipedia confirms Algol is widely known as the "ghost and demon star" and even the "first star of Medusa's head." In Greek myth, Perseus used that very head to turn the sea monster Cetus to stone — which brings us to our next target.

4 — Where is the Sea Monster hiding in the sky?

Following the mythology, look for Cetus (the whale or sea monster) low in the southeast after sunset. In legend, Cetus terrorized the coast of Aethiopia until Perseus, wielding Medusa's petrifying stare, slew the beast and turned it to stone. The constellation's brightest patch, the Cetus star group, glitters just to the right of the Pleiades. It is a reminder that the same stories written in our myths are written in the stars overhead.

5 — Why do lunar "spiderwebs" appear around the full moon?

Around the full moon on October 26, scan the bright lunar disk with binoculars. You will see lace-like patterns — "spiderwebs" — radiating from the young craters Tycho, Copernicus, and Kepler. These are not the webs of moon-dwelling spiders, but streaks of bright material cast across the surface by the violent impacts that carved those craters. When the Sun is directly behind Earth, its light fills the craters from behind, lighting the rays so they stand out like silver threads on a dark cloth.

6 — How do you catch a Halloween "fireball"?

Your last spooky target takes patience: the Taurid fireballs. As Space.com explains, these bright, slow-moving meteors appear to stream away from a patch of sky near the Pleiades in the constellation Taurus (hence the name). Wikipedia confirms the Taurid meteor shower runs annually from late September through early December, peaking in late October to early November. Unlike the swift flash of the December meteors, Taurid fireballs move slowly and often blaze green or orange — perfect, glowing ghosts for a Halloween night.

Try this at home

Make a "demon star" blink! Shine a flashlight through a small hole cut in a piece of paper, then swivel a second, smaller paper baffle back and forth in front of the beam. When the baffle blocks the light, the "star" dims — just like Algol being eclipsed every few days. You can even time your own 3-day cycle by marking a calendar, echoing the rhythm astronomers use to study binary stars across the galaxy.

Fun Fact

The Pleiades cluster is so famous that more than 50 cultures have their own names and stories for it. The Japanese call it Subaru, which is why the car logo shows six bright stars — the six sister stars of the Pleiades that are visible to the naked eye.
