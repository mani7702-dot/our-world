Did plant-eating dinosaurs snack on seaweed from the beach?

By Our World Published 15 November 2026 at "19:00" IST
Reading options

"Scientists found a chemical clue inside fossil teeth that suggests coastal dinosaurs - plant-eaters and meat-eaters alike - were getting part of their dinner from the sea, most likely seaweed washed up on the shore."

Imagine a wide, flat coastline about 100 million years ago, in the middle of the age of dinosaurs. A river carries bits of plants into the sea. Storms throw seaweed up onto the beach in slimy heaps. And then something surprising: a big plant-eating dinosaur wanders down and eats some of it.

That is the picture a new study is suggesting. Nobody found a dinosaur with seaweed in its mouth. Instead, the evidence was hiding inside fossil teeth.

1. A chemical fingerprint in the enamel

Teeth and bones are made of minerals, and the atoms locked inside them carry a record of what an animal ate. One of those atoms is a heavy form of carbon called carbon-13.

Plants from the sea, like seaweed, hold a little more carbon-13 than most plants on land. So if an animal eats a lot of seaweed, its teeth end up with slightly more carbon-13 too.

Researchers led by geologist Clayton Forster at the University of Arkansas ground tiny samples of tooth enamel from fossils of dinosaurs, crocodiles, turtles and fish. Some came from places that were once coastline. Others came from places that were dry land far from the sea.

The coastal fossils were different: they were richer in carbon-13 - up to about 5.5 parts per thousand more than you would expect from an animal eating ordinary land plants. The inland fossils were not.

2. It was not just one lucky dinosaur

The surprising part is how consistent the pattern was. Coastal sites from different latitudes and different slices of time all showed the same boost.

And the signal turned up in both plant-eaters and meat-eaters. That told the team something important: the sea-flavoured carbon must have entered the food chain near the bottom. A predator does not eat seaweed, but it eats the animals that do, and the carbon travels up with every bite.

The best fit for something eaten by plant-eaters, low on the food chain, and unusually rich in carbon-13? Seaweed and other marine plants.

"It's likely that most of the sampled herbivorous dinosaurs were no different" from modern coastal plant-eaters that nibble seaweed, Forster said.

3. Why would a dinosaur eat seaweed?

For the same reason a deer or a sheep on a modern shore might: sometimes the land is not offering much.

On today's coasts, storms wash marine plants and animals ashore, and land animals eat them - especially when the land is dry and food is scarce. Scientists call this marine subsidization: the sea quietly topping up the land's food supply.

The study is the first time this has been spotted in a prehistoric ecosystem. It also means some older estimates of dinosaur diets need a small correction, because those calculations assumed all the carbon in a dinosaur's teeth came from land plants.

4. What we still do not know

Be careful with the word "ate". This is chemical evidence, not a fossilised meal. Nobody has found a dinosaur stomach full of seaweed, and the study cannot say how much of the diet came from the sea - a snack, not a whole menu.

What it does show is that coastal food webs 100 million years ago were tangled in the same way ours are: land and sea trading food back and forth.

Try this at home: be a beach detective

Next time you are near a shore - or even a lake after a windy day - look at the line of stuff the water pushed up.

  1. Find the washed-up seaweed or water plants. Where did they come from?
  2. Look for small animals nearby: crabs, flies, birds, snails. Are any of them eating the pile?
  3. Draw the food chain you found: sea plant, then the animal that eats it, then whatever eats that animal.

That is exactly the chain the scientists traced through fossil teeth - just a lot faster.

For grown-ups

"Forster, Suarez, Cullen, Zanno & Hyland (Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution, published 30 September 2026, DOI 10.3389/fevo.2026.1895247) measured stable carbon isotopes in tooth enamel of Cretaceous vertebrates from coastal and upland formations in North America, including the Antlers and Woodbine formations and the Mussentuchit Member. Enamel from coastal assemblages is enriched in carbon-13 by up to about 5.5 per mille above the values expected for animals eating ordinary land plants, and the enrichment is consistent across coastal sites regardless of latitude or age; the landlocked/upland assemblage does not show it. Because macroalgae and marine plants use a modified C3 photosynthetic pathway that raises their carbon-13 content, and because the signal appears in both herbivores and carnivores, the authors argue the carbon entered the food chain low down - as seaweed or similar marine plant matter eaten by herbivores, then passed to predators. This is indirect geochemical evidence, not stomach contents or bite marks: no fossil gut contents show seaweed being swallowed. The authors also note that modern large coastal herbivores routinely supplement their diets with seaweed, and that the finding matters for earlier back-calculations of dinosaur diet that assumed only land plants."
