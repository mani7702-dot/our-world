A Third Kind of Magnetism? Physicists Find Hidden Magnetic State Called Altermagnetism

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 23:10 IST
Reading options

For more than 100 years, physicists thought magnets came in just two flavors — ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic. Now a new magnetic state called "altermagnetism" joins the list, and its discoverers have just won Europe's top condensed-matter prize.

If magnets had flavors, for more than a century we thought there were only two: the kind that sticks to your fridge (ferromagnet), and the kind where all the tiny magnets point in opposite directions and cancel out (antiferromagnet). Now physicists have quietly discovered a third flavor — and it behaves a bit like each of the other two at once.

Scientists call it altermagnetism. It is a magnetic state that, like an antiferromagnet, has no overall "north" or "south" pull, yet — unlike one — its electrons still act like they are magnetized all the way through. That strange mix could one day power computers that are faster, smaller, and use far less energy.

The fridge test and its secret twin

Every ferromagnet (the fridge kind) has magnetic "atoms" that mostly point the same way, giving the material a real magnetic pull. In an antiferromagnet, neighboring atoms point opposite ways, so the pulls cancel — the material feels non-magnetic, even under a microscope.

Altermagnets are the sneaky middle child: their atoms also cancel out, so the material has no net magnetism — but their electrons carry spin in a patterned way that produces effects usually seen only in ferromagnets. The result is a material that behaves magnetically without announcing it.

The prize for a hidden idea

Three physicists shared the 2026 Europhysics Prize, one of Europe's most prestigious honors in condensed matter physics, for work that turned this hidden state into a recognized branch of physics. The prize went to Professor Jairo Sinova of Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz, Dr. Libor Šmejkal, and Professor Tomas Jungwirth.

The trail started with theory: by combining math about crystal symmetries with the study of how electron "spin" flows through devices (spintronics), the team realized that a whole class of magnetic materials had been sitting in plain sight, mislabeled. Their 2022 symmetry classification identified hundreds of candidate materials. Experimental labs soon confirmed the predictions, first with semiconducting manganese telluride (MnTe), and the field has since exploded into one of the hottest corners of modern physics.

"It shows that even the most familiar topics in physics can still surprise us," said Sinova.

Why a third kind of magnetism matters

Beyond adding to the textbook list, altermagnetism may be a bridge. It has been linked to other deep ideas in modern physics — including topological phenomena (odd states of matter tied to crystal shape) and unconventional superconductivity (materials that carry electricity with zero resistance). If those connections deepen, altermagnets could become a hub that ties together several "next big thing" areas of physics.

For everyday technology, the win is simpler: because altermagnets show strong spin behavior without producing a stray magnetic field, they are ideal for spintronic devices — chips that use electron spin instead of electric charge to store and move information. That could mean computers that stay cooler, phones that last longer, and sensors sensitive enough to spot the tiniest magnetic signals.

Seeing the invisible magnetism

The first clear experimental images of altermagnetic order came in 2024 from researchers at the University of Nottingham. Using nitrogen-vacancy microscopy (a way to "see" magnetic fields with a tiny diamond sensor) and X-ray techniques, they directly visualized the alternating spin pattern in a crystal — proving the effect was real and not just a theoretical curiosity.

Where the magic happens

Altermagnetism was predicted and observed mostly in crystal structures where magnetic atoms are arranged by rotation or mirror symmetry, rather than just by pointing the same or opposite way. Materials such as manganese telluride show the clearest signs, and scientists are racing to test others. Unlike a fridge magnet you can hold, an altermagnet announces itself only to instruments that read spin — but that quiet behavior may be exactly what tomorrow's ultrafast electronics need.

Try this at home: Hold a regular magnet against the needle of a compass. Now imagine a material that barely affects the compass (no stray field) but still steers tiny electron spins inside its crystals. That is the opposite-of-ordinary magnetism that altermagnets offer — invisible tug, magnetic talent.

For the grown-ups

Altermagnetism is a collinear magnetic order with zero net magnetization (like an antiferromagnet) but a wavevector-dependent, spin-split electronic band structure (like a ferromagnet), first classified by symmetry in 2022 by Sinova, Šmejkal, and Jungwirth and confirmed experimentally in semiconducting MnTe by 2024. The 2026 Europhysics Prize, awarded by the European Physical Society's Condensed Matter Division, cited their establishment of altermagnetism as a fundamental category of magnetic order alongside ferromagnetism and antiferromagnetism. Connections have been reported to topological band theory, unconventional (e.g., cuprate and iron-based) superconductivity, and spin-orbit physics; the same groups have highlighted spintronic applications (efficient spin-charge interconversion, low-power logic). Direct experimental imaging of altermagnetic order was reported by the University of Nottingham group using nitrogen-vacancy center microscopy and X-ray magnetic linear dichroism (XMLD).
