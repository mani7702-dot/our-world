"Why did crocodiles survive the dinosaur-killing asteroid?"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 07:34 IST
Reading options

"When an asteroid slammed into Earth 66 million years ago and wiped out the dinosaurs, crocodiles lived on. Scientists now explain how these ancient reptiles found safe havens in warm zones and moved to survive."

Why did crocodiles survive the dinosaur-killing asteroid?

The day the sky went dark

About 66 million years ago, a giant asteroid the size of a small city slammed into what is now Mexico. The impact was so fierce it threw dust and ash high into the sky, blocking the Sun for months. It became so dark and quiet that plants stopped growing, and the dinosaurs — the biggest animals on Earth — slowly starved.

Most dinosaurs died out. But crocodiles survived.

How did these scaly reptiles make it through one of the worst disasters in Earth's history? Scientists from University College London have a new answer: crocodiles simply followed the warmth.

An ancient map of warm and cold

The research team studied over 7,000 fossils and records from more than 400 living and extinct crocodile species. They built computer models that rewound Earth's climate and geography across 245 million years.

The picture that emerged is striking: crocodile relatives have always been climate chasers. During hot periods, they spread far and wide — even all the way to Antarctica! When the planet cooled, they pulled back toward the tropics, hugging warm coastlines and rivers.

A safe spot near the equator

When the asteroid struck, scientists think sunlight dropped by 5% to 10% and global temperatures plummeted. But the models showed something important: warm zones near the equator stayed just warm enough for crocodiles to survive.

Dinosaurs, on the other hand, needed enormous amounts of food every day. When plants stopped growing, they ran out of meals. Crocodiles — being cold-blooded — needed far less food. They could bask, wait, and survive on whatever prey was left.

"The asteroid did not make the whole planet equally hostile to every group," said Dr. Alfio Alessandro Chiarenza, the study's lead author. "Crocodiles' long-standing preference for warm environments may have placed survivors exactly where suitable climates held up best — like around the equator. These refuges could have sheltered populations that later recovered."

Not your average swamp creature

Crocodiles are sometimes called "living fossils," but their ancestors were anything but ordinary. The broader crocodile family once included:

  • Sarcosuchus — a 12-meter-long predator nicknamed the "super crocodile"
  • Postosuchus — a land-dwelling hunter that walked on two or four legs

Even today, crocodiles keep one ancient superpower: they are ectothermic, meaning they do not need to burn food to stay warm. A single large meal can keep a crocodile going for months. That is a handy trick when the Sun does not shine.

Imagine you are a crocodile trying to stay warm. Draw a simple map and color in the hot spots (near the equator) green and the cold spots (near the poles) blue. Now trace a path that always stays in the green — that is exactly what crocodiles did to survive!

Fun Fact

Crocodiles have survived two of Earth's five major mass extinctions. The secret? Always follow the warmth.

For grown-ups

"An international team led by University College London studied over 7,000 fossils from 400 living and extinct crocodile species, combining evidence with computer climate models spanning 245 million years. Published in Science Advances, the research shows that crocodile relatives spent most of their evolutionary history following warm climates. After the Chicxulub asteroid impact 66 million years ago, regions near the equator remained warm enough to support crocodile populations even as environments for dinosaurs deteriorated. The asteroid caused wildfires, tsunamis, and a 'impact winter' that blocked sunlight for months, halting photosynthesis. Non-avian dinosaurs, many weighing over 25 kg, could not survive. But crocodiles, being ectothermic (cold-blooded) with modest energy needs, small body sizes, semi-aquatic habits, and flexible diets, had advantages. Their tropical preference placed them exactly where warm refuges persisted. The study also reveals crocodile relatives were once far more diverse — including Sarcosuchus (the 12-meter 'super croc') and Postosuchus (a land-hunting predator) — showing the lineage has repeatedly weathered environmental upheaval. Wikipedia confirms the Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction killed ~75% of species including all non-avian dinosaurs, while ectothermic species like crocodilians survived; crocodiles are cold-sensitive tropical reptiles."
