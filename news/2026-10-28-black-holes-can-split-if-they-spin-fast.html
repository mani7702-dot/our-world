Can Black Holes Split in Two? Only If They Spin at Almost Top Speed

The surprising idea

When two black holes crash together, they merge into one — a heavier black hole that rings like a bell, shaking space and time with ripples called gravitational waves. That much is well-tested science: LIGO and Virgo have seen dozens of these mergers. Now, new research suggests the opposite might also be possible: a single black hole might be able to split in two. But there is a catch — the original black hole must be spinning at nearly its maximum possible speed. That extreme spin is what keeps the two halves from immediately falling back together.

Why splitting is normally impossible

The laws of physics fight back against a black hole splitting. The main obstacle comes from thermodynamics — the science of heat and disorder. Every black hole carries a measure of "disorder" called entropy. Thanks to the Bekenstein–Hawking formula, a black hole's entropy is directly tied to the area of its event horizon: the bigger the horizon, the more entropy. The second law of black hole thermodynamics says this area can never, on its own, become smaller.

Imagine a toddler's crayon box: when the crayons are neatly arranged, order is high and entropy is low. Scatter them, and entropy rises. To restore order, a parent must put energy in from outside. Similarly, a black hole splitting in two would shrink the total horizon area — a decrease in entropy that nature strongly resists. But there is a loophole for a rapidly rotating black hole.

The spinning loophole

A black hole that spins at almost its maximum rate behaves very differently from a static one. Rotation adds energy and twists space itself around the hole (a phenomenon called frame-dragging). The research team found that when these effects are included, the thermodynamics barrier relaxes just enough to open a tiny window where a split is allowed.

"The two resulting black holes must also be able to separate rather than immediately fall back together," team member Francesco Sannino of the University of Naples Federico II in Italy said in a statement. "When we include this requirement, the window largely closes again, except possibly for black holes rotating close to their maximum possible rate."

Black hole trousers?

The team calculated just how such a split could happen. They found that the division of the black hole's outer boundary — its event horizon — proceeds in an oddly trouser-like shape, with one resulting black hole slipping down each "leg." They termed this a pair-of-pants topology. A split is also more likely when one of the two resulting black holes is considerably smaller than its newly born twin.

"We cannot yet give a single probability for a black hole to split," Sannino said. "It depends on how gravity behaves beyond Einstein's theory, and we are working to quantify this." He added that the smallest primordial black holes — left over from the infant universe — are the likeliest candidates to split.

How we might catch one splitting

Because splits would favour the tiniest black holes, the signals would be faint but potentially detectable. Sannino noted that these splits may show up as flashes of high-energy light called gamma rays. Telescopes like Fermi-LAT, which watches the sky in gamma rays, could search for the telltale signals from the small black-hole fragments that might form during such a split.

The team's research was published on Oct. 22 in the journal Physical Review D.

try this at home

Take a balloon and stretch it — that's a single black hole horizon. Now spin it fast (a slow twirl is fine). In theory, at truly extreme spin, the horizon might "pinch" into two. You can't do that with a balloon, but you can model the idea: take a circle of soft clay on a turntable and spin it. At the edge, the clay may form two lobes — a playful nod to the pair-of-pants shape a splitting black hole might take. (Adult help for the turntable!)

Fun Fact

The mathematical shape a splitting black hole takes is called a pair-of-pants topology — so named because the event horizon briefly resembles a pair of trousers, with each leg becoming a new black hole. The bigger hole keeps most of the mass, while the smaller "twin" is much lighter, exactly as the researchers predicted would make splits more likely.

for-grownups: When two black holes merge, the final horizon area is always larger than the sum of the parts — consistent with the second law (Bekenstein–Hawking entropy S = A/4, where A is horizon area, cannot decrease). A single black hole splitting into two would decrease total horizon area and so is forbidden in ordinary situations. Sannino's team show the ban can be lifted for near-extremally rotating Kerr black holes, where spin energy and frame-dragging open a thermodynamic loophole; the horizon then bifurcates in a pair-of-pants topology, each "leg" forming a daughter black hole. Splits are more likely when one daughter is far smaller than the other, and primordial black holes are the best candidates. Any tiny leftover fragment would emit detectable gamma rays via Hawking radiation (searchable with Fermi-LAT). Published Oct 22, 2026 in Physical Review D.
