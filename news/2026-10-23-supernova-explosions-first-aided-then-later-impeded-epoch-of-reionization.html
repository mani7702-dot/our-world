"Supernovae helped light up the early universe — but not how scientists thought"

By Our World Published 23 October 2026 at 09:54 IST
Reading options

"For decades, astronomers assumed that supernova explosions were the main engines clearing paths for the ultraviolet light that ended the cosmic Dark Ages. New observations suggest young stars did most of the work first, before the first supernovae even went off."

Did Supernovae Light Up the Universe — or Did Young Stars Beat Them to It?

The Dark Ages that needed brightening

About 13.8 billion years ago, the entire universe was a fog. It was not empty — it was filled with a thin soup of neutral hydrogen atoms that scattered light in every direction, making space opaque. This eerie chapter is called the Dark Ages, and it began right after the Big Bang and lasted until somewhere between 150,000 years and 1 billion years after the beginning.

Then, something changed. The first stars and galaxies began to form, showering space with intense ultraviolet light. That radiation ionized — or split apart — the neutral hydrogen atoms scattered across the cosmos. When most of that hydrogen had been ionized, the universe went from foggy to clear, and the Dark Ages finally ended. Scientists call this pivotal moment the Epoch of Reionization, and it is often described as the universe's last major phase transition.

The surprising hero of reionization

For many years, astronomers thought supernovae were the heavy lifters of this story. A supernova is the dramatic explosion that happens when a massive star runs out of fuel and collapses, and it releases enough energy to carve bubbles in a galaxy's gas. The thinking went: those explosions blew channels through the gas so that ionizing radiation could escape into intergalactic space.

But new research challenges that picture. A study published in Nature Astronomy — titled "Lyman continuum escape during the onset of galactic winds in compact starbursts" — shows that young stars did the important work first. Using the Hubble Space Telescope, scientists led by Dr. Cody Carr (then at Zhejiang University in China, now at the University of Michigan) studied nearby galaxies that look very much like those in the early universe. They found that intense radiation and stellar winds from newborn star groups were already clearing escape routes before the first supernovae had a chance to explode.

So while supernovae do play a role, the study's co-senior author Renyue Cen explains that the real story starts even earlier: the light and winds from the youngest stars shape the surrounding gas first, controlling whether ionizing photons can break free. That means the galaxies that ended the Dark Ages were lighting up the cosmos in a more orderly, gentler way than simulations had assumed.

A cleaner view of our cosmic dawn

Cosmological computer simulations have long treated supernovae as central players in reionization. But as Dr. Carr notes, "we know it took place, but we don't have complete answers to those questions." The new findings suggest those models need updating — the earliest generations of stars may have done the heavy lifting on their own, clearing the way for the brilliant, transparent universe we see today.

With upcoming data from the James Webb Space Telescope and next-generation ground observatories, astronomers are closing in on the exact sequence of events that first lit up the darkness. One thing is certain: the universe's grand reopening after the Dark Ages was a stellar affair, powered by the very first suns and the galaxies they made.

Try this at home

Fill a clear glass with water — that is your early universe, cloudy with tiny particles. Now drop in a few drops of milk (the young stars) and stir gently. The milk scatters the light so you can barely see through. But if you wait, the particles begin to clump together and the water clears in patches — those are the channels carved by stellar winds letting light escape into space.

Fun Fact

The Epoch of Reionization is sometimes called the "universe's second infinity" because, after it ended, space became transparent for the first time since the Big Bang — letting light travel freely across the cosmos, just as it does today.

for-grownups: The Epoch of Reionization marks when the universe's neutral hydrogen was reionized by ultraviolet photons from the first stars and galaxies, ending the Dark Ages roughly 150,000 years to 1 billion years after the Big Bang (per Wikipedia).
