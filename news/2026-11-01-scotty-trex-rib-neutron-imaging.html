What Was Hiding Inside a T. rex's Broken Rib for 66 Million Years?

Meet Scotty — the largest Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton ever found. He lived in what is now Saskatchewan, Canada, about 66 million years ago. And one of his ribs was broken while he was alive.

Inside that broken rib, scientists have now found something they had almost never seen before in any fossil: fossilised blood vessels, still frozen in the middle of healing.

A fossil that kept a secret

When an animal dies, soft parts like skin, eyes and blood vessels are usually the first things to rot away. Normally only hard parts survive — bone, teeth, shells. So for a century, palaeontologists mostly studied dinosaurs by studying their bones.

Scotty's rib was a rare exception. After the rib cracked, iron-rich blood rushed into the injured spot, and new blood vessels grew to help rebuild the damaged bone. Scotty died before the break had fully healed, and his body settled into a salty marsh. That salty, low-oxygen mud worked like a natural refrigerator, slowing decay just enough to lock the tiny vessels in stone.

"It's like winning the lottery," said physicist Mauricio Barbi of the University of Regina in Canada. "Scotty's rib contains a vast network of mineralized blood vessels that has never before been observed in a fossil."

Seeing through solid rock — without breaking it

You cannot cut open a priceless fossil to look inside. So the team used a clever trick: neutron imaging.

Neutrons are tiny particles found in the centre of atoms. When a beam of them passes through an object, it reveals what is hidden inside — without harming the sample. The scientists fired neutrons at Scotty's rib at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory in the United States, building detailed 3D pictures of the vessels trapped within.

Neutrons are especially good at spotting light elements like hydrogen, which are common in soft tissue. X-rays, by contrast, are better at showing heavy elements like the calcium in bone. Using both is a bit like using an MRI scan to see muscles and an X-ray to see bones — each reveals a different part of the picture.

A detective story that took six years

The discovery did not happen overnight. Back in 2020, a then-undergraduate student named Jerit Mitchell first spotted signs of blood vessels inside the rib using X-ray scanning at the Canadian Light Source. Step by step, more scanning techniques filled in the details, until the neutron images confirmed the fragile network.

"There are more fossils than you think sitting in collections, hiding secrets from millions of years ago," Mitchell said. "Putting them in a synchrotron or neutron source allows us to make new discoveries about ancient life like never before."

Fun Fact: Neutron beams are usually used to study batteries, jet engines and other modern materials — this is one of the first times they have been used to read the "medical record" of a dinosaur.

Try this at home: You do not need a neutron beam to look inside things! Shine a torch against your hand in a dark room. Light passes through, glowing red — that is your blood. Thick bone blocks more light than thin skin. Imaging scientists use the same idea, just with cleverer "light" that can pass right through rock.

Quick quiz: Why did Scotty's rib keep its secret for so long? Because a salty marsh slowed decay — and because human beings had to invent neutron imaging to see inside without breaking the bone.

For grown-ups

"A study led by the University of Regina (Mauricio Barbi's group; Jerit Mitchell is the doctoral candidate leading the work) used neutron imaging at the U.S. Department of Energy's Oak Ridge National Laboratory — the MARS instrument at the High Flux Isotope Reactor and the VENUS instrument at the Spallation Neutron Source, both in April 2026 — to map a network of mineralized blood vessels inside a fractured rib of Scotty (RSM P2523.8), the largest known Tyrannosaurus rex. Earlier micro-CT and synchrotron X-ray work at the Canadian Light Source (from 2020) had first detected the soft tissue. The iron-rich blood and the salty marsh where the animal was buried slowed decay enough to preserve delicate structures that normally vanish before fossilization. Scientists stress it is a rare 'snapshot' of an injury that was still healing when the animal died — and a demonstration that neutron imaging, usually used for batteries and engineering materials, can reveal hidden biology inside fossils. Sources: ScienceDaily (22 Sep 2026), Oak Ridge National Laboratory, SciTechDaily, EurekAlert (Oak Ridge National Laboratory), and Wikipedia."
