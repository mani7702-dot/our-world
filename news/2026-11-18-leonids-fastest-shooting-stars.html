Why Are the Leonids the Fastest Shooting Stars of the Year?

By Our World Published 18 November 2026 at 18:15 IST
Reading options

Every November Earth ploughs through dust from Comet Tempel-Tuttle, and the grains hit our air at up to 71 km/s - the fastest shooting stars of the year.

Why Are the Leonids the Fastest Shooting Stars of the Year?

Every November, Earth sails through a river of dust left behind by a comet, and the tiny grains strike our atmosphere at almost 71 kilometres per second. Those are the Leonids - the quickest shooting stars on the calendar - and their peak arrives this week.

1. A Comet That Comes Back Every 33 Years

The Leonids come from Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, a ball of ice and dust that loops around the Sun once every 33 years. Each pass sheds a fresh ribbon of grit. Earth crosses that ribbon every November, between about November 6 and November 30, which is why the shower comes back on schedule year after year.

2. Why They Are So Fast

Most showers are quick. The Leonids are the quickest of all. The reason is a head-on meeting: Comet Tempel-Tuttle travels around the Sun in the opposite direction to Earth. Astronomers call that a retrograde orbit. So instead of chasing the dust, Earth slams into it almost face first. Earth's own orbital speed (about 30 km/s) and the dust's speed add together.

That is how the Leonids reach a blistering 71 km/s - the fastest of any annual shower. The Taurids of late October, by comparison, drift in at a gentle 28 km/s.

3. How to Watch the Peak

The Leonids seem to stream out of a point in Leo, the constellation shaped like a backwards question mark. In 2026 the planets Jupiter and Mars sit a few degrees apart in Leo, acting like signposts. Leo climbs highest in the hours before dawn, and this year the Moon sets before then, leaving darker skies. Look about 40 to 60 degrees away from Leo - a few hand-spans at arm's length - for the longest trails. No telescope needed: just a dark spot, a blanket, and about 20 minutes for your eyes to adjust. Expect a modest show - up to around 15 meteors an hour, though NASA gives a more cautious fewer than 3.

4. The Night the Sky Rained Meteors

Once in a while the Leonids go wild. In 1966, Earth crossed an especially dense trail and observers in the southwestern United States saw thousands of meteors a minute for about 15 minutes. That is a true meteor storm. The last big one was in 2002, and astronomers think another could come as early as 2031, with possibly spectacular displays predicted for 2033 and 2034.

Try this at home

Wait until after midnight, then find the darkest spot you can, away from streetlights. Lie back with your feet pointing east, look up at as much sky as possible, and put your phone away. Phone glow spoils your night vision. Count every meteor you see and see who spots the most by dawn.

For grown-ups

The Leonids' 71 km/s geocentric speed comes from Tempel-Tuttle's retrograde orbit: the dust meets Earth nearly head-on, so the relative speed is Earth's 30 km/s plus the stream's, not their difference. The comet's 33.3-year period lays down dense dust trails; Jupiter resonances bunch them up, and Earth crosses fresh trails in the years after perihelion (last in 1998) - that is when storms happen. Ordinary years: ~15 meteors an hour.
