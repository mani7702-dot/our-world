A magic fabric blanket that triples strawberry harvests — and keeps bugs away without chemicals

Imagine if you could wrap your garden in a sweater that keeps the bugs off, warms the soil just right, and lets the sun shine through — all while tripling your harvest. That is exactly what a group of scientists at North Carolina State University built, and it is already working in strawberry fields.

A blanket made for soldiers, now made for strawberries

The fabric has a surprising origin story. Researchers were first trying to make military uniforms that would keep mosquitoes away from soldiers wearing body armor in hot climates. The knitted, spongy material blocked insects but still let air move through — and somehow stayed cool.

Then, a eureka moment: what if this same fabric could protect crops instead?

The answer is Plant Armor — a clever 3-D-knitted textile that farmers simply drape over their strawberry plants like a cozy blanket.

The strawberry secret

In field tests inside growing tunnels, scientists covered some strawberry patches with Plant Armor and left others uncovered. After three full growing seasons, the covered plants produced up to 3.56 times more fruit than the bare ones.

That is more than triple the harvest — from a simple sheet of fabric.

Why does it work?

The fabric is full of tiny holes and air pockets — like a very fancy kitchen sieve, but designed by engineers. Here is what it does:

  • Keeps bugs away: Insects can't reach the berries through the 3-D mesh, so plants don't get eaten
  • Lets the sun through: Despite looking opaque, the fabric allows light to penetrate — crucial for fruit growth
  • Traps gentle warmth: The fabric acts like a light blanket, keeping the plants warmer and helping them ripen faster
  • Lets rain and air flow: Water and breeze pass right through

One of the researchers, Gabriel Olawuyi, explained the surprise: "We expected that Plant Armor's seemingly opaque fabric would lead to an increase in vegetative biomass at the expense of fruiting — because reduced sunlight would trigger a 'shade-avoidance' response, making the plant divert energy toward stem and leaf growth rather than fruit production."

But that is not what happened. The plants did not go into leaf-over-drive. They just grew more strawberries, faster.

Bugs, plants, and the balance of nature

What makes this story special for nature lovers is that it can reduce chemical pesticide use. By keeping insects out mechanically, farmers might need far fewer chemical sprays. That means:

  • Fewer pesticides washing into soil and waterways
  • Less harm to beneficial insects like bees and ladybugs
  • Healthier soil ecosystems

When fewer chemicals are sprayed, the whole web of life around the farm — from earthworms to birds to the insects that pollinate — gets a break.

Try this at home

You can try a mini version of Plant Armor with a common household item: a white nylon stocking or mesh produce bag. Drape it loosely over a potted plant (with adult help) and observe:

  1. Does the plant stay warmer on a cool morning? (Touch the leaves gently.)
  2. Can you still see light through the mesh?
  3. Are there fewer bugs on the leaves compared to an unprotected plant?

This is how real scientists start — by noticing a simple pattern and asking "what if?"

Fun fact

The name "Plant Armor" was inspired by the original military application: the same fabric technology that helped soldiers stay comfortable in body armor now helps plants grow stronger. One researcher joked: "First we made soldiers comfortable, then we made strawberries happy — both with the same fabric!"

For grown-ups

Plant Armor is a knitted 3-D porous textile cover developed by NC State University researchers, led by Gabriel Olawuyi with R. Michael Roe as senior author. The fabric was a spinoff of military mosquito-repellent uniform research — originally designed to make body armor more comfortable by blocking insects. Field trials in North Carolina strawberry tunnels found yields increased 3.56-fold (matching GNN's "up to 3.56 times"), because the fabric's 3-D structure deters insects without blocking sunlight, rain, or airflow. The warming effect helps plants reach fruiting stage earlier via accumulated growing degree-days. The paper "Knitted 3-D, Porous Textile Cover to Enhance Strawberry Fruit Production and Prevent Insect Feeding" was published in the journal Agriculture (NC State News, Sept 29 2026; confirmed by GNN Oct 16 2026). The fabric could reduce or eliminate chemical pesticide use in agriculture, benefiting soil insects, pollinators, and the broader ecosystem that depends on healthy crop landscapes. Patent held by NC State (US Patent); funded by North Carolina Agricultural Foundation grant AG00463770 and USDA NIFA HATCH project 02853.
