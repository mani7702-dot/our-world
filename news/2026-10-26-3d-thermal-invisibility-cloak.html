"How did scientists build a 3D cloak that hides things from heat vision?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 19:08 IST
Reading options

"Scientists have built the world's first 3D thermal invisibility cloak that can hide objects from infrared cameras while keeping them safe from extreme heat and cold."

How did scientists build a 3D cloak that hides things from heat vision?

Why hide something from heat?

Every object gives off heat — it radiates infrared energy that night-vision goggles and thermal cameras can "see." That is incredibly useful for firefighters, soldiers, and astronomers, but it also means that anything warm stands out like a spotlight in the dark. What if, instead of blocking heat or reflecting it, you could guide it around an object, like water flowing around a smooth rock in a stream? Then the object would be invisible to thermal cameras — and at the same time, shielded from temperature extremes.

That is exactly what a team of researchers has now done. They built the world's first three-dimensional thermal invisibility cloak, and it works in every direction.

How the cloak works

Previous thermal cloaks were flat — they could only hide things in two dimensions, or only when heat came from one direction. The new design, by scientists at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and the Technical University of Denmark, uses a clever lattice material printed in 3D.

Inside the lattice, some parts are made of aluminum (which conducts heat well) and others are filled with a rubber-like material. By adjusting the structure in three directions, the cloak creates pathways that bend heat around whatever is hidden inside. Heat flows smoothly past the object instead of getting stuck on it or building up around it.

From simulation to real device

The team did not just run computer simulations. Using 3D metal printing, they built a real aluminum lattice and filled the gaps with the rubbery material. Then they placed the cloak between hot and cold regions and watched with an infrared camera.

The result: the hidden object vanished from the thermal image. Temperatures inside stayed perfectly even, and the surrounding heat pattern looked completely normal — as if nothing was there at all. They even hid complex shapes that "resembled human heads," proving the cloak works on objects with intricate 3D geometry.

What could this be used for?

Imagine electronics that never overheat because heat is quietly steered away from delicate circuits. Or spacecraft components protected from the scorching heat of re-entry on one side while keeping instruments at a comfortable temperature inside. The same idea could even lead to new kinds of stealth technology.

The researchers say their lattice design is also compatible with "spherical harmonics," a mathematical tool that lets them create cloaks with record-breaking complexity — meaning future cloaks could fit almost any shape.

Fun Fact

The paper's abstract describes successfully hiding "an apple inside a pear" — the thermal cloak guides heat around the apple so thoroughly that even an infrared camera cannot detect it, while the pear-shaped outer shell remains at a perfectly steady temperature. The technique works not by absorbing heat, but by routing it around the hidden object, like an invisible hallway for warmth.

Try this at home

You can demonstrate the idea of "heat going around" with a simple experiment: place a metal coin in a bowl of warm water. Watch how the water swirls and flows around the coin instead of stopping at it. A thermal cloak does something similar — it creates a path for heat to move past an object without ever touching it.

For grown-ups

The original research was published in Nature Communications (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-73167-0) by Weichen Li, Yibo Wang, Ole Sigmund, and Xiaojia Shelly Zhang. The team used a 3D de-homogenization approach to engineer anisotropic thermal conductivity in a lattice composite, physically realized via 3D-printed aluminum and mold-cast rubber. The method integrates with spherical-harmonic design for arbitrarily complex 3D shapes, marking the first physical realization of omnidirectional 3D thermal cloaking from transformation thermotics theory.
