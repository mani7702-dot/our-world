"How Indian workers are helping robots learn to do human things"

By Our World Published 27 October 2026 at 00:45 IST
Reading options

"In cities across India, ordinary workers are wearing small head cameras to record everyday tasks — cutting hair, stitching clothes, sweeping floors — so that robots can learn how humans move. It's a new kind of job that pays a few extra dollars a day, but it also raises big questions about the future of work."

How Indian workers are helping robots learn to do human things

A new job: being filmed at work

Imagine wearing a small camera on your head while you work — just like a sports player wearing a helmet cam. That is exactly what some people in India are doing now, but instead of filming a football match, they are filming themselves doing everyday jobs like cutting hair, sewing clothes, and sweeping floors.

Their goal? To help teach robots how to do these same tasks.

Why can't robots just learn in a computer?

You might think that teaching a robot to fold laundry or pour a glass of water would be easy — just show it in a computer simulation, right? But real life is messy. A robot trained only on a computer often gets confused by the real world. Real fabric is crumpled, not flat. Real water spills a little. Real hands shake a tiny bit.

That is why companies working on artificial intelligence want real videos — thousands of hours of people actually doing things. The videos teach AI systems (the brains inside robots) what real movements look like, so robots can copy them later.

Who are the people filming their work?

The people doing this work come from all walks of life:

  • Mohammed Ashim, age 22, films himself cutting hair and trimming beards in Bengaluru. He says it feels "futuristic" and pays good money.
  • Armhan, a textile worker in Bangalore, films himself sewing garments. He says it is "simple" because he just gets paid a little more for doing work he already does.
  • Jolly Arifur, a science teacher in Guntakal, films herself doing household chores like scrubbing, cooking, and folding laundry. It gives her extra income on top of teaching.

These workers wear head-mounted cameras that only record their hands and movements — faces are blurred or kept out of frame. They get paid about $2 to $3 per hour, which is meaningful income in their communities.

When will robots replace these jobs?

Not any time soon, say experts. Right now, there are 96,000 data collectors across Latin America and Southeast Asia, and demand is only growing. The companies that buy this footage are building AI that could one day let robots help with housework, caregiving, or dangerous jobs like handling hazardous materials.

But some worry about what happens when this technology gets good enough to actually replace human workers. So far, the workers are recording the same jobs they already do — they are not losing income, just earning extra for letting a camera watch their hands.

Try this at home

Be a robot trainer. Put on a pair of gloves that are too big (or use oven mitts). Now try to do simple tasks: pour cereal from a box, button a shirt, or use scissors. Notice how hard it is to do things without your normal grip and dexterity. This is exactly the kind of challenge robot engineers are trying to solve — and now real people are helping by recording how their own hands do these things naturally.

Fun Fact

The footage doesn't just go to one company. Robotics labs in the United States and elsewhere buy the data to teach AI systems how to do everything from industrial assembly-line work to household chores. The goal is to eventually have robots that can help people at home — maybe folding laundry or cooking dinner — by learning from how real humans already do those tasks.

For grown-ups

"Hundreds of thousands of workers across India, Latin America, and Southeast Asia are hired by data-collection companies to wear head-mounted cameras while performing routine tasks. The footage is labeled and sold to AI/robotics companies to train 'physical AI' systems — machine-learning models that teach robots how to grasp, move, and interact in real-world environments. Workers earn $2–3 per hour, above local averages in some regions. The effort is driven by the realization that robots trained only in simulation fail in the messy real world, where forces, friction, and unpredictability differ. Wikipedia's articles on Machine Learning and Computer Vision confirm that AI systems learn patterns from large datasets of real-world examples, and that 'training data' is the fuel behind modern AI. The story raises questions about fair compensation, consent, and whether governments should regulate AI data collection. Companies like Humyn Labs and others are racing to build the world's first large-scale dataset of human physical activity."
