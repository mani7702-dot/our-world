"How You Squeeze Planetary Minerals Changes Everything"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 09:00 IST
Reading options

"Scientists have long assumed that the extreme pressures and temperatures deep inside planets make the direction you squeeze a crystal irrelevant. A new experiment using powerful lasers showed that is not true for magnesium oxide — the squashing direction changes whether the mineral stays solid or turns to liquid, which reshapes how we model planetary interiors."

Why does the direction of a squeeze matter inside a planet?

Deep beneath our feet — and certainly inside other worlds — the pressure and temperature are absolutely crushing. On Earth, the bottom of the mantle sits under pressures more than three million times the weight of this column of air on your desk, and temperatures that could melt rock. To understand how planets form and evolve, scientists need to know what happens to the minerals inside them under these extreme conditions.

One mineral matters more than almost any other: magnesium oxide, also known as the mineral periclase. Magnesium and oxygen are two of the most common elements in rocky planets, so magnesium oxide appears all the way down in the deep interior, near the boundary between the mantle and the core. If scientists get its behavior wrong, every computer model of a planet's inner life starts to drift off course.

For a long time, researchers assumed one comforting simplification: at such extreme pressures and temperatures, does it really matter which way you squeeze the crystal? Surely it all washes out, right?

Squeezing a crystal with lasers

A new study published in Physical Review X — led by scientists at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory and Johns Hopkins University — decided to test that idea directly. They placed tiny cubes of magnesium oxide (each just a few millimeters across) and hit them with powerful laser pulses from the Jupiter Laser Facility.

The lasers delivered an ultra-fast shock wave that compressed the crystal, reaching pressures up to 572 GPa (gigapascals) — that is roughly three million times the pressure at sea level on Earth's surface. The temperature climbed to about 13,000 Kelvin (12,700 degrees Celsius), which is hotter than Earth's core. In fact, it set a new experimental record for the pressure and temperature at which anyone has watched a mineral melt.

The researchers did something clever: they sent the shock in three different directions relative to the crystal's geometry — diagonally through a face, straight through the center of a face, and perpendicular to that. Then they watched what happened as the compression raced through the cube.

Direction changes everything

The answer surprised them. The orientation of the squeeze did matter, and dramatically.

When compressed along one path, the crystal transformed cleanly and predictably. But along the other two, the structure broke down into a messy, disordered growth pattern behind the shock front. Different directions gave different responses — the exact opposite of the long-held assumption that extreme conditions erase directional effects.

Even more striking, the team watched the magnesium oxide cube melt at 572 GPa and 13,000 K, marking a new laboratory record for the pressure and temperature of melting. Knowing exactly where and how a mineral turns from solid to liquid is crucial for modeling how heat moves through a planet's interior — and heat flow drives everything from magnetic fields to volcanic activity.

Why it matters for other worlds

Magnesium oxide does not just sit quietly at the bottom of Earth's mantle. On super-Earth exoplanets — rocky planets larger and more massive than ours — MgO may become a electrically conductive liquid in the mantle rather than staying solid. That conductivity is linked to the magnetic fields some of these worlds are thought to host, fields that can be much stronger than Earth's own.

In gas giants like Jupiter and Saturn, magnesium oxide can exist as either solid or liquid deep inside their cores, depending on the local temperature conditions.

Each of these questions depends on getting the melting behavior of MgO right — including how crystal direction influences what happens when the squeeze arrives.

Try this at home

You can explore the idea of directional effects with a simple model. Roll a ball across a bedsheet held tight at all four corners. Try shaking one corner sharply (that is like hitting the crystal from one direction) versus shaking two opposite corners at once (a different direction). Notice how the wave travels and reflects differently depending on where you push. The sheet's response is not the same from every angle — just like the shocked crystal.

Fun Fact

Magnesium oxide stays solid under pressures and temperatures that would vaporize almost anything else on Earth. That is why it is used in refractory bricks that line steel furnaces — it can take the heat and the pressure without giving way.

Grow-up corner

The 572 GPa / 13,000 K melting measurements refine equations of state used by planetary dynamicists, geodynamicists, and astrophysicists modeling super-Earth and gas-giant interiors. The three-direction compression geometry maps to distinct crystallographic orientations in the rock-salt lattice, revealing direction-dependent Hugoniot elastic precursor decay and disordering kinetics that standard isotropic models cannot capture. Future work using larger laser facilities will extrapolate these results to core conditions of super-Earths. Periclase (MgO) forms a ferropericlase solid solution with FeO and is a major lower-mantle phase alongside bridgemanite perovskite.
