How steam is cleaning strawberry soil without chemicals

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 05:40 IST
Reading options

A farm machine that pushes steam into the dirt is helping organic strawberry growers in California grow healthier crops — without the chemical fumigants that worry scientists.

Why strawberries are so delicate

A strawberry is one of the trickiest crops to grow. It grows low to the ground, its fruit is soft and juicy, and it sits right in the dirt where soil fungi and weeds love to attack. One disease called fusarium wilt can wipe out an entire harvest.

For a long time, farmers had only two choices: spray strong chemicals into the soil to kill the pests before planting, or simply gamble their whole crop every season. Those chemicals — called fumigants — work, but many scientists worry they harm farmworkers, wildlife, and the land around the fields.

Big machines, puffs of steam

Now researchers from the University of California, Santa Cruz have a clever new idea: clean the soil with steam.

Before planting, a big machine trundles across the field and pushes hot water vapor deep into the dirt. The heat is just enough to kill the harmful fungi, bacteria, and weed seeds hiding in the soil — but it does it without any chemicals at all.

Steam has been used in greenhouses for decades to keep plants healthy. The new trick is doing it outdoors, on huge open fields where strawberries grow.

Nearly double the berries

The scientists invited four organic strawberry farmers to try it. One of them, a longtime grower named Rod Koda, said the steam-treated plots were a revelation: bigger plants, healthier leaves, and roughly twice as many strawberries as his other fields.

The steamed soil also suffered far less fusarium wilt, the disease that usually scares strawberry farmers the most.

Still work to do

There's one big catch the researchers are honest about: steam costs money and takes time. The machines still need to be made faster and cheaper so they can treat big fields without breaking the bank. Scientists are also checking that the steam doesn't accidentally remove the helpful bacteria that plants need.

Try this at home: Strawberries are covered in tiny seeds on the outside — they're the only fruit in the world with seeds on their skin! Next time you eat one, count the little seeds. A single strawberry can have more than 200.

Fun fact: Chemicals aren't the only trick farmers are testing. Some researchers are also trying to grow strawberries that are naturally stronger against disease, so they need less help from any soil treatment at all.

For grown-ups

UC Santa Cruz researchers (and partners in the Biological and Integrated Farming System, or BIFS, project) are testing steam soil disinfestation as a scalable replacement for pre-plant soil fumigation in strawberries. Large machines inject high-temperature (~90-100°C) steam into planting beds, killing soil-borne pathogens (notably Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. fragariae, cause of fusarium wilt) and weed seed banks before planting. Organic growers cannot use chemical fumigants, so they formerly risked total crop loss or turned to hydroponics. In trials at four California farms, steam-treated beds on Rod Koda's farm produced roughly double the organic strawberry yield with less fusarium wilt damage, per both Good News Network and AP News. Steam has been standard in greenhouse horticulture for decades; the novelty is adapting it to open-field scale. Open challenges remain: cost per acre, machine throughput, and whether steam also removes beneficial soil microbes.
