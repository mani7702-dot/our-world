"Can a Magic Liquid Paint Stop Cavities Without Drilling?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 11:17 IST IST

Can a Magic Liquid Paint Stop Cavities Without Drilling?

The sugar bug that turns into a hole

Every day, billions of tiny bacteria throw a party on your teeth. They feed on leftover sugar — from your breakfast cereal, that afternoon snack, even the juice you sip — and spit out acid as waste. That acid slowly eats away at your tooth's outer layer, called enamel. Over time, this creates a hole called a cavity, or dental caries (say: KAR-eez).

Most kids will get at least one cavity before they turn 17. And for many children, the visit to fix it means drills, needles, and sometimes even being put to sleep for surgery. But what if there was a way to stop the sugar bugs right where they live — with a quick paint and no pain at all?

A liquid that stops decay in seconds

Scientists from the University of Michigan ran a large clinical trial — the kind doctors trust — to test a simple idea. Instead of scraping out the hole, they painted a few drops of a liquid called silver diamine fluoride (SDF) directly onto the cavity.

Here is how it works:

  1. The dentist dries the tooth and paints SDF onto the affected spot with a tiny sponge-tip applicator.
  2. The liquid sinks in and does two jobs at once: the silver kills the bad bacteria that are eating the tooth, and the fluoride helps the tooth rebuild its own natural mineral strength.
  3. The whole thing takes seconds — no drilling, no shots, no crying.

The study followed 830 children under age 6 across dental clinics and Head Start programs. When the treatment was reapplied every six months, it stopped decay from getting worse in more than half of the treated teeth.

Why this is a big deal for kids

Across America, more than 40% of children deal with tooth problems. Many never see a dentist early enough — some kids visit one for the very first time because they are in so much pain they end up in the hospital emergency room.

SDF changes that story. Because it needs no special equipment, no needles, and no drill, it can be given:

  • Right in a school or community health setting
  • By doctors and nurses, not just dentists
  • In under a minute

That means a child who has never set foot in a dentist's chair could still get help stopping a cavity before it becomes a serious infection.

The one catch? SDF turns the treated spot dark. That is purely cosmetic — it does not hurt or weaken the tooth. For many families, avoiding pain and expensive surgery is worth a small dark spot, especially on a baby tooth that will fall out anyway.

Try this at home

Ask a grown-up to help you test what SDF does — without any actual liquid! Grab two clear cups. Add water to both, then drop one crushed-up vitamin C tablet (to represent sugar) and one plain cracker. Leave them for a few minutes. The cup with the "sugar" gets cloudy — that is like acid attacking enamel. The plain cup stays clearer. The lesson? Sugar feeds the bugs that make cavities, and stopping that bug party early is the best defense.

Fun Fact

Silver diamine fluoride is not new — dentists in Japan have used it safely for over 80 years! The FDA cleared the 38% version for sale in the U.S. back in 2014. Now, with this major new study from the University of Michigan, doctors and dentists have the strong evidence they need to bring this simple, painless treatment to millions of children who struggle with dental visits.

for-grownups: The Phase III trial (NCT03649659), funded by the NIH's National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research, was conducted across Michigan, New York, and Iowa through dental clinics and Head Start programs. It enrolled 830 children under age 6, the population most affected by early childhood caries. The product tested was Advantage Arrest 38% SDF (Elevate Oral Care). Results, published in JAMA Pediatrics, confirm SDF arrests active carious lesions in over 50% of primary teeth with biannual application. The mechanism: silver ions (Ag+) provide antimicrobial action against Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus, while fluoride (F−) enhances remineralization of demineralized dentin. The treatment is on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines and is endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry. Side effects are limited to the cosmetic brown-black staining of arrested lesions. Wikipedia confirms SDF is a "colorless (or blue-tinted) odourless liquid" used for over 80 years in Japan and other countries, and that dental caries (tooth decay) is caused by acid-producing bacteria in dental plaque — affecting 3.6 billion people worldwide as of 2016 per the WHO.

For grown-ups

"A Phase III randomised clinical trial led by the University of Michigan and published in JAMA Pediatrics followed 830 children under age 6 across dental clinics and Head Start programs in Michigan, New York, and Iowa. The study confirmed that a single application of 38% silver diamine fluoride (SDF) — a painless liquid painted onto the tooth in seconds — successfully arrested tooth decay in over half of treated baby teeth when reapplied every six months. SDF works because silver ions kill cavity-causing bacteria while fluoride strengthens tooth mineral structure. Unlike traditional fillings, SDF requires no drilling, no needles, no sedation. The treatment is already FDA-cleared for use in children and is on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines. The main tradeoff is that SDF darkens the treated area cosmetically — a purely cosmetic change that is often acceptable when the alternative is a painful dental procedure under general anesthesia."
