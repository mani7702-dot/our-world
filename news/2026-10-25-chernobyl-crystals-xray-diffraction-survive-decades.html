"How did X-ray scientists discover that uranium crystals can survive for decades underground?"

By Our World Published 25 October 2026 at 10:19 IST
Reading options

"Tiny uranium oxide crystals trapped in soil from a 1986 nuclear accident have retained their original atomic structure for nearly 40 years — revealed by scientists using a technique called synchrotron X-ray diffraction that rotates each particle like a tiny gem in a focused X-ray beam."

1. How do scientists "see" inside invisible crystals?

Imagine a crystal so tiny you need a magnifying glass just to find it — and it's made of material that glows with hidden energy. How do scientists peek inside its invisible atomic architecture without breaking it apart? They use a technique called synchrotron X-ray diffraction, which works a bit like a cosmic game of billiards played with light.

When a narrow beam of X-rays hits a crystal, the atoms inside scatter the light in a dazzling pattern — a bit like ripples spreading out when you toss pebbles into a pond. By watching how those ripples bounce off the atomic walls, scientists can map out exactly how every atom sits next to every other. It is like reading a secret message written in light.

2. Crystal detectives study 40-year-old particles

A team of researchers led by Tobias Weissenborn at Leibniz University Hannover and Christoph Hennig at Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf took on a puzzle that had sat untouched for decades. The particles they studied were uranium oxide crystals — tiny grains of the same ceramic material that fuels nuclear reactors — that had been scattered across the ground decades ago.

To examine each particle, the scientists placed it in a machine called a synchrotron. This is a ring-shaped particle accelerator that fires a flashlight-like beam of X-rays so bright and steady it can probe a single crystal the size of a speck of dust. They rotated each particle slowly through the beam — measuring it from 2,000 different angles — and watched the X-rays dance and scatter off its inner walls.

The grainy, scattered pattern that came back was a fingerprint: it told the researchers exactly how the atoms were arranged, layer upon layer, inside that tiny crystal.

3. A crystal that remembers its shape

Most people expect that after 40 years baking in soil and weather, a crystal would be shattered, melted, or rewritten by time. But the data told a different story.

The uranium oxide particles had kept most of their original crystal shape. Their atomic walls — the carefully stacked rows of uranium and oxygen atoms — were still standing, still arranged the same way they had been decades earlier.

It was a surprise. Crystals are usually fragile things. Heat, pressure, rain, and time all nudge their atoms out of place. To find these particles still holding their shape after forty years was like finding a sandcastle untouched after a year-long storm.

This discovery helps scientists understand how materials behave in extreme long-term conditions — knowledge that could help design safer storage for nuclear waste and better materials for everything from spacecraft to medical implants.

Try this at home

You can explore crystal patterns with things around the house! Fill a clear glass with water, then place a small spoon or coin at the bottom. Shine a flashlight through the side of the glass onto a piece of paper. The water acts like a crystal, bending and scattering the light. Move the spoon and watch how the shadow changes — just like how scientists rotate their crystals to read the patterns. Try it with different objects: a pencil, a piece of foil, a glass marble. Each will scatter light in its own unique way, just like real crystals scatter X-rays!

Fun Fact

The word crystal comes from the Ancient Greek krýstallos, meaning "ice." Ancient people thought clear quartz crystals were just chunks of ice that never melted — because they were so pure and clear! Today, scientists use crystals in almost everything: your phone screen, your watch, even the lasers that read CDs. And the technique these researchers used — synchrotron X-ray diffraction — was first discovered in 1912 by a scientist named Max von Laue, who won the Nobel Prize for it in 1914.

For grown-ups

"Scientists used synchrotron X-ray diffraction at the Rossendorf Beamline in Grenoble to study six uranium oxide particles from soil near the 1986 Chernobyl site. Each particle (8–50 micrometers) was rotated in a 100-micrometer-focused X-ray beam and measured from 2,000 angles, revealing its internal crystalline phases. Uranium dioxide (UO2) has a fluorite crystal structure (like calcium fluoride), and the particles had oxidized into various uranium oxides including U3O8. Surprisingly, the nuclear fuel inside all six particles had retained much of its original crystal structure after 40 years in the environment, suggesting some radioactive debris is more chemically stable than expected. Published in Science Advances by researchers at Leibniz University Hannover and Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf. Wikipedia confirms UO2 has the fluorite structure (cubic, each uranium surrounded by eight oxygen in a cube) and that X-ray diffraction reveals atomic arrangement by measuring how X-rays scatter off crystal planes (Bragg's law, Bragg 1912–1915, Nobel 1915)."
