"How did NASA's OSIRIS-REx asteroid mission find organic building blocks from the dawn of the solar system?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 01:21 IST IST
Reading options

"After a decade of surprising discoveries, NASA has wrapped up the OSIRIS-REx mission — but the spacecraft's journey continues onward to a new target: asteroid Apophis. Along the way, it brought back the largest asteroid sample in history from Bennu, filled with clues about how our solar system began."

How did NASA's OSIRIS-REx asteroid mission find organic building blocks from the dawn of the solar system?

1. A Ten-Year Journey to a Ancient Rock

On September 30, 2026, NASA officially said goodbye to one of its most daring space adventures. For ten years, the OSIRIS-REx spacecraft traveled through space, visited a tiny asteroid called Bennu, and brought back precious dust that holds clues about how our entire Solar System began.

"Our Solar System formed from a swirling disk of gas and dust more than 4.5 billion years ago," explains the mission's Principal Investigator, Dante Lauretta. "Bennu is like a time capsule from that era — a piece of the original material that never quite made it into a planet."

2. Touching the Skull of an Ancient Asteroid

When OSIRIS-REx arrived at Bennu in December 2018, scientists expected to find a smooth, sandy world. Instead, they discovered a boulder-strewn landscape that looked more like a giant pile of rocks than a tidy ball. In October 2020, the spacecraft gently touched down at a spot named Nightingale and fired a burst of nitrogen gas to stir up dust and pebbles — filling its sample container with material from Bennu's surface.

To everyone's amazement, the spacecraft collected far more than expected: 121.6 grams (about 4.3 ounces) of asteroid material — more than twice the mission's goal. Inside that tiny amount were organic molecules — the raw chemical ingredients that, on Earth, are essential for life. These compounds formed in space long before our planet ever existed, surviving billions of years in the cold darkness between the planets.

3. From Bennu to Apophis: A New Adventure

Even though its primary mission is winding down, OSIRIS-REx is not done flying. NASA has renamed the spacecraft OSIRIS-APEX and pointed it toward asteroid Apophis — another near-Earth space rock that will make a close flyby of Earth in 2029. This extended mission, launching in late 2026, will study how Apophis's surface changes as the Sun's heat makes its rocks expand and crack.

The Bennu samples are now being carefully studied at NASA's labs, with over 200 scientific papers already written by researchers around the world. Each new discovery from the mission helps us piece together the story of how water and organic materials were delivered to the early Earth — ingredients that may have helped life begin here.

Try this at home

Next time you look up at the night sky, remember that every speck of "starlight" could be a world like Bennu. Use a flashlight in a dark room and sprinkle a little glitter or cinnamon on a plate — those tiny particles represent asteroids and dust that, over millions of years, slowly came together to form planets. The same kind of cosmic dust that made Bennu still falls gently to Earth as meteorites you can sometimes find in your garden!

Fun Fact

OSIRIS-REx holds a Guinness World Record: it orbited Bennu at just 1.75 kilometers (about 1 mile) above the surface — the closest any spacecraft has ever orbited a celestial object. That's like flying a drone just above a football stadium, except the "stadium" is a lumpy, boulder-covered asteroid millions of miles away.

NASA's OSIRIS-REx (Origins, Spectral Interpretation, Resource Identification, Security-Regolith Explorer) was a $1 billion spacecraft launched on September 8, 2016 atop an Atlas V 411 rocket from Cape Canaveral. The mission traveled to asteroid 101955 Bennu, a 500-meter-wide carbonaceous C-type asteroid whose dark surface is rich in carbon and water-bearing minerals. After arriving in December 2018, OSIRIS-REx spent over two years studying Bennu from 2.5 km away, setting a Guinness World Record for the closest orbit around any known celestial object. On October 20, 2020, the spacecraft performed a touch-and-go sample collection at the Nightingale site using its Touch-And-Go Sample Acquisition Mechanism (TAGSAM), firing nitrogen gas to fluidize regolith and collecting 121.6 grams — over twice the required minimum. The samples returned to Earth on September 24, 2023, landing at the Utah Test and Training Range inside a 46 kg sample return capsule. Bennu was chosen as the target because it is a "rubble-pile" asteroid of the CM group, thought to be fragments of a disrupted water-rich parent body from the early solar system, rich in carbonaceous material and organic compounds. The returned samples, containing amino acids, sugars, and water-bearing minerals, have already yielded over 200 peer-reviewed publications as scientists study how organic materials — the building blocks of life — were distributed throughout the early Solar System. NASA formally concluded the primary mission on September 30, 2026. The spacecraft has been renamed OSIRIS-APEX and is en route to asteroid Apophis, which will approach Earth in 2029; the extended mission will study how solar heating alters Apophis's surface over time.
