How did Brazil give millions of workers an extra day off each week?

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 05:14 IST
Reading options

"Brazil's Senate voted 75-1 to end the 6-day workweek, cutting the maximum workweek from 44 to 40 hours and guaranteeing two paid days off — the first change to labor hours since the 1988 Constitution."

How did Brazil give millions of workers an extra day off each week?

Imagine having to work six days in a row, every week, with only one day to rest before starting again. For millions of Brazilian workers, that was everyday life — until now.

In a vote of 75 to 1, Brazil's Senate has approved a new law that ends the famous "six-days-on, one-day-off" schedule. The change gives workers a true two-day weekend, cuts the maximum workweek from 44 hours to 40 hours, and does it all without cutting anyone's pay.

1. What was the old schedule — and why was it so hard?

Brazil's "6x1" schedule meant workers put in six consecutive days of work followed by just one day of rest. Most other countries already work five days a week, so Brazil was one of the last major economies still doing six.

The schedule was especially tough because:

  • Six long days meant less time for family, school, and rest
  • Many workers — especially in shops and services — felt constantly tired
  • It was hard to plan anything on that single day off, since it fell on different days for different people

2. The vote that changed everything

On October 14, 2026, Brazil's Senate voted overwhelmingly to approve PEC 221/2019 — a proposed constitutional amendment that:

  • Eliminates the 6x1 schedule entirely
  • Reduces the maximum workweek from 44 hours to 40 hours
  • Guarantees two paid days off every week (Saturday or Sunday — workers choose)
  • Protects salaries — no one earns less

The transition is gentle. First, after 60 days, the workweek drops to 42 hours. Then, one year later, it reaches the final 40-hour week. This gives companies time to hire extra help or adjust shifts.

The change affects about 26.8 million workers across Brazil.

3. Why did this finally happen now?

The idea had been around since 2019, when deputy Reginaldo Lopes first proposed it. But it really took off when a viral video from pharmacy clerk Rick Azevedo sparked the Life Beyond Work Movement.

Millions of Brazilians joined protests in cities like São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, and Minas Gerais. Artists, students, and labor unions all said: it is time for a change.

President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva supported the measure, and even opponents agreed it was fair. The result? A rare moment of unity in a divided Congress.

This is the first change to Brazil's labor hours since the 1988 Constitution, which originally cut the workweek from 48 hours to 44 hours.

Try this at home

Draw six circles in a row — that is a six-day work week! Put an X in one circle for the single day off. Now, erase one X and draw a second day off. How much better does that feel? Now think of all the things you could do with that extra day — homework, sports, playing with a pet, or just sleeping in. Brazil's workers are about to find out!

Fun Fact

Brazil is one of the largest countries in the world by both population and area, and it is one of the last developed economies to switch from a six-day workweek to five. Countries like Germany and most of Europe made this change decades ago.

For grown-ups

"Brazil's Senate approved PEC 221/2019 on October 14, 2026 by a vote of 75-1. This constitutional amendment eliminates the 6x1 work schedule (six consecutive days of work followed by one day of rest) that had been common in Brazil. The change reduces the standard maximum workweek from 44 to 40 hours, with a two-step transition: after 60 days the workweek drops to 42 hours, then a year later to the final 40 hours. Two days of paid weekly rest are guaranteed, with the rest days left to negotiation between unions and employers (typically Saturday or Sunday, whichever was previously worked). Importantly, no worker's salary will be reduced. The amendment affects roughly 26.8 million Brazilian workers. It was supported by President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and both major political parties. This is the first change to labor hours in Brazil since the 1988 Constitution, which originally reduced the workweek from 48 to 44 hours. The movement was sparked by a viral campaign and the 'Life Beyond Work Movement' (Movimento Vida Além do Trabalho), with trade unions and left-wing parties supporting the measure as a way to protect worker health, reduce burnout, and give families more time together."
