"How did a hidden crater 700 meters under the North Sea get there — and what does an ancient asteroid tell us?"

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 15:39 IST
Reading options

"Scientists have confirmed that the Silverpit Crater deep under the North Sea was carved by a 160-meter asteroid that struck 43 to 46 million years ago, after more than two decades of scientific debate."

How did a hidden crater 700 meters under the North Sea get there — and what does an ancient asteroid tell us?

A mystery hidden beneath the waves

Imagine a giant circle, three kilometers wide, lying 700 meters below the seabed in the southern North Sea. It sits about 130 kilometers off the coast of Yorkshire, England — quietly buried under layers of sediment, invisible from above the water.

Scientists first discovered this strange structure in 2002 using sound waves that bounce through the rock (a technique called seismic imaging). At first, it looked like something made by an asteroid from space. But not everyone agreed. Some geologists thought underground salt movement had shaped it instead. For more than twenty years, the debate continued — until now.

The "smoking gun" buried in an oil well

A team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson from Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh found the proof they needed — tiny mineral crystals with a very special signature.

The crystals came from rock cuttings drilled deep near the crater. Inside them, the scientists found "shocked" quartz and feldspar — minerals whose internal crystal structure has been permanently rearranged by an incredible pressure wave. These features can only form when a space rock slams into Earth at extreme speed. Ordinary natural processes like salt flow or volcanic activity cannot make them.

The team also used brand-new seismic imaging data to study the crater's shape in unprecedented detail. The nearly perfect circular outline, the elevated central peak (like the bump in the middle of a splash), and the surrounding rings of fractured rock all matched what scientists expect from an asteroid impact — not from slow underground salt movement.

The moment of impact, 43 million years ago

With the crater's origin confirmed, the researchers used powerful computer simulations to rewind the clock. They found that about 43 to 46 million years ago — when tiny mammals were just beginning to diversify on land — an asteroid roughly 160 meters across (about the length of a football field) struck the ancient seabed.

The asteroid hit at a shallow angle from the west. Within minutes, the collision blasted a 1.5-kilometer-high curtain of rock and water into the air — nearly a mile high. That towering wall then collapsed back into the ocean, sending a tsunami over 100 meters tall (more than 30 times the height of a two-story house) rippling across the ancient sea.

This was a dramatic event — but remember, it happened tens of millions of years ago, long before humans existed. It sits safely in the pages of Earth's deep history.

Why this hidden crater matters

Earth is constantly bombarded by space rocks, but most evidence of past impacts gets erased. Wind, rain, erosion, and the slow creep of tectonic plates gradually wear down, bury, or destroy craters over millions of years. That is why sites like Silverpit are so rare and so valuable to scientists.

Because Silverpit is so well preserved, researchers can study how an asteroid fracture network develops beneath a crater — something that is nearly impossible to observe on other planets without digging deeply. The same kinds of underground fractures might exist on Mars or the Moon, and studying them here helps scientists read the surfaces of worlds they can only explore from orbit.

Silverpit now joins an exclusive group of confirmed underwater impact sites, including the famous Chicxulub crater in Mexico (linked to the end of the dinosaur era). By studying these ancient scars, scientists learn not just how impacts shaped our planet's past — but how to better understand what happens when a space rock meets an ocean world, anywhere in the solar system.

Fun Fact

The deepest-known impact crater on Earth sits 2 kilometers below the ocean surface: the larger Chicxulub structure, which is still being studied by the DSSG (International Ocean Discovery Program) decades after its discovery. Its peak ring rises some 11 kilometers above the crater floor — taller than Mount Fuji.

Try this at home

Fold a paper plate in half to make a "splash" shape. Sprinkle a few drops of blue water near the folded edge, then let the paper rest flat. Watch how the "water" spreads outward in rings — just like the circular fault patterns that radiate from an impact crater. Draw the rings with a marker to show how an asteroid's energy travels through rock and water in expanding waves.

For grown-ups

"The Silverpit Crater, 3 km wide and buried 700 m below the North Sea about 130 km off Yorkshire, England, was discovered in 2002 via seismic imaging. Its origin was debated for over 20 years. A Heriot-Watt University team combined new seismic data with microscopic mineral evidence (shocked quartz and feldspar crystals from an oil-well core) to confirm an asteroid roughly 160 m across struck the seabed at a low angle about 43-46 million years ago (Eocene epoch), producing a >100-meter tsunami. Published in Nature Communications (DOI 10.1038/s41467-025-63985-z), funded by NERC. The crater joins rare submarine impact sites like Chicxulub. This is presented as deep geological history — no modern hazard."
