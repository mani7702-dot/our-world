"Why do some volcanoes shoot lava into the sky while others just ooze?"

By Our World Published 17 November 2026 at 00:59 IST
Reading options

"Volcanoes come in two moods: fireworks and slow ooze. New experiments on rock from the 2021 La Palma eruption found the difference may come down to how hot the magma got before it started to rise."

Some volcanoes put on a firework show. Others just spill.

At one volcano, glowing lava rockets hundreds of metres into the air in fountains so bright you can see them from kilometres away. At another, the same kind of runny, dark lava simply pours out of the ground and creeps downhill like a very slow river. Both are basaltic volcanoes. Both erupt the same family of molten rock. So why the difference?

A team led by the University of Manchester may have found a big part of the answer, and it comes down to how hot the magma was before it ever started to rise. Their results are published in Nature Communications.

1. The clue was buried in the rock

The scientists studied volcanic material from the 2021 Tajogaite eruption on La Palma, in the Canary Islands. That eruption lasted 85 days, sent rivers of lava to the sea, and made news around the world.

Back in the lab, they did not just look at the rock. They heated and cooled tiny samples while watching crystals form, using intense X-rays at a synchrotron - the Diamond Light Source in the UK, a huge machine that works like a giant microscope.

What they found was a race. A very fast one.

2. Twenty minutes versus eight hours

Magma is not pure liquid. It is a slush of molten rock with crystals floating in it, a bit like a smoothie with ice bits in it. The more crystals, the thicker and stickier the magma becomes.

In the experiments, magma that had not been superheated started growing new crystals after about 20 minutes.

Magma that had been strongly superheated did not start growing crystals for more than eight hours.

That is a huge gap. And it matters, because magma takes time to climb from deep underground to the surface. If crystals start forming during that climb, everything changes.

3. Melting the seeds

Why does extra heat delay crystals so dramatically?

Growing a crystal is a bit like starting a snowball. It is much easier if you already have a tiny lump to roll it around. In magma, those starter lumps are called nuclei - microscopic crystal seeds already sitting in the melt.

Superheating - pushing the magma hotter than the temperature at which its crystals can survive - melts those seeds away. No seeds, no head start. The magma also becomes more evenly mixed inside, which makes it harder for new crystals to organise themselves into a solid.

The team calls this a nucleation delay.

4. Why runny magma means fountains

Now picture the magma rising up the volcano's plumbing.

If crystals form early, the magma turns thick and slow. It crawls upward. That gives the gases trapped inside plenty of time to bubble out through the sticky mush. By the time the magma reaches the top, much of the pressure is gone - so it glugs out quietly as a lava flow.

If crystals are delayed, the magma stays thin and fast. It shoots up quickly, with its gases still trapped and expanding, and bursts out of the vent as fountains of molten rock. In the team's computer model of this fast case, magma travelled the whole way up in roughly 30 minutes.

The study's lead author, Dr Barbara Bonechi, says this ties a volcano's "thermal history" - its temperature story - to how it erupts.

Why this matters for warnings

Volcano scientists build hazard models: computer tools that try to predict what a volcano will do next, so people can be warned and moved in time.

Right now those models mostly look at the chemistry of the magma, how much gas it holds, and how pressure changes underground. This work suggests they should also pay attention to how hot the magma got, and how long crystals were held back.

That could eventually sharpen forecasts - not just whether a volcano will erupt, but whether it will spray or spill.

The takeaway

The same molten rock can behave completely differently depending on its recent past. Heat it a little more, and it arrives at the surface faster, thinner and far more dramatic. Volcanoes, it turns out, are shaped by their history as much as by their ingredients.

Try this at home

You can watch crystals form in your own kitchen - slowly.

Dissolve as much sugar as you can into a jar of hot water (ask a grown-up to help with the hot water). Tie a piece of clean string to a pencil and hang it into the jar so it dangles in the liquid. Leave it somewhere still for a week.

Tiny sugar crystals will start growing on the string, then keep building on each other. Now try the same thing with a second jar where you drop in one grain of sugar first. That jar grows crystals much faster, because you gave it a seed. That is exactly the head start that superheating takes away.

Fun fact

La Palma's 2021 eruption lasted 85 days and covered more than 1,000 football pitches with lava. Nobody died, but thousands of people had to leave their homes - which is exactly why scientists want to understand what makes a volcano spray rather than spill.

"Bonechi, B., Arzilli, F., Polacci, M. et al., 'Superheating in mafic magmas controls clinopyroxene nucleation delay and magma ascent dynamics', Nature Communications 17: 4962 (2026), DOI 10.1038/s41467-026-73352-1 (University of Manchester-led). Tephritic magma from the 2021 Tajogaite eruption (La Palma, Spain) was studied with in situ and ex situ synchrotron X-ray experiments. Superheating (heating above the liquidus, where clinopyroxene is unstable) dissolves pre-existing crystal nuclei and homogenises the melt structure, so on return to subliquidus conditions crystallisation is suppressed. Non-superheated magma began nucleating clinopyroxene in about 20 minutes; strongly superheated magma was delayed by more than 8 hours. Isothermal experiments: melt superheated to 1265 C showed crystallisation onset at 1073 C (peak 1061 C); melt heated to 1365 C showed onset at 1058 C (peak 1032 C). Feeding those measured delays into a conduit ascent model, a long delay keeps crystal content and viscosity low, allowing ascent in roughly 30 minutes and mass eruption rates near 4x10^4 kg/s - conditions consistent with lava fountaining. Early crystallisation instead raises viscosity, slows ascent and gives gases time to escape, favouring effusive lava flows. The authors note hazard models currently key on magma chemistry, gas content and pressure changes; pre-eruptive thermal history and crystallisation kinetics should be added. Caveats: experimental plus numerical work, not a monitoring dataset; eruption style also depends on gas content and conduit geometry."
