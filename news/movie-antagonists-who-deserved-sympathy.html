Which movie "bad guys" are actually the good guys in disguise?

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 20:30 IST
Reading options

"Every movie has a hero and a villain, but what if the person getting in the hero's way isn't really the bad guy at all?"

Every movie gives us a hero to root for and a villain to boo at, right? But what if the person standing in the hero's way isn't really the bad guy at all?

Sometimes the person we're supposed dislike actually has the better argument. Here are 10 movie characters who might deserve a second look.

1. Jerry — Liar Liar (1997)

Imagine this: your ex-wife starts dating someone new, and he seems perfect — kind, dependable, great with kids. But you have to win her back, so the story paints him as the boring guy who gets in the way. In Liar Liar, Fletcher plays the absent dad who suddenly has to be honest thanks to his son's wish. Jerry, the new boyfriend, shows up for the kid, tries to be a dad, and even plays the Claw game — but gets mocked because he's not as fun as a wish-granting magic trick.

The real tragedy? Once Fletcher "learns his lesson," Jerry disappears. The reliable dad who was actually there loses everything to the biological parent who spent years lying and missing birthdays.

2. Meredith Blake — The Parent Trap (1998)

Two scheming kids hate their dad's new girlfriend, so they pull off the most elaborate pranks imaginable: lizards in water bottles, sugar-soaked clothes, rock-filled backpacks, and an air mattress pushed into a lake. But Meredith? She's the adult who showed up to a camping trip and realized this wasn't what she signed up for.

She openly says she doesn't love camping and asks for a choice — marry Dad or lose the daughters. Harsh? Maybe. But the kids never gave her a chance to step into a blended family. Years later, the actress still defends her character. Sometimes being the "mean stepmom" is the only sane response to supernatural kid schemes.

3. Sharpay Evans — High School Musical (2006)

Sharpay wanted the lead role in the school musical. She'd practiced, rehearsed, and prepared. Then two newcomers waltz in after auditions are over, sing an accidental duet, and suddenly the rules change. In any real theater program, that's how you'd lose your spot!

Yes, Sharpay schemed — but her underlying frustration was real. Imagine training for years, then having the director rewrite the playbook for "natural chemistry." Her crime? Taking the drama club seriously while everyone else treated it like a rom-com audition.

4. Sid Phillips — Toy Story (1995)

From the toys' view, Sid is a monster: he dismembers dolls, builds mutant creations, and straps Buzz Lightyear to a rocket. But here's the thing — Sid has no idea the toys are alive. To him, they're just plastic objects he can take apart and rebuild. Kids explore the world by testing, breaking, and remaking things. That's how they learn.

When the toys come to life to scare him, every kid who's ever dismantled a toy learns a lesson. Sid's "crime" is normal childhood curiosity — he just got caught by animated plastic.

5. Walter Peck — Ghostbusters (1984)

An EPA inspector shows up at a weird facility and asks questions. Peter Venkman's response? Insults and ejection. Any reasonable person would be suspicious of a team running a ghost-capturing operation without permits or safety plans. Peck's concern about a mysterious, unregulated business is totally fair.

His mistake? Shutting off the containment system without knowing what it did. But his instinct — "hey, this looks dangerous" — was right. The Ghostbusters really were storing supernatural entities in a basement with no backup plan. Peck became the villain for asking the right questions the wrong way.

6. Mr. Freeze — Batman & Robin (1997)

Before the ice puns and neon lighting, Victor Fries was a scientist trying to save his wife from a mysterious disease. He put her in cryogenic sleep, hoping to find a cure. A lab accident turned him into a man who could only live in freezing temperatures.

Everything bad he does — stealing diamonds, freezing people — happens after his wife is (apparently) killed by Poison Ivy. He's not evil; he's grieving and desperate. And in the end, Batman offers him a lab to keep searching for the cure, and Freeze helps save Alfred from the same illness. A villain who becomes an ally when his real motivation is love.

7. Inspector Javert — Les Misérables (1998 / 2012)

Javert spent his life chasing Jean Valjean, a man who broke parole after serving 19 years for stealing bread. But Javert isn't evil — he was born into poverty and crime, and the law was his only path out. He believes the system is right and people can be divided into "good" and "bad."

The tragedy hits when Valjean shows mercy at the barricades, spares his life, and saves his friend's daughter. Javert can't reconcile a world where mercy and law don't match up. His suicide isn't punishment — it's the breakdown of a man whose entire identity crumbles when the rules stop working.

8. Samuel Gerard — The Fugitive (1993)

Dr. Richard Kimble's wife was murdered, and he's accused of the crime. Deputy U.S. Marshal Samuel Gerard is assigned to hunt him down. When Kimble insists he's innocent, Gerard says: "I don't care." That sounds cold — but Gerard isn't investigating the murder. His job is to capture a fugitive.

As the hunt continues, Gerard notices Kimble behaves nothing like a guilty killer. He leads his team strategically, protects his marshals, and never uses lethal force. By the end, Gerard helps expose the real killer and removes Kimble's handcuffs once the danger passes. He followed procedure — and procedure happened to be right.

9. General Francis Hummel — The Rock (1996)

Hummel starts by stealing rocket launchers and holding tourists hostage on Alcatraz. He threatens to destroy San Francisco with VX nerve gas. Clear villain, right?

Wrong. Hummel spent years doing secret military operations, only to watch his soldiers die while the government denied they ever existed. Their families got no recognition, no compensation — because admitting the missions existed would expose classified operations. Hummel's crime is that he cared enough to fight for forgotten men.

His plan is a bluff: he launches one missile but redirects it into the ocean. He never intended to kill civilians. His final act? Dying to stop the massacre he threatened but never planned. Even his enemies acknowledge he had a point.

10. Roy Batty — Blade Runner (1982)

Roy Batty is introduced as a deadly fugitive: superhuman strength, razor-sharp nails, and a mission to kill his creator. He's the Blade Runner's target. But Batty isn't evil — he's running out of time.

Replicants like Batty are built for dangerous off-world labor but given only four years to live. They sneak back to Earth seeking longer life from their maker. When Batty corners Deckard on a rooftop, he could easily push him to his death. Instead, he saves him.

In his final moments, Batty reflects on the extraordinary experiences that will be lost — experiences no human ever witnessed. His "tears in rain" monologue forces us to ask: if a machine fears death, grieves for others, and grants mercy, who's the real monster?

Try this at home

Next family movie night, watch one of these films together and ask: Who is really in the wrong here? You might be surprised by the answer. The best movie "villains" aren't the ones who break the rules — they're the ones who believe too strongly in the wrong ones.

Fun Fact

In Toy Story, Sid's house was modeled after a real California home that later sold for over $1 million. And the mutant toys he creates became so popular that they got their own spin-off short films — finally getting the redemption arc they deserved!
