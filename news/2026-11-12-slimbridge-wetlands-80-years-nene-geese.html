"How do you bring back a goose that was down to 30 birds?"

By Our World Published 12 November 2026 at 20:26 IST
Reading options

"Eighty years ago a naturalist dug ponds on a marsh beside a river. The Hawaiian goose he bred there went from 30 birds to more than 3,000 — and the marsh itself turned out to be one of the best carbon lockers on Earth."

How do you bring back a goose that was down to 30 birds?

Some rescues start with a van and a net. This one started with a cottage, a spade and a lot of patience.

In November 1946 a naturalist called Peter Scott moved into a small cottage on a marshy bend of the River Severn in Gloucestershire. He was not there for the view. Thousands of swans and geese fly down from the Russian Arctic to spend winter on those mudflats, and Scott wanted to watch them without scaring them off — so he dug ponds, cut channels and built hides.

That marsh became Slimbridge, the first reserve of the charity now called the Wildfowl and Wetlands Trust. This week it turned 80, and the people who run it used the birthday to ask for something bigger than a party: one million hectares of the world's wetlands put back.

The goose that almost ran out

Among the birds Scott worked with was the nēnē, or Hawaiian goose. It lives nowhere else on Earth. By 1952 there were around 30 left, after hunting, roads and introduced animals such as mongooses and cats.

Scott asked Hawaii to send him a few. Three birds arrived at Slimbridge, and he hand-reared them. Nine goslings followed, then more, and by 1979 birds bred in captivity were being released back onto Hawaiian islands.

Today there are more than 3,000 nēnē. In 2019 the species was moved off the strictest endangered list. David Attenborough called Peter Scott "the patron saint of conservation", and it is hard to argue with a bird count that went from 30 to 3,000.

Why the wet bit is the important bit

Here is the part that surprised the scientists. The geese were never the whole story — the water was.

Wetlands are the habitats people drain first. Roughly 85% of the world's wetlands have been lost, mostly to farms and building. Yet about 40% of all species depend on them at some point, and they do a job forests cannot do as quickly.

When the tide runs over a saltmarsh, plants growing there get flattened and buried in mud. Buried under water there is very little oxygen, so the plant material rots far more slowly than a fallen log in a wood. The carbon stays in the ground instead of drifting back into the air.

How much faster? The US ocean agency NOAA measures saltmarsh and mangrove carbon burial at about ten times the rate of a tropical rainforest for the same area, and three to five times more carbon stored per acre. The Trust's own estimate is more modest — four times faster than trees — and even that is a lot.

What the birthday ask actually is

Alongside the anniversary, the Trust wants 100,000 hectares of wetland restored in the UK and one million hectares around the world. It runs ten reserves, and it has started small in cities too: in Bristol, rafts of wetland plants were moored in the harbour so roots can hang in the water and clean it.

None of it is exotic. It is mostly letting water stay where it lands.

Try this at home

Take two clear cups and punch a hole in the bottom of each. Fill one with dry sand and one with garden soil mixed with dead leaves. Pour the same amount of water into both, slowly, and time how long it takes to come out the bottom.

The sand dumps it straight through. The leafy soil holds on, then releases it in a trickle. That is a miniature wetland: ground that drinks slowly is ground that keeps its carbon, feeds plants and soaks up floods. Put a saucer under both and compare what is left after ten minutes.

Takeaway: a marsh looks like nothing much. Underneath, it is a slow, stubborn storage system — and one patient person with a spade started 80 years of putting one back.
