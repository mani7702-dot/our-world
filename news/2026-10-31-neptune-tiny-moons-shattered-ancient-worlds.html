"What Happened to Neptune's Missing Moons?"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at "12:25" IST
Reading options

"Neptune has almost no big moons at all — just one giant one and a scatter of tiny specks. Now the James Webb Space Telescope has found strange clay on those little moons, and it may be the fingerprint of a cosmic catastrophe."

Our Solar System has some very tidy planets. Jupiter has four big, neat moons lined up like a family photo. Saturn has its giant moon Titan. Uranus has a set of five medium moons. But Neptune is the odd one out.

Neptune has exactly one big moon — a strange, frosty world called Triton — and then a scatter of tiny moons no bigger than small cities. Scientists have long wondered why. Now the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has found a clue hiding on those little moons, and it points to an ancient disaster.

A telescope that reads chemistry

You cannot visit Neptune's small moons, so scientists do the next best thing: they study the light coming off them.

Instruments like JWST's near-infrared spectrograph split that light into its separate colours, the way a prism splits sunlight into a rainbow. Different chemicals leave different patterns in that rainbow, so the pattern tells scientists what a moon is made of — a bit like reading a barcode.

A team at Caltech pointed JWST at three of Neptune's small inner moons — Larissa, Galatea and Proteus — plus the planet's rings. Nobody had ever measured their chemistry before.

Clay where clay should not be

The result was a surprise. Larissa, Galatea and the rings showed signs of phyllosilicates — a family of clay minerals. On Earth, clays like these only form where there is liquid water.

But way out at Neptune, almost everything is frozen solid. "That's really surprising, because everything out in this part of the solar system is really icy," says lead researcher Ryleigh Davis.

Clay also needs to come from somewhere big. A tiny moon freezing in deep space could never hold enough heat to melt water. So the clay must have been born deep inside something much larger — and then got scattered across the little moons.

The story the clay tells

The team thinks Neptune once had a normal family of big moons, similar to the ones we see at Uranus today. Then Triton arrived.

Triton orbits Neptune backwards — the opposite way to the planet's spin — which is a strong hint that it did not form there. It was probably captured from the icy Kuiper Belt. As Triton's gravity barged in, it likely smashed Neptune's original moons to pieces.

Later, some of that rubble clumped back together into the small moons we see now — carrying clay from deep inside the shattered worlds. Curiously, Proteus had no clay signature at all, suggesting it may have been rebuilt from a different pile of debris.

Try this at home

Drop a fizzy antacid tablet into a glass of water and watch the bubbles rise and the bits swirl. Debris stirred up in a liquid doesn't just sink — it can clump together into new shapes. Astronomers think something similar happened to Neptune's smashed-up moons, as gravity slowly gathered the drifting rubble back into new worlds.

Fun fact

Neptune's moons may be the only place in the Solar System where we can see the insides of a big icy world. Normally that material is buried forever, under kilometres of ice. Out at Neptune, a catastrophe turned those ancient moons inside out — and left the evidence on display.

For grown-ups

"A Caltech-led team (lead author Ryleigh Davis, PhD '26; lab of Prof. Mike Brown) used JWST near-infrared spectroscopy to measure Neptune's rings and the inner moons Larissa, Galatea and Proteus — the first spectroscopy ever obtained for these bodies. They detected magnesium-rich phyllosilicates (clay minerals that require liquid water to form) on Larissa, Galatea and the rings, but no water ice. Phyllosilicates had never previously been detected in the outer Solar System beyond Jupiter. Proteus showed no clay signature. A companion study by Caltech postdoc Matthew Belyakov suggests Nereid may be the only survivor of Neptune's original moon system. The favoured explanation is that Neptune's primordial moons were destroyed when Triton — which orbits backwards, suggesting it was captured from the Kuiper Belt — was pulled in by the planet's gravity; debris reaccreted into today's small inner moons. An alternative is the tidal shredding of a Pluto-sized Kuiper Belt object. Published in Science Advances, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aeb1437, paper titled 'Neptune's Inner Moons and Rings Are Exposed Icy Body Interiors'. Co-authors: Belyakov, Brown, Zachariah Milby, Ian Wong (STScI). Funded by NASA via STScI."
