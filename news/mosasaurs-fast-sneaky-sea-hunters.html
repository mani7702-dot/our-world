Mosasaurs: the fast, sneaky hunters of the ancient seas

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 10:05 IST
Reading options

While dinosaurs ruled the land, bus-sized mosasaurs hunted the ocean. New physics models show they did it with sudden, sneaky bursts of speed.

While dinosaurs walked on land, truly giant monsters ruled the sea. They were called mosasaurs, and some grew as long as a city bus. Yet a new study shows they were not slow sea monsters at all — they were fast, sneaky ambush hunters.

Sea monsters that were not dinosaurs

Mosasaurs were huge marine reptiles, close cousins of today's snakes and lizards. They lived in the oceans of the Late Cretaceous, at the same time as T. rex, and they hunted smaller animals in the water.

The surprise: a powerful burst

Scientists used computer models of their bodies to work out how fast they could move. Their big clue was the tail. Some mosasaurs had long, flexible tails that could curl — and that shape is built for a sudden burst.

The study calls this a "slam-start": the animal curls its tail, then sweeps it sideways against the water to launch itself forward in a flash. Some could reach around 15 miles per hour in that single surge — far faster than a human can swim.

Built for a sneak attack

A snake-like, flexible-tailed mosasaur is perfect for lying hidden and then striking out of nowhere. The scientists describe them as skilled ambush predators — hiding, waiting, then exploding forward to catch prey by surprise.

Other mosasaurs with different tails were better at steady cruising, chasing prey across open water. Different hunters, different tricks — but none of them slow.

Why fossil physics matters

These animals lived 65 million years ago, so we can never watch them swim. Instead, scientists build computer models from the bones and from living relatives like the Komodo dragon, then let physics guess how the bodies worked. It is a new way to bring extinct animals back to life — one simulation at a time.

So next time you picture an ancient sea monster: not a slow giant, but a patient, lightning-fast ambusher, coiling its tail in the dark water, ready to strike.

For grown-ups

The short version for curious grown-ups: paleontologist Kiersten Formoso and colleagues (Current Biology, published online Oct 7) used biomechanical models of mosasaur tail morphology to estimate burst swimming performance. Long-flexible-tailed forms like Platecarpus and Tylosaurus produced faster lunges relative to body length via a "slam-start" — a curled-tail sweep propelling the animal forward — pointing to ambush predation, while Mosasaurus and Plotosaurus were better suited to sustained cruising. These are biomechanical estimates from fossil anatomy and living relatives (snakes and monitor lizards), not direct measurements of swimming animals.
