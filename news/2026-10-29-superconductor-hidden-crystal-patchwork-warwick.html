Inside a 40-year-old superconductor, scientists found a hidden patchwork

By Our World Published 29 October 2026 at 12:13 IST
Reading options

For four decades scientists thought a famous superconductor was the same all the way through. A new 3D X-ray picture shows it is secretly patchy inside — with wide borders that may be quietly slowing it down.

Inside a 40-year-old superconductor, scientists found a hidden patchwork

The magic material that never wears out

Some materials can do something almost magical: they let electricity flow with zero resistance — no wasted energy, no heat, nothing. These are called superconductors, and scientists have studied one particular family of them for 40 years.

Here is the catch: for all that time, everyone assumed that these materials were the same all the way through — a neat, uniform crystal, like a perfectly tiled floor where every tile is identical. Nearly every theory about them was built on that one picture.

A CT scan for atoms finds a surprise

A team at the University of Warwick in the UK, working with a giant X-ray machine in France, decided to look inside these crystals for the very first time.

They used a clever technique called scanning 3D X-ray diffraction. Think of it as a medical CT scan — the kind doctors use to peek inside your body — but built to map individual atoms instead of bones and organs.

What they found shocked them. The crystal was not uniform at all. It was a patchwork — a patchwork of regions holding two slightly different arrangements of atoms, like a quilt sewn from two different fabrics.

Even stranger were the borders between these patches. They were hundreds of times wider than anyone expected. These wide borders were so broad that they behaved almost like separate structures of their own — not thin dividing lines, but whole wide zones hiding inside the crystal.

Why the hidden borders matter

Those invisible borders may not be harmless. The scientists think the wide patches and borders could actually be working against the superconductor, quietly interfering with how easily electricity flows through it.

That could explain a mystery scientists have noticed for years: why some superconductors perform better than others, even when they look almost identical from the outside.

The discovery means that 40 years of scientific assumptions need a rethink. It also suggests that similar hidden patchworks may be hiding inside many other superconductors — ones that researchers are still testing today.

Try this at home

You cannot test superconductors at home (they need temperatures colder than deep space!). But you can test the idea of a hidden inside: grab a chocolate bar with a soft filling, or a candy with layers, and notice how what you see from the outside does not tell you what is inside. Scientists just discovered that the same is true for one of the most famous materials in physics — you have to take a really close look to find what is really there.

For grown-ups: The team, led by Mark Senn in Warwick's Department of Chemistry and working with the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF), used scanning 3D X-ray diffraction to map a cuprate (copper-oxide) high-temperature superconductor. Published in Physical Review Letters (2026), the paper reports intrinsic heterogeneity — coexisting regions of two subtly different crystal structures separated by "giant domain walls" roughly 150 nm wide, far broader than conventional twin boundaries. The authors argue these domain walls likely impair superconductivity rather than passively coexisting with it, which may explain run-to-run variation in bulk transport measurements and will require theoretical models that incorporate structural complexity. The work was enabled by the ESRF's recent €150M upgrade for brighter 3D X-ray beams.
