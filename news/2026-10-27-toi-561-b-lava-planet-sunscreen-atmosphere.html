"What If a Lava Planet Wore a Giant Sunscreen?"

By Our World Published 27 October 2026 at 14:14 IST IST
Reading options

"James Webb Space Telescope data shows that TOI-561 b, a rocky world where it is always daytime, reflects 75% of its star's light back into space — thanks to a silicate cloud 'sunscreen' and an atmosphere that moves heat to the dark side."

What If a Lava Planet Wore a Giant Sunscreen?

Some planets are so close to their star that one side never, ever goes to sleep. Imagine a world where it is always daytime — forever. And imagine that this scorched, lava-covered world somehow stays a little cooler than scientists expected. How? A planet-sized sunscreen made of sparkling clouds.

A rock that runs on fast-forward

Meet TOI-561 b. It is a "super-Earth" — a rocky world a bit bigger than our own planet — but it lives in the ultimate fast lane. This planet zooms all the way around its star in just 11 hours. To put that in perspective: Earth takes a whole year to complete one orbit. That means TOI-561 b finishes 780 trips while Earth takes just one.

Because it is so close to its star, the dayside of TOI-561 b bakes at temperatures above 2,000 °C (3,600 °F) — hot enough to melt rock into rivers of lava. The same face always points toward the star, so the nightside never sees direct starlight at all.

A mirror in the sky

When astronomers first looked at TOI-561 b, something seemed off. The dayside was surprisingly cool — too cool for a dark, airless rock that should soak up every ray. Where was the extra heat going?

James Webb watched the planet for 37 hours, catching nearly four of its lightning-fast orbits. By measuring how the planet's brightness and warmth changed throughout its orbit (a trick called a "phase curve"), the team found the answer: TOI-561 b reflects about 75% of its star's light right back into space. That is almost as reflective as Venus, the shiniest planet we know.

What is doing the reflecting? Scientists think high clouds made of silicate minerals — the same stuff glass and sand are made of — form over the blazing dayside. These tiny, sparkling particles hover like the highest layer of Earth's atmosphere, bouncing the star's energy away before it can turn the surface into a cosmic kiln. It is as if the planet grew its own giant, invisible sunscreen.

Heat on the move

The atmosphere does something else amazing: it carries heat from the scorching dayside around to the dark, nightside hemisphere. That is why the nightside is warmer than expected, too. Think of it like a planetary game of hot-potato — heat travels across the whole world instead of sitting in one spot.

This matters because it helps the planet keep an atmosphere at all. Without that heat-transport, the dayside would cook away gases while the nightside would freeze them out. Instead, the circulating air keeps things (relatively) even — like a planet-wide blanket that refuses to quit.

What this teaches us about Earth

You might think a world of boiling lava is boring for the search for life. It is far too hot for plants, animals, or even microbes. But TOI-561 b is teaching us something precious about how rocky planets hold onto — or lose — their atmospheres. Our own young Earth may have looked a lot like this: a molten ball of rock wrapped in steam, long before oceans and skies formed. By studying this distant lava world, we are peeking back in time at our planet's dramatic babyhood.

Try this at home

Weather vs. climate on a speed-demon world: On Earth, we wait a whole year for the seasons. TOI-561 b cycles through day and night in just 11 hours. If Earth orbited that fast, a single day would last less than the time it takes to watch a movie! Draw two pictures of TOI-561 b: one from the "always day" side (lava oceans, silicate clouds) and one from the "always night" side (dark, cooler, but kept warm by air currents). Label how heat and light move between them.

Materials activity: Fill a clear cup halfway with water to represent the planet's atmosphere. Shine a flashlight on one side to represent the star. Use a small mirror floating on the water's surface to represent the reflective silicate clouds — angle it so the light bounces back. Watch how the water (atmosphere) carries the heat around to the other side.

Fun Fact

TOI-561 b orbits its star closer than Mercury orbits our Sun — and yet, thanks to its reflective cloud sunscreen, it may still keep an atmosphere. Venus is the only other planet we know that is this reflective, and it also keeps a thick atmosphere despite being baked by the Sun. Two very different worlds, one surprising shared trick.

for-grown-ups: TOI-561 b is a rocky super-Earth (1.4 R_Earth, ~2 M_Earth) in the constellation Taurus, completing an orbit every 10.9 hours at just 0.014 AU from its Sun-like star. JWST phase-curve observations over 37 hours revealed it reflects ~75% of incident starlight and transports heat from dayside to nightside, implying a thick atmosphere with silicate (SiO2, MgSiO3) clouds acting as a reflective sunscreen. The study (Astronomical Journal, led by U. Waterloo/L. Dang and U. Birmingham/A. Piette) suggests the atmosphere may be CO2- or SO2-rich, possibly sustained by outgassing from a global magma ocean. Wikipedia confirms the 11-hour orbit, dayside temperature >2000 K, and volatile-rich atmosphere detection. ScienceDaily reported the findings (Oct 26, 2026).
