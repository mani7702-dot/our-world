"Can a clever machine clean toxic algae from our lakes before it makes us sick?"

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 10:23 IST
Reading options

"Hot summer weather turns some lakes green and pea-soup thick with toxic blue-green algae. Now a scientist's idea — born after watching his own child's favorite park close — could give nature a hand."

Can a clever machine clean toxic algae from our lakes before it makes us sick?

Why does lake water turn pea-soup green?

On hot summer days, still water in lakes and ponds can suddenly turn pea-green and thick — like someone spilled a giant smoothie into the water. What you are seeing is a bloom of blue-green algae (scientists call them cyanobacteria, which is a type of bacteria, not true algae).

These tiny organisms love warm, still water. When the sun shines and the air is hot, they multiply faster than anyone can count — sometimes going from invisible to carpet-thick in just a few days. The problem is that some kinds of blue-green algae release toxins that can make people and animals sick if the water is swallowed. That is why signs go up, swimming is banned, and model boat clubs have to wait.

A dad's idea born at a closed park

Dr. Sanjay Nagarajan, who studies how to protect the environment at the University of Bath, was at Warminster Pleasure Grounds in Wiltshire with his child when he noticed something sad: the lake was closed because of a bloom, and warning signs were posted all around. The water that should have been sparkling for summer fun was instead covered in green scum.

That moment sparked an idea. What if a machine could gently clean the algae out of the water — without hurting fish, plants, or the good microorganisms that keep lakes healthy?

Dr. Nagarajan and his team built just such a machine. It is designed to float on the surface of the lake and carefully pull the blue-green algae out of the water, gathering it before the bloom gets big enough to release dangerous toxins. The goal is to help towns and cities keep their lakes safe for swimming, boating, and wildlife — all summer long.

Why this matters more than ever

As the planet warms, hot weather that used to be unusual is becoming normal. More hot days means more algae blooms in lakes from here to Australia. Scientists know that algal blooms are nature's way of responding to extra warmth and nutrients — but when they produce toxins, they can shut down whole lakes and make people sick.

The machine being tested in Warminster is still small — just a prototype — but if it works well, it could one day be used in lakes and ponds in cities and towns everywhere. The dream is to give families a safe, clean place to enjoy the water, even on the hottest days of summer.

Fun Fact: The Earth's oceans and lakes contain so much water that if all the world's land were covered evenly to a depth of about 8,400 feet (2,560 meters), there would still be room for every drop — but only if we keep it clean!

Try this at home

Fill a shallow baking dish with clear water and sprinkle in a pinch of green food coloring. Shine a bright flashlight on one end (your "sun") and leave the other end darker. Watch how the green spreads and swirls — that is what an algae bloom looks like from space! To see the "cleaning machine" effect, gently drag a clean coffee filter through the water; it will catch floating green bits, just like Dr. Nagarajan's machine catches algae.

For grown-ups: Blue-green algae blooms (cyanobacteria) thrive in warm, nutrient-rich water and some species produce cyanotoxins such as microcystins. The prototype developed by Dr. Sanjay Nagarajan at the University of Bath's Institute for Sustainability and Climate Change uses gentle surface collection to remove blooms before toxin release. Wikipedia confirms cyanobacteria are "autotrophic bacteria" that obtain energy via oxygenic photosynthesis and are the basis of the common name "blue-green algae." Algal blooms are defined as "a rapid increase or accumulation in the population of algae in fresh water" and can be harmful when toxin-producing species dominate.
