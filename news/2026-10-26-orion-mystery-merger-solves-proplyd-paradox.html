"How a Stellar Merger Solved Orion's Time Paradox: Why Baby Disks Look Older Than Their Stars"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 16:20 IST
Reading options

"In the nearby Orion Nebula, planet-making disks called proplyds looked too old for their one-million-year-old stars. Astronomers say a violent stellar merger — two stars crashing into one — explains the time paradox."

1. The Orion Puzzle: Why Do Planet-Disks Look Older Than Their Own Stars?

High in the winter sky, the Orion Nebula glows like a fuzzy star to naked eyes — but through a telescope, it reveals one of astronomy's great time puzzles. Baby disks of dust and gas swirl around infant stars that are about a million years old, yet something about those disks says they've been alive much longer. It's a paradox that has puzzled scientists for decades.

2. The Tails That Shouldn't Be There

When the Hubble Space Telescope first pointed at Orion in 1993, it found hundreds of glowing cosmic "disks" with long, wispy tails. Astronomers named them proplyds — short for "protoplanetary disks."

The trouble was their tails. Each tail streams away from a fierce star called Theta 1 Orionis C ("One-Oh-Ri-Cee"), the brightest member of the Trapezium Cluster at Orion's heart. That star blasts ultraviolet light that slowly boils away the disk material, and the tails record how fast that "boiling" has happened. But when astronomers measured the tails, they found something odd: the disks looked like they had been evaporating for far longer than the stars themselves had existed.

3. The Merger That Changed Everything

New research published in Astronomy & Astrophysics offers a dramatic solution: two stars crashed into one big star.

Lead author Maite Wilhelm, an astronomer at Leiden University in the Netherlands, and her team say the star Theta 1 Orionis C is not what it first appears. It is actually the product of a stellar merger — a violent collision between two younger stars that happened at just the right time.

Before the collision, the region's radiation was gentler, and the proplyds survived quietly. After the merger, the new, more massive star became far brighter and hotter. Its renewed ultraviolet flood suddenly began stripping the disks much faster — making them look prematurely aged. The single merger event reset the clock on Orion's whole nursery.

4. A Few Other Mysteries, Solved

The merger story also helps explain two other strange features of Theta 1 Orionis C:

  • Its magnetic field is tilted — something mergers can scramble.
  • It has an unexpected excess of nitrogen — material typically mixed in during a stellar collision.

"This is the first time we have witnessed the consequences of a major stellar collision in a our own cosmic neighbourhood," said co-author Simon Portegies Zwart. "It also shows that stellar collisions may be much more common than we previously thought, and that they can have far-reaching consequences."

Try this at home

Find Orion's fuzzy "star" in the sword hanging below Orion's Belt on a clear, dark night. Through binoculars it resolves into a tiny star cluster surrounded by a gray glow — that's the Trapezium Cluster lighting up the nebula. Use a free planetarium app like SkySafari or Stellarium to point out the Trapezium's four brightest stars. For the full Hubble view of the proplyds and their tails, visit NASA's Orion gallery at https://hubblesite.org/ and search "Orion Nebula."

Fun Fact

Orion is the third-brightest star-forming region visible in Earth's night sky and the closest one where we can watch planets being born in real time. The single star at the heart of the Trapezium Cluster — Theta 1 Ori C — weighs more than 33 times our Sun, yet it formed from the merger of smaller stars that existed for only about a million years.

For grown-ups

"The Orion Nebula (Messier 42) is a star-forming region about 1,267 light-years away, the closest massive star-forming region to Earth. Hubble imaged it in 1993 and discovered 'proplyds' (ionized protoplanetary disks) with distinctive tails pointing away from the Trapezium Cluster's most massive star, Theta 1 Orionis C (1 Ori C1). The paradox: the stars are about one million years old, yet the proplyds' tails and the nebula's expanding shell both showed dynamical ages of only ~0.3 million years — they looked far older than their natal stars. New research by Maite Wilhelm (Leiden University) et al. in Astronomy & Astrophysics proposes that 1 Ori C1 formed from the merger of two binary stars, mediated by von Zeipel-Lidov-Kozai (vZLK) oscillations induced by its current companion 1 Ori C2. Before the merger, the combined radiation was weak and the proplyds survived longer; after the merger, 1 Ori C1 became a more luminous and hotter O-type star (>33 solar masses) whose UV radiation suddenly intensified, sharply accelerating photoevaporation and shell expansion. The merger also explains 1 Ori C1's tilted magnetic field and excess nitrogen — both signatures of fresh stellar material mixed during collision. The findings confirm that stellar collisions may be more common than thought and profoundly shape the environments where planets form."
