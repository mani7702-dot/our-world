"Why Is Antarctica's 'Doomsday Glacier' Melting Faster Than Anyone Expected?"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 08:30 IST
Reading options

"Scientists have discovered that warm ocean water reaches much farther inland beneath Antarctica's Thwaites Glacier than they thought — and that hidden melting could raise sea levels faster than models predicted. The glacier nicknamed the 'Doomsday Glacier' sits in West Antarctica and drains enough ice to reshape coastlines worldwide if it ever slips away."

What is the 'Doomsday Glacier' — and why does its nickname matter?

Hidden in the icy wilderness of West Antarctica, a river of ice larger than England flows slowly toward the sea. This is Thwaites Glacier, and scientists call it the "Doomsday Glacier" not because it will end the world — but because if it ever collapses, the rise in sea level could flood coastal cities on every continent.

Thwaites sits in a bowl-shaped dent of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet called the Amundsen Sea Embayment. The region drains roughly one-third of all the ice that melts off Antarctica each year, and if the glacier were to fully disintegrate, it could contribute up to 65 centimeters (about 2 feet) to global sea level rise over centuries.

But here is the puzzling part: for decades, computer models predicted a slow, steady retreat. Yet satellite observations told a different story — Thwaites was thinning and flowing faster than anyone expected. What were the models missing?

A hidden highway under the ice

For years, glaciologists imagined the boundary where Thwaites leaves the land and begins to float on the ocean as a thin, well-defined line — the grounding line. Like a dam holding back water, they assumed it was narrow and mostly stable.

But new research published in Nature Communications reveals that this boundary is not a line at all — it is a vast, kilometer-wide zone.

Warm seawater from the Amundsen Sea slips far inland beneath the glacier, traveling several kilometers under ice that is still resting on the seafloor. As the ocean tides rise and fall, the water lifts the glacier from below and repeatedly bathes its underside in warm, pressurized seawater. This hidden melting carves tunnels and weakens the ice structure in ways that narrow grounding-line models simply could not capture.

When the researchers rewrote their computer models to account for this broad "grounding zone," something remarkable happened: the simulated changes in Thwaites from the 1990s to today suddenly matched satellite observations — and the changes were four times more dramatic than what old models produced.

Why the ocean is the real sculptor

The warm water reaches Thwaites through ocean currents that have been altered by a changing climate. As these currents shift, they deliver more heat from deeper in the ocean to the glacier's base. The ice shelf — the floating portion that acts like a giant brake — is thought to collapse within a decade, and once it is gone, the entire glacier accelerates its flow toward the sea.

Satellite radar data from Finland's ICEYE constellation revealed that the grounding zone is not static. It flexes and shifts across several kilometers each day as tides push seawater in and out, eroding the ice from below in a slow-motion collapse that happens mostly out of sight.

Today, Thwaites accounts for about 4 percent of global sea level rise. The full loss of the glacier would ultimately raise seas by 65 centimeters — more than twice the total amount every other glacier and ice sheet has contributed so far in human history.

Try this at home

You can model the grounding zone with a simple experiment. Fill a shallow baking dish with water and place a block of ice on a small slanted board so it reaches into the water. Add a pinch of salt and food coloring to represent warm seawater. Over the next hour, watch how the "warm" colored water creeps under the ice, melting it from below — just like the hidden highway beneath Thwaites. Ask an adult to help you set this up safely.

Fun Fact

The nickname "Doomsday Glacier" was coined by reporters — not scientists — and many researchers actually dislike it because it sounds alarmist. But the physics behind it is real: Thwaites sits on bedrock that slopes downward inland, meaning the deeper it melts, the more warm water can get in. It is a self-reinforcing cycle that, once started, is very hard to stop.

Grow-up corner

Thwaites Glacier flows at rates exceeding 2 kilometers per year near its grounding line in the Amundsen Sea Embayment of West Antarctica. The glacier was named after American glaciologist Fredrik T. Thwaites (1891–1963), who led early 20th-century expeditions to the region. The UC Irvine-led study that revealed the broad grounding zone used synthetic aperture radar from the ICEYE satellite constellation and compared simulations against more than two decades of observations. Wikipedia's article on the grounding line confirms that this boundary — where grounded ice transitions to floating ice shelf — is where warm ocean water begins to erode the ice sheet from below, and that its position is the single most important factor in predicting sea level rise.
