"Why is NASA racing to put humans back on the Moon — and what did the latest update reveal?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 23:15 IST IST
Reading options

"NASA wants to return humans to the Moon faster than ever — and it just gave a new update on the timeline for the big comeback."

1 — Is NASA racing to get back to the Moon faster than ever?

2 — A Practice Run Before Touching Down

Before NASA sends astronauts walking on the Moon again, the space agency wants to rehearse the hardest parts of the mission — docking with a lunar lander — while still close to home. That is the plan for Artemis III, which NASA now says is targeted for mid-2027.

But here is the surprise: Artemis III will not go all the way to the Moon. Instead, it will be a highly elliptical orbit around Earth — looping as close as 29 kilometers and stretching as far as 1,806 kilometers away. This gives astronauts a chance to practice every critical step in the safety of Earth's neighborhood, where help is always nearby.

3 — Three Rockets, One Dream: Reaching the Moon

The Artemis III test brings together three of the world's most powerful rockets, all launching from Florida's Space Coast:

  1. New Glenn (by Blue Origin) lifts off first, carrying the Blue Moon lander to orbit.
  2. SLS (NASA's Space Launch System) follows, carrying the Orion capsule with four astronauts.
  3. Starship (by SpaceX) launches last and meets Orion in orbit for a short docking test.

Both Blue Moon and Starship are being tested for the same job — landing astronauts on the Moon. NASA is keeping both options open, because having backups means the mission can still succeed even if one lander hits a snag.

4 — Why the Rush? China Is in the Race Too

NASA's urgency is clear: China is making big plans of its own for the Moon, with crewed landings expected in the 2030s. The U.S. wants to be first to establish a sustainable, permanent presence on the lunar surface — not just a quick visit and return.

After Artemis III's successful Earth-orbit test, the next step is Artemis IV, planned for early 2028. That mission aims to be the first crewed lunar landing since 1972 — more than 50 years after Apollo 17 last flew humans to the Moon.

Try this at home

Build a simple rocket launcher! Tape a plastic water bottle to a bicycle pump using a needle valve. Fill the bottle one-third with water, pump air in, and watch the water squirt out the bottom, pushing the "rocket" skyward. Measure how high it flies with different amounts of water. This shows how rockets work — just like real Starship and New Glenn lifting off from Earth!

Fun Fact

Orion's egg-shaped orbit for Artemis III is no accident. Flying close to Earth and then far away in one giant loop lets engineers test how the spacecraft handles extreme temperature changes — the scorching heat of being close to our planet and the bitter cold of space. It is like testing a sleeping bag by spending one night in a sauna and the next in a freezer!
