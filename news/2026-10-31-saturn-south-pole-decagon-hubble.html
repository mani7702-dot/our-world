"Why Did a Giant Ten-Sided Shape Appear on Saturn?"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at "16:20" IST
Reading options

"Saturn already had a famous six-sided shape spinning above its north pole. Now telescopes have caught something brand new at the opposite end of the planet — a huge, ten-sided wave that was not there just a few years ago."

Saturn is famous for a strange six-sided shape spinning above its north pole — a giant polygon made of clouds. Astronomers have watched that hexagon for more than 40 years. But here is the puzzle: nobody had ever seen a matching shape at the planet's south pole. Then, suddenly, one appeared.

A shape that was not there before

Scientists studying new Hubble Space Telescope images found a huge ten-sided shape — a decagon — circling Saturn's south pole. Each side stretches for thousands of kilometres across the clouds.

The best part? It is brand new. When astronomers looked back through older pictures, the shape only began to appear around 2023, and it has been growing clearer ever since. "The most intriguing part to me is that this seems to have just formed recently," said Amy Simon, a scientist at NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center who worked on the study, published in the journal Science Advances.

An amateur astronomer spotted it first

The story started with ordinary sky-watchers. A team led by Agustín Sánchez-Lavega of the University of the Basque Country runs a website where people around the world send in their own photographs of the planets. In a 2024 picture, he noticed a small, wavy bump near Saturn's south pole. Two amateur astronomers, Trevor Barry and Jean-Paul Oger, saw it too.

That hint was enough to send the researchers hunting through NASA's Hubble archive for a closer look.

Why a ten-sided shape, and why now?

Saturn is wrapped in powerful jet streams — bands of wind that race around the planet, some at about 400 kilometres per hour. The decagon sits inside one of these streams.

The new shape is not just painted on the cloud tops. It stretches down through several layers of Saturn's thick atmosphere, so it is more like a tall, spinning wave than a flat pattern. Strangely, the clues suggest it might have been triggered by a nearby spinning storm (an anticyclone) sitting just to its north, which darkened a couple of years ago — right before the decagon showed up.

Scientists still do not know for sure. Was it the storm's doing? Will the ten-sided wave last decades like Saturn's northern hexagon, or melt away?

Try this at home

You do not need a telescope to see what is going on. Fill a wide bowl with water and drop in a few drops of food colouring. Now spin the water with a spoon, then lift the spoon and let the water settle. Watch how the swirling liquid starts to bunch into repeating shapes around the edge — waves do not like to be messy, so they tidy themselves into patterns. That is the same idea behind Saturn's giant polygons: a fast-moving band of air finding a shape.

Fun fact

Saturn's north-pole hexagon is so large that one side of it is longer than the diameter of Earth. The new southern decagon is not quite as wide — but it has ten sides instead of six, making it the first of its kind scientists have ever caught forming.

For grown-ups

"A study published 2 September 2026 in Science Advances reports a new regularly shaped polar jet feature in Saturn's southern hemisphere: a ten-sided (decagonal) wave at roughly 60.5°S. Lead author Agustín Sánchez-Lavega (University of the Basque Country) first noticed a faint wavy perturbation in a ground-based image submitted to the Planetary Virtual Observatory Laboratory (PVOL) in 2024; amateur astronomers Trevor Barry and Jean-Paul Oger helped confirm it. Co-authors Amy Simon (NASA Goddard, OPAL principal investigator) and Michael Wong (UC Berkeley Space Sciences Laboratory) reconstructed the feature's emergence from Hubble Outer Planet Atmospheres Legacy (OPAL) images going back to 2023. The wave sits inside one of Saturn's east-west jet streams, moving at about 400 km/h, and is three-dimensional — it extends through several atmospheric layers, and its apparent position shifts slightly depending on the wavelength used. Saturn's northern hexagon, discovered in 1980 by Voyager, has persisted for more than 40 years; NASA's Cassini orbiter (2004–2017) found no comparable southern structure. Notably, the decagon lies just south of an anticyclone vortex near 55°S that darkened sharply in 2025, shortly before the decagon emerged, hinting the vortex may have seeded the perturbation. Sánchez-Lavega modelled the pattern with shallow-water equations. Open questions: what triggered the shape, and whether it will stabilise like the northern hexagon or keep changing."
