What is Virgin Galactic's new Delta-class space plane named Apeiron?

A new space plane finds its home

More than two decades after SpaceShipOne won the $10 million X Prize for private spaceflight, Virgin Galactic is back in the spotlight with a brand-new rocket ship. On October 28, 2026, the company ferried its first next-generation Delta-class suborbital space plane from its factory in Mesa, Arizona, to its home base at Spaceport America in New Mexico — firmly attached to the belly of its carrier aircraft, Eve.

The spaceship was given a name chosen by people around the world. More than 80 countries voted in an online contest, and the winning word is Apeiron (pronounced "app-err-on"), drawn from a Greek term meaning "boundless" or "endless."

Built like the originals, upgraded for more flights

The Delta-class craft works on the same clever principles as the original SpaceShipOne and Virgin Galactic's earlier SpaceShipTwo designs. A carrier mothership lifts it high into the sky, then the space plane fires its rocket motor to climb to the edge of space. After a few minutes of weightlessness, it folds up its wings and glides home to a runway landing — no fancy vertical landing required.

Virgin Galactic says this new design is stronger and easier to maintain. The company is aiming for each space plane to make twice-weekly trips to space, with a vehicle built to fly more than 500 times over its lifetime. That is a big jump from the original SpaceShipTwo, whose program was shaped by the 2014 loss of the VSS Enterprise in a test-flight accident and the later retirement of the VSS Unity after more than 30 spacefarers — including founder Richard Branson — rode it to space.

Ready for paying passengers

Now that Apeiron has arrived at Spaceport America, engineers will finish connecting the computers and get the craft ready for its first test flights. Virgin Galactic hopes to begin flying paying customers by mid-2027. Each ticket to the edge of space currently costs about $750,000 — enough to buy most homes in our world.

A second Delta-class ship is already taking shape in Mesa. Virgin Galactic plans to start a small assembly line and hopes that second space plane will enter commercial service before the end of 2027. With its nearest competitor, Blue Origin, pausing its New Shepard suborbital program earlier this year, Virgin Galactic may have the sky (and space) almost to itself for a while.

Fun Fact

Virgin Galactic founder Richard Branson was the first crew member on a commercial SpaceShipTwo flight in July 2021, flying on the same day his 70th birthday. He was also a passenger on a Delta-wing aircraft that attempted — but did not complete — a transatlantic balloon flight in 1987.

Virgin Galactic's Delta-class program represents a shift toward higher flight-rate commercial suborbital spaceflight. The first vehicle, named Apeiron, began its journey to Spaceport America on October 28, 2026, having been rolled out of the Spaceship Factory in Mesa, Arizona. Wikipedia confirms that SpaceShipOne (flown for the 2004 Ansari X Prize) established the air-launch-to-suborbital trajectory that both SpaceShipTwo and the new Delta-class continue to use: release from a mothership at altitude, rocket boost to the 80–100 km boundary of space, then unpowered gliding return. Wikipedia also records that SpaceShipOne's success inspired the creation of Virgin Galactic, and that Blue Origin's New Shepard — Virgin's main suborbital competitor — suspended flights after 17 crewed missions to focus on lunar programs. Each Apeiron customer ticket is currently priced at approximately $750,000, reflecting the premium nature of early commercial space tourism. CEO Michael Colglazier projected commercial Delta-class flights beginning in mid-2027, with a target of twice-weekly operations and a 500-mission vehicle lifetime.
