What happened when the universe's mightiest explosion kept glowing for 27 whole days?

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 12:45 IST
Reading options

Astronomers tracked an ultra-long gamma-ray burst — GRB 220706A — whose engine kept flashing for 27 days, the longest-lasting cosmic explosion of its kind yet seen. The blast, spotted by the Zwicky Transient Facility, may have been powered by a rapidly spinning magnetar born from a collapsing blue supergiant star.

Galaxies are like cosmic gemstones — some glitter with sweeping spiral arms, others are stretched and smashed by crashes with their neighbors. And every once in a while, the universe pulls off its biggest magic trick: an explosion so bright it can be seen in the next galaxy over, and it just keeps going... and going... and going.

A 27-day gamma-ray burst

Deep in galaxy SDSS J1422+4010 — about 4.5 billion light-years away — a massive star ran out of fuel and collapsed in on itself. In a flash, it launched a gamma-ray burst, the most powerful explosion nature can make. But this was no ordinary burst. Where most gamma-ray bursts flare out in seconds to minutes, this one — called GRB 220706A — kept erupting for 27 whole days.

That makes it an ultra-long gamma-ray burst, a rare and still-mysterious cousin of the more common short and long bursts. Think of a typical burst as a firecracker that pops and fades in a heartbeat; an ultra-long burst is like that same firecracker burning steadily for a month.

Why do some bursts last so long?

The team that studied the burst — led by Dr. Michael Gompertz from the University of Birmingham — found a surprise near the scene: a supernova, the spectacular death explosion of the star itself. The supernova told the astronomers that a star had indeed collapsed, but the star's collapse alone can't explain why the gamma-ray jet kept firing for 27 days.

Two puzzles emerged:

  1. A spinning stellar leftover. The collapse may have left behind a magnetar — a city-sized neutron star spinning roughly 1,000 times per second, wrapped in magnetic fields so strong they would flatten atoms. Such a rapidly whirling remnant can keep powering a jet for weeks.
  2. A giant, slow-falling star. To power 27 days of flaring, the team calculated the collapsing star needed to be about 1,000 times wider than our Sun — a blazing blue supergiant. A blue supergiant's outer layers collapse more slowly, so the jet it launches can stay "on" for far longer than a smaller star's.

The hunt for more ultra-long bursts

Finding ultra-long bursts is tricky. Most gamma-ray satellites — like NASA's Swift and the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope — circle Earth in low orbit and are blocked by our planet every 90 minutes or so. That's fine for catching a burst that lasts seconds, but a month-long glow gets chopped into pieces. The burst also shines more in X-rays than in gamma rays, and the next generation of X-ray missions is built to catch exactly that.

The Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF) at Palomar Observatory was the telescope that first spotted GRB 220706A's long afterglow, scanning the sky night after night with its wide-eye camera. Its discovery — paired with observations from space telescopes Chandra, Swift, and the James Webb Space Telescope — gives astronomers their second well-studied ultra-long burst, after the famous GRB 111209A from 2011. With two cases to compare, the team can start testing which of their ideas — the magnetar engine or the blue-supergiant collapse — truly drives these cosmic marathons.

As Dr. Gompertz put it: "The incredibly late flares also strain our models for how jets are powered. It will be fascinating to see how future theoretical work can incorporate a power source from a collapsing star that can last for a month!"

Try this at home: On a dark, clear night, find the brightest fuzzy patch you can see without a telescope. That soft glow is light from billions of stars bundled into one patch — and that patch is another galaxy, like the one that hosted this 27-day explosion. Each one is a whole island of stars, and every so often, one of those stars dies in a blast a million times brighter than our Sun. That's the kind of event that can, for a brief time, outshine an entire galaxy.

For the grown-ups

Gamma-ray bursts are divided into "long" (>2 s, from massive-star collapse) and "short" (<2 s, from neutron-star mergers). Ultra-long GRBs form a debated upper-duration class (>10,000 s ≈ 2.8 days). GRB 220706A reached ~27 days (≈2.3 million seconds) of X-ray flaring — far beyond the classical long-GRB regime. Its host galaxy SDSS J1422+4010 lies at z=0.451. The explosion site also produced supernova SN 2022jfy. The leading central-engine models are a fast-spinning, highly magnetized neutron star (magnetar) and/or a blue-supergiant progenitor (~1,000 R☉) whose larger radius extends the collapse timescale, keeping the relativistic jet active for weeks. Detection bias arises because low-Earth-orbit satellites are Earth-blocked ~every 90 min and ulGRBs emit proportionally more in X-rays than gamma-rays; wide-field X-ray missions (e.g., Einstein Probe) are the optimal hunters. The team's preprint is on arXiv (arXiv:2609.35998).

For grown-ups

"Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are the brightest explosions in the universe since the Big Bang. Most 'long' GRBs last from tens of seconds up to a few hundred seconds, powered by the collapse of a massive star and the birth of a black hole or neutron star. Ultra-long GRBs (ulGRB) represent a rare upper tail, lasting more than ~10,000 seconds (roughly 2.8 days) and extending to weeks. GRB 220706A reached ~27 days of flaring — ~100x longer than a typical long GRB — and was caught in optical light by the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF) at Palomar Observatory, then followed up across the electromagnetic spectrum. Its afterglow brightened alongside supernova SN 2022jfy in galaxy SDSS J1422+4010 at redshift z=0.451 (about 4.5 billion light-years away, when the universe was ~30% of its current age). Dr. Michael Gompertz (University of Birmingham) led the analysis. The leading explanations: (1) a magnetar — a neutron star spinning ~1,000 times per second with a monster magnetic field, left over from the stellar collapse; or (2) a collapsing blue supergiant, a star 1,000x the radius of the Sun whose core takes much longer to fall in on itself, feeding the GRB jet for weeks. Detecting such events is hard: most gamma-ray and X-ray satellites orbit in low Earth orbit and pass behind or ahead of Earth every ~90 minutes, periodically blocking the signal. The James Webb Space Telescope and next-generation X-ray missions are better suited to catch the long, X-ray-bright tail of these explosions. Preprint: arXiv:2609.35998."
