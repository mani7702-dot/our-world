"Why are four planets lining up in the sky before breakfast?"

By Our World Published 17 November 2026 at "21:39" IST
Reading options

"Mercury, Venus, Mars and Jupiter are strung out in a diagonal line in the morning sky this November. You do not need a telescope, just an early alarm and a view of the sunrise side of the sky."

Four planets are hanging in the sky before the Sun comes up this month, and they are arranged in a neat diagonal row.

You do not need a telescope to see any of them. You need a clear view towards the sunrise, a dark-ish spot, and somebody willing to wake you up early.

The line-up, from bottom to top

Look towards the east or south-east about an hour before sunrise.

Mercury is the tricky one. It is small, faint, and it sits very low — almost hugging the horizon — so you need a flat, open view with no houses or trees in the way. It gets a little easier to spot as the month goes on, and around 20 November it climbs as far from the Sun as it gets in the morning sky.

Venus is the easy one. It is the brightest thing in the night sky after the Moon, so it will look like a dazzling white lamp. On 27 November it reaches its brightest point of the whole year.

Higher up and to the right come Mars and Jupiter. Jupiter is a big creamy-white dot. Mars is smaller and slightly orange-red. On the mornings of 15 and 16 November the two of them looked almost like a double star, only about a finger's width apart when you hold your hand out at arm's length. They are still close neighbours in the sky for the rest of the month.

Why do they make a line?

This is the part that surprises people. The planets are not actually lined up in space. They are spread across hundreds of millions of kilometres, each on its own enormous racetrack around the Sun.

But all of those racetracks lie almost flat, like plates stacked on a table. Our whole solar system is a fairly flat disc. So when we look out from Earth, the planets always appear somewhere along the same curved band of sky — the path the Sun and Moon follow too. Astronomers call it the ecliptic.

When several planets happen to be on the same side of the Sun as us at the same time, that band fills up and you get what skywatchers call a planet parade. Nothing is colliding. Nothing is unusual about the planets themselves. We are simply standing in the right spot at the right moment to see several of them at once.

One more giveaway: planets shine with a steady light, while stars twinkle. Stars are so far away that they are true pinpoints, and the moving air above us smears and flickers them. A planet is close enough to show a tiny disc, and that disc smooths the wobble out.

How to actually catch it

  • Set your alarm for 45 to 60 minutes before sunrise. Any later and the sky turns pale blue and the fainter planets vanish. Check your local sunrise time — it is different in every city.
  • Give your eyes about 20 minutes to adjust. No phone screen in your face; the light from a screen makes your eyes less sensitive for several minutes afterwards.
  • Get away from streetlights if you can, and find somewhere with a clear eastern horizon.
  • Binoculars help but are optional. They can bring out faint Mercury, and if you are up early you might also catch Uranus setting in the west.
  • Bonus: the Leonid meteor shower peaks around 17-18 November, so a shooting star might streak past while you are waiting.

The moon joins in later

On 24 November the full moon arrives — the first of three supermoons in a row, when the Moon is a bit closer to Earth than usual and looks slightly bigger and brighter. Skywatchers in the Southern Hemisphere see the same row of planets, but without Mercury, which stays too near the Sun from down there.

Try this at home: hold your arm out straight and look at your little finger. At arm's length, its width covers about one degree of sky — roughly twice the gap between Mars and Jupiter on 16 November. Skywatchers really do measure the sky with their hands: a fist is about ten degrees, and a spread hand about twenty. Try it on the Moon one evening and see how many fingers fit across it.

For grown-ups

"All four bright planets sit close to the ecliptic — the plane of the solar system — so from Earth they always appear along one arc of sky, and when several are above the horizon at once they look like a row. In November 2026 Venus, Mars and Jupiter are spread up the eastern morning sky while Mercury, near greatest western elongation on 20 November, sits low in the same direction; Mars and Jupiter come within roughly 1.2 degrees on 15-16 November, a perspective coincidence rather than a real approach (their true separation is hundreds of millions of kilometres). Venus reaches its greatest brilliancy of 2026 on 27 November, when it is about magnitude -4.7; the full moon of 24 November is the first of three consecutive supermoons (perigee within about 90% of the closest approach). Steady versus twinkling light is atmospheric scintillation: a star is a true point source, so a wobbling air column breaks it into shimmering colours, while a planet's small but real disc averages that wobble out. Southern Hemisphere observers see the same line-up minus Mercury, which stays too close to the Sun there."
