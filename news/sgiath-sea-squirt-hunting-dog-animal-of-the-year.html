Who's the dog that sniffs out an invasive "sea vomit" before it smothers the coast?

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 16:37 IST
Reading options

Sgiath, a black Labrador from the Scottish Highlands, is the UK's first dog trained to track down the invasive carpet sea squirt — a slimy creature nicknamed "sea vomit" that can smother native marine life.

The slimy invader that looks like seaweed

Picture a sticky, slimy sheet spreading across a rocky beach at low tide. It squeezes into cracks, coats seaweed, climbs over oyster cages, and if it finds a quiet spot, it stays — growing, spreading, and slowly smothering everything underneath. That's the carpet sea squirt, and many people just call it "sea vomit."

It doesn't look dangerous. But this little creature is one of the world's most successful marine invaders. Left unchecked, a single colony can blanket hundreds of square metres, crowding out native mussels, barnacles, and algae that local fish and crabs depend on.

A dog's nose knows

Dr Texa Sim is a marine ecologist working in the Scottish Highlands. A few years ago, she and her black Labrador, Sgiath (pronounced "Skee-ah"), were out on the beach together — and an idea popped into her head. Dogs have an incredible sense of smell; could Sgiath learn to track the sea squirt the way she once tracked lost hikers as a mountain-rescue pup?

No one had tried it before. But Sgiath proved to be a natural.

After some training, the Labrador learned to pick out the sea squirt's scent from seaweed, rocks, and plastic pontoons. When the tide goes out, Sgiath and Dr Sim walk the shoreline together, and the dog sniffs out hidden colonies that human eyes almost always miss. She's always right when she points, says Sim, and she's found hundreds of colonies so far.

How a rescue pup became a conservation hero

As a puppy, Sgiath was being trained for mountain rescue. She learned to navigate tough terrain and follow a scent no matter how faint. When Sim wondered whether those same skills could help with marine conservation, Sgiath adapted in a heartbeat.

Conservation dogs are already common on land — tracking tigers, sniffing out poachers, finding rare plants. But the sea is a much trickier place. The smells are different, the ground is wet, and the targets are slippery. Sgiath is thought to be the very first dog in the UK trained specifically to hunt sea squirts.

Why this matters

Invasive species are a leading threat to biodiversity worldwide. In the ocean especially, new arrivals can multiply fast because there are often no natural predators to keep them in check. The carpet sea squirt arrived in UK waters and started spreading quietly — until Sgiath showed people just how widespread it had become.

The International Fund for Animal Welfare (IFAW) recently named Sgiath its Animal of the Year, recognising the real difference a clever dog can make when she teams up with a scientist who listens.

Try this at home

Next time you visit a rocky beach, kneel down and peer under seaweed at low tide. Can you spot anything slimy that doesn't look like a normal seaweed? Take a photo, but don't touch — let an adult help you learn what you found.

Fun Fact

A dog's nose can detect smells that are thousands of times fainter than what humans notice. That's what lets Sgiath find sea squirts hidden under a thin film of seaweed and slime — a super-power we're still learning to use for saving our oceans.
