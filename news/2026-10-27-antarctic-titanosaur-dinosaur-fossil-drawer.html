5 Things a Dinosaur Tail Bone Found in a Drawer Tells Us About Ancient Antarctica

In 1985, a geologist on James Ross Island off the coast of Antarctica sketched a strange fossil into his notebook. He wrote: "vertebra of large reptile." The fossil went into a drawer and stayed there for 40 years—until a museum curator spotted something extraordinary.

It wasn't a marine reptile. It was a dinosaur—the first dinosaur bone ever found on the frozen continent.

1. A Fossil Misidentified for Four Decades

The fossil is a single tail vertebra (tail bone) from a titanosaur—a group of long-necked, plant-eating dinosaurs that included the largest animals to ever walk the Earth. When it was discovered in 1985, the field team thought it belonged to a marine reptile, so it was stored away in the British Antarctic Survey's geology collection in Cambridge. Curator Dr. Mark Evans only realized its true identity decades later when he was cataloging old specimens.

2. Titansaurs: The Giants of the Dinosaur Age

Titanosaurs were the last of the great sauropod dinosaurs. Some species—like Patagotitan and Argentinosaurus—reached 115 feet (35 meters) long and weighed around 60 tonnes. Their bodies were supported by sturdy, columnar legs and they had tiny heads with long necks that helped them reach treetops. Unlike their earlier sauropod cousins, titanosaurs survived until the end-Cretaceous extinction event 66 million years ago.

3. Antarctica Was a Lush Forest 82 Million Years Ago

The tail bone dates to the Late Cretaceous period, about 82 million years ago. At that time, Antarctica was very different from today. Instead of ice sheets kilometers thick, it was covered in lush green forests that provided plenty of vegetation for giant plant-eaters. Scientists believe the Antarctic titanosaur was a modest-sized individual—about 23 feet (7 meters) long—possibly a juvenile or a naturally small adult.

4. Why Antarctica Has So Few Dinosaur Fossils

Even though dinosaurs lived in Antarctica, very few fossils have been found. The continent is covered by massive ice sheets that hide the rock layers where fossils are typically preserved. Today, Antarctica is one of the most challenging places on Earth for paleontologists to work. But the few fossils that are found—like this titanosaur tail bone—help fill in the sparse puzzle of ancient life at the bottom of the world.

5. From Drawer to Peer-Reviewed Paper

The discovery was confirmed by Professor Paul Barrett from London's Natural History Museum, who noted the vertebra has a distinctive ball-and-socket joint pattern unique to titanosaurs. The find was written up in the scientific journal Acta Palaeontologica Polonica. It's a reminder that major discoveries can hide in plain sight—even in museum drawers filled with thousands of specimens.

Try this at home

Look at a sauropod skeleton in a natural history museum. Count how many vertebrae are in the tail. Notice how each bone has a ball on one end and a socket on the other—that's the same joint pattern that helped scientists identify this fossil as a titanosaur.

Fun Fact

Titanosaurs were the last surviving group of long-necked sauropod dinosaurs. They lived on every continent that exists today, including Antarctica, until their extinction alongside the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago.

For grown-ups

The fossil was collected during a 1985 British Antarctic Survey expedition on James Ross Island. It was initially misidentified as a marine reptile vertebra and stored in the BAS geology collection. In 2025, curator Dr. Mark Evans identified it as a titanosaur sauropod tail vertebra—the first confirmed dinosaur bone from Antarctica. The vertebra measures ~10 cm wide and shows the distinctive ball-and-socket articulation unique to titanosaurs. Size estimates suggest the individual was ~7 m (23 ft) long. The study was published in Acta Palaeontologica Polonica.
