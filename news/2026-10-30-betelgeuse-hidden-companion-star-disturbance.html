What Is Betelgeuse's Hidden Companion Star?

The giant star that wobbles

Betelgeuse is the red star you can spot with your naked eye in the constellation Orion — it marks the hunter's shoulder. It is a red supergiant, one of the biggest stars in our sky. If you swapped Betelgeuse for our Sun, it would swallow Mercury, Venus, Earth, and even Mars! But something strange has been puzzling astronomers for years: Betelgeuse's brightness changes, and its atmosphere seems to ripple and wobble in surprising ways.

Scientists used to think Betelgeuse was alone. But new evidence reveals that a smaller "shadow" companion star named Siwarha has been stirring up the giant's atmosphere, creating a mysterious wake of gas and dust.

Who is Siwarha?

Siwarha is a hot, young star — much smaller and brighter than Betelgeuse, but far too faint for us to see directly. It orbits very close to Betelgeuse, swinging around inside the red supergiant's extended outer atmosphere. The distance between them is only a few times the width of Betelgeuse itself!

As Siwarha orbits, its gravity tugs on Betelgeuse's atmosphere. The result is like dropping a pebble in a pond — ripples spread outward. Astronomers call this effect a "trailing wake." The wake stretches behind the companion like a tail, and it helps explain why Betelgeuse's brightness changes over time.

What do the radio telescopes tell us?

A study published in The Astronomical Journal used two of the world's most powerful radio observatories: the Very Large Array (VLA) in New Mexico and the Atacama Large Millimeter-submillimeter Array (ALMA) in Chile. These telescopes can peer through Betelgeuse's hazy atmosphere at radio wavelengths, revealing details that optical telescopes cannot see.

The team found that Betelgeuse appears slightly egg-shaped at certain times of the year — exactly when Siwarha is predicted to be in a specific part of its orbit. This correlation strongly supports the idea that the companion's gravity is distorting the star. The VLA and ALMA observations also show how Betelgeuse's atmosphere has been evolving since the "Great Dimming" of late 2019, when the star faded to about one-third of its usual brightness.

Why does this matter?

Betelgeuse's changing brightness has intrigued astronomers for centuries. It is a semiregular variable star — its light dims and brightens in patterns that are predictable, but not perfectly so. The discovery of Siwarha helps explain those irregularities.

But there is a bigger picture: Betelgeuse is expected to go supernova someday — possibly even in our lifetimes. Understanding its companion and how they interact helps astronomers prepare for that spectacular event, and teaches us how stars evolve, how they lose mass, and how they shape the clouds of gas and dust that will one day form new stars and planets.

Try This at Home

On a clear autumn or winter evening, look for Orion in the southern sky after sunset. Find the bright red star that marks Orion's shoulder — that is Betelgeuse! Over the following weeks and months, notice how its color and brightness seem to change. You can even make a simple "variable star" model: shine a flashlight through a jar of water mixed with a little milk. Tilt and swirl the jar — the light changes as particles move around. It is a tiny model of how a companion star stirs up a giant star's atmosphere.

Fun Fact

The name Siwarha means "her bracelet" in Arabic. It was proposed by astronomers in 2025 and officially approved by the International Astronomical Union's Working Group on Star Names in September 2025. Betelgeuse itself means "the hand of al-Jawzaa" — the arm of the giant in Arabic, referring to its position in Orion's shoulder!

For Grown-ups

Betelgeuse (Alpha Orionis, HD 39801) is a red supergiant at approximately 500–700 light-years from Earth. It is a semiregular variable star (GCVS type SRc) with an apparent magnitude ranging from about 0.0 to 1.6 — the widest range of any first-magnitude star. Wikipedia confirms: "Betelgeuse is a variable star whose visual magnitude ranges between 0.0 and +1.6. There are periods during which it surpasses Rigel to become the sixth brightest star." It also confirms the Great Dimming event of late 2019/early 2020, when the star faded by roughly a factor of 3, and documents the probable companion star Siwarha — named in 2025, officially recognized by the IAU WGSN on 22 September 2025.

The companion is consistent with a low-mass F-type pre-main-sequence star with about 1.6 times the mass of the Sun. Gemini North and VLT/SPHERE observations (2024) detected a point source at an angular separation of 52 milliarcseconds east of north, near the predicted time of greatest elongation (orbital phase ~0.25). The Matthews et al. (2026) IOP study reports VLA+ALMA observations at seven radio frequencies (22 GHz to 485 GHz) spanning 2021–2026, finding a global spectral index α = 1.35 ± 0.03 and a direct correlation between Betelgeuse's radio-measured ellipticity and the companion's orbital phase — confirming periodic atmospheric perturbations. Siwarha's gravitational pull concentrates Betelgeuse's surrounding stellar wind, creating a trailing and expanding wake. Spectroscopic evidence from Dupree et al. (2026, ApJ) identified variability in circumstellar Mn I absorption lines matching the 2,170-day (5.94-year) secondary periodicity, further supporting the companion hypothesis.
