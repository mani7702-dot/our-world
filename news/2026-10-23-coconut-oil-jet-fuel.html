10 Ways Coconut Oil Could Power Your Next Flight — and Other Uses for the Miracle Nut

You already know coconut oil is great for frying, baking, and that one sibling who puts it in their coffee. What you probably don't know is that scientists in Japan just proved it could also help fuel an airplane — and not just any airplane, but a real jet engine.

The big idea: waste oil into jet fuel

Chemists at Osaka Metropolitan University asked a simple question: what if we took the coconut oil that's thrown away after making coconut milk, oil, and cosmetics, and turned it into fuel? Instead of using the high-pressure, high-heat factories that normally make biofuel, they used a gentler "co-solvent" method — mixing the leftover coconut oil with a little acetone and an alcohol, all at room temperature.

That matters because gentler chemistry uses less energy, which means the resulting fuel has a smaller carbon footprint before it even touches an airplane tank. Then they blended it with regular jet fuel at ratios from 10% coconut oil up to a half-and-half 50-50 mix, and tested the blends in a real micro jet engine.

How did it do in the engine?

The coconut-oil blends performed impressively. Their thermal efficiency — basically how much bang you get per sip — was comparable to pure kerosene, the fuel airlines already use. Levels of carbon dioxide and nitrogen compounds stayed about the same across every mix, so the engine wasn't spewing extra greenhouse gases.

The real standout was cleaner combustion: the blends cut unburned hydrocarbons (tiny pollution particles called UHCs) by between 5% and 40%, with the stronger coconut blends cleaning up the most. For a quick kid-translated version: more coconut oil in the mix = fewer invisible pollutants coming out the back.

There's one homework note left. At higher coconut blends, carbon monoxide ticked up slightly — a reminder that ground crews around airports would need safety monitoring before 100% coconut flights took off. The scientists say that's the last mountain to climb before your next trip could run partly on tropical leftovers.

The bonus: nothing goes to waste

The coolest part isn't just the jet fuel. The same coconut-oil process also creates glycerin and biodiesel as side products, and the solvents get recovered and reused. That's a full-circle factory: one batch of coconuts could give you jet fuel, skin-care glycerin, and diesel all at once, with the chemicals recycled back to start the next batch.

Try this at home

You can't build a jet engine in your kitchen, but you can explore coconut oil's powers safely. Freeze a tablespoon of coconut oil and a tablespoon of olive oil side by side — the coconut oil stays solid at room temperature (it melts around 76°F) while olive oil stays liquid. That difference in melting points is a tiny window into why coconut oil behaves differently as a fuel. Draw both on a plate, label them, and you've modeled the same property that makes coconut oil a candidate for engines in cooler climates.

Fun fact

One mature coconut palm can produce 10,000 to 15,000 coconuts per year, and the world harvests roughly 60 million tons of coconuts annually. Even if only the leftover oil after food and cosmetics went into fuel, it would be enough to fill thousands of aircraft — which is exactly why researchers are treating every drop like liquid opportunity instead of kitchen scrap.

For grown-ups

Chemists at Osaka Metropolitan University tested whether coconut oil — a low-cost waste product left over from commercial coconut-oil extraction — could become a sustainable aviation fuel. They used a co-solvent method (coconut oil + 10% acetone + an alcohol such as methylene or ethylene chloride) that works at room temperature and lower heat, avoiding the high pressures and intense heating normally needed to make biofuel from other feedstocks — which shrinks the fuel's carbon footprint before blending. They blended the coconut biofuel with conventional kerosene at ratios from 10% biofuel-90% kerosene up to an even 50-50, then poured it into a micro jet engine and ignited it. The mixture delivered thermal efficiency comparable to pure kerosene, with carbon dioxide and nitric-oxide emissions staying on par with kerosene across all blends. The biggest win was in unburned hydrocarbons (UHCs/VOCs such as butane): depending on the blend, these fell between 5% and 40%, with larger biofuel fractions cutting more. Byproducts included glycerin and biodiesel, and the acetone/alcohol reagents were recovered for reuse. The team says this is the first-ever experiment turning coconuts into a tested jet fuel, though an observed rise in carbon monoxide at higher blends means more work is needed before commercial flights could run on coconut — the CO jump could be a concern for ground crews near airports.
