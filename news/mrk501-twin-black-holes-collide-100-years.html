Could two giant black holes crash together in just 100 years?

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 20:30 IST
Reading options

Astronomers watching galaxy Mrk 501 for 23 years have found a hidden second black hole sweeping around the first — and the two giants may merge within a human lifetime.

A galaxy with two dancing giants

At the heart of a distant galaxy called Mrk 501 — about 400 million light-years away — something incredible is unfolding. Two supermassive black holes, each weighing between 100 million and a billion times our Sun's mass, are locked in a slow cosmic dance. They circle each other once every 121 days, and they may smash together in as little as 100 years.

That is an eyeblink in space terms — and scientists are watching it happen.

The second jet that gave it away

Astronomers have known for a long time that Mrk 501's central black hole shoots a powerful jet of particles into space at nearly the speed of light. This jet acts like a lighthouse beam, and because it points almost straight at Earth, it makes the galaxy shine exceptionally bright.

But in 2022, something strange happened. As they watched the jet over time, scientists noticed a second jet emerging from behind the main one, sweeping counterclockwise across the sky. The whole pattern repeated over and over — like a lighthouse with two rotating beams instead of one.

The explanation? A second, invisible black hole is orbiting the first, dragging the whole jet system with it as the pair swing around each other.

Racing toward a collision

After tracking the dance for 23 years using radio telescopes, the team calculated that the two black holes are spiraling inward. They are currently separated by 250 to 540 times the distance between Earth and the Sun — tiny by galactic standards.

As they get closer, they will lose energy in the form of gravitational waves — ripples in the fabric of spacetime itself. Future space-based observatories should be able to detect these waves as the black holes spiral ever tighter, producing a characteristic "chirp" signal.

A rare cosmic fireworks show

Supermassive black hole mergers happen in galaxies across the universe, but they are almost impossible to catch in the act. Most pairs are too far apart to observe, or too hidden in distant galaxies to study. Mrk 501 is special: it is close enough, and its double jets make the invisible visible.

Try this at home: On a dark night, find the brightest patch of "sky fog" you can see without a telescope — that soft glow is the combined light of billions of stars bundled into one small spot. Every such patch is likely a whole galaxy, each with its own central black hole. And somewhere out there, two giants may already be racing toward their final embrace.

Fun fact: The June 2022 alignment created an "Einstein ring" — a cosmic illusion where the black hole bent the light from the second jet into a perfect circle around itself, like a glowing halo in space.

For grown-ups

A team led by Silke Britzen at the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy tracked radio observations of Mrk 501 over 23 years. They discovered a second jet of particles pointing in a different direction than the known jet. The orbital period is about 121 days with a separation of 250-540 AU, and the black holes (100M-1B solar masses each) could merge in as little as 100 years, producing gravitational waves detectable by pulsar timing arrays (PTAs). Accepted to MNRAS; DOI 10.1093/mnras/stafinal.
