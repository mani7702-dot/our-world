"Is whole milk really worse for you than skimmed milk?"

By Our World Published 12 November 2026 at 19:40 IST
Reading options

"For years the advice was simple: pick the low-fat milk. A new 12-week trial found that adults on whole milk did not put on weight or raise their cholesterol — and ate more calcium and protein."

Is whole milk really worse for you than skimmed milk?

Stand in a supermarket milk aisle and the labels do the arguing for you: skimmed, 1%, 2%, whole. For decades the quiet rule has been "go for the watery one" — full-fat milk, cheese and yogurt were lumped in with chips and butter as things to be careful with.

A new Canadian trial has muddied that. Adults who drank and ate three servings of full-fat dairy every day for 12 weeks did not gain more weight, did not raise their cholesterol, and did not slide towards diabetes. Some of them ended up with better blood pressure.

Why milk got a bad name

The worry was never milk itself. It was one ingredient: saturated fat, which full-fat dairy has more of. Saturated fat can push up LDL cholesterol, the kind that clogs arteries over years.

That is a fair worry, and it is why so many countries told people to choose low-fat dairy. The catch is that the advice was based on the fat, not on the food. Milk is not a bag of fat with a handle — it is protein, sugar, calcium, vitamins and water all packaged together, and your body handles the whole package differently than it handles any one part.

What the scientists actually did

Researchers at the University of Toronto took 74 adults and split them into three groups for three months:

  • one group ate very little dairy and cut 500 calories a day;
  • one group ate three servings of full-fat dairy daily and trimmed other food to stay level;
  • one group ate three servings of full-fat dairy daily and changed nothing else.

Full-fat milk, yogurt and cheese were handed out by the researchers, so nobody had to guess what "full fat" meant.

After 12 weeks, the three groups were compared. No meaningful difference in body weight, body shape, cholesterol, blood sugar or resting metabolism. The dairy groups came out ahead on blood pressure, and got noticeably more calcium, protein and vitamin D — the building blocks for bones and muscles.

The squishy explanation: food is not a sum of parts

The lead scientist, Harvey Anderson, has a theory for why. Milk holds two proteins, casein and whey, tangled up with the fat and the nutrients. That tangle slows how fast your gut releases everything, so a glass of whole milk lands differently than the same amount of fat eaten alone.

He calls it keeping the food whole. His own advice, after all the measuring, is almost boring: "Keep it simple, eat a variety of foods and not too much of anything."

The bits to be careful with

Three months is not a lifetime. Seventy-four people is a small study. Everyone in it was an adult carrying extra weight, so it does not automatically speak for a ten-year-old or a marathon runner. And the study measured numbers on a chart, not heart attacks thirty years later.

So: whole milk is not a villain. It is also not a magic potion. It is milk.

Try this at home

Line up three glasses: skimmed, semi-skimmed and whole. Pour a splash of each into a spoon and look at how it coats. Then taste. Notice which one feels thickest in your mouth and which one tastes sweetest — fat carries flavour, which is why skimmed milk tastes flatter. Ask whoever shops in your house why they buy the one they buy.

Takeaway: weak evidence says nothing good about a food, but it is worth checking whether the evidence is about the food or about one number inside it.
