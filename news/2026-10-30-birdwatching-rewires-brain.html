"Why birdwatching might be the best brain exercise you've never tried?"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 10:30 IST
Reading options

"Scientists have discovered that the keen eye and sharp memory needed to spot and name birds actually strengthens the brain in the same way that exercise strengthens muscles."

Why birdwatching might be the best brain exercise you've never tried?

1. A hobby that trains your brain

When you watch birds, your brain is working hard. You have to spot a tiny flash of colour, listen for a familiar song, and decide fast: Is that a sparrow or a finch? Scientists used to think that only happened in movies. But a real study found that people who are good at birdwatching have brains that work a little differently — in a very good way.

2. The brain changes when you practice

Dr. Erik Wing at Baycrest Academy in Toronto studied 29 expert birdwatchers and 29 beginner birdwatchers. Using special brain scans called MRI, he looked at the parts of the brain that handle attention and seeing things clearly.

The experts showed something exciting: their brains were more neatly organized in those areas, kind of like how a well-kept garden grows better plants. This kind of brain change is called neuroplasticity — the brain's superpower that lets it get better at whatever you practice, even as you get older.

3. Fun for your mind, good for your heart

Another study of over 3,600 people who feed birds and watch them found that most said looking at birds made them feel happier and calmer. So birdwatching helps your brain stay sharp AND it makes you smile — a perfect combination.

4. Try this at home

You don't need special equipment to start! Just sit quietly in your backyard or a local park and watch for birds. Try to notice:

  • What colour is the bird?
  • Is it big or small?
  • What does its song sound like?
  • What part of the yard does it visit?

Even spotting five different birds in a week makes you a mini birdwatcher — and your brain will thank you for it.

Fun Fact

The world's largest flock of a single bird species was a group of over 700,000 snow geese — all spotted by a single birder in Idaho! And some expert birders can identify over 500 bird species just by their songs.

Sources: Wing et al., "The Tuned Cortex: Convergent Expertise-Related Structural and Functional Remodeling across the Adult Lifespan," Journal of Neuroscience (2026); Langhans et al., "It's a good reason to get up every morning!": Perspectives on how feeding and observing birds affects people's wellbeing," Ambio (2026); Wikipedia, "Birdwatching" and "Neuroplasticity."

For grown-ups

"A 2026 study by Erik Wing and colleagues at Baycrest Academy for Research and Education in Toronto scanned 29 expert birdwatchers (ages 24–75) and 29 beginner birdwatchers using diffusion-weighted MRI. Experts showed lower mean diffusivity in frontoparietal regions — the brain's attention and visual-perception areas — meaning their brain tissue was more tightly organized through practice. A separate study of 3,658 bird feeders and watchers in the USA and Canada found that people who feed and observe birds report positive impacts on their mental wellbeing. The effect is domain-specific: the brain remodels in the exact circuits used for bird identification, not just generally. Note: birdwatching expertise develops over years of practice; casual observation also confers benefits, but the structural changes measured by Wing et al. were found in long-term experts."
