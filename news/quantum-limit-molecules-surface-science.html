How sharp is a crystal's cleaning trick? Scientists hit the ultimate quantum limit

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 19:30 IST
Reading options

A Max Planck team cooked up an ultra-clean crystal surface that lets molecules keep their quantum weirdness for as long as physics allows — the first time any molecule glued to a surface has reached that fundamental ceiling.

The quantum ceiling

In the quantum world, things can be in two places at once and talk to each other without even touching. But that delicate "quantumness" is fragile — it lasts only as long as the system stays quiet. That time limit is called coherence time, and physics sets a hard ceiling on it: the Fourier limit, which is fixed by how fast a quantum emitter can spit out a single particle of light.

For years, molecules glued to a surface never got close. The surface was always a little dirty, a little shaky, and the molecule's quantum coherence collapsed long before the ceiling.

The crystal that cleans itself

Scientists at the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light found a clever workaround. They started with an organic crystal and let its top layers slowly evaporate — a process called sublimation, where a solid turns straight into gas. As the layers boiled off, they swept away every stray contaminant like a self-cleaning oven made of science.

Then they chilled the crystal to just a hair above absolute zero, the coldest temperature anything can get. The icy stillness froze the surface in place, and they nudged single molecules onto it one by one.

Molecules hit the ceiling

With the surface clean enough, the molecules did something no one had seen before: they kept their quantum properties alive for exactly the maximum time physics allows. They hit the Fourier limit dead-on.

That means the environment around each molecule was so quiet and stable that it could not steal the molecule's quantumness any faster than the universe itself demands. It is the first time any molecule sitting on a surface has touched that fundamental ceiling.

The team also noticed the surface gently nudged the molecules into tidy rows and tweaked their energy levels — giving scientists a new way to steer single quantum emitters with atomic-scale control.

Try this at home

Place an ice cube on a flat plate in a warm room. Watch (and listen) as the edges melt and refreeze — the crystal surface you see changing is doing its own tiny version of sublimation, shedding impurities as it shifts. You will not reach the quantum limit, but you will be watching the same idea: a clean boundary reshaping a surface.

Fun Fact

The researchers' "self-cleaning" crystal works because organic crystals can sublimate at room temperature — they skip the liquid stage entirely, going straight from solid to gas. If they ever made a quantum fridge, it would probably run on the same trick.

For grown-ups

"Quantum emitters are tiny light-sources, and their 'coherence time' is how long they stay quantum; the Fourier limit is the absolute ceiling set by how fast they can emit a photon. The Max Planck team (Shkarin et al., Science 2026, doi:10.1126/science.aeg5014) grew an organic crystal that 'self-cleans' by sublimation down to a few Kelvin, then deposited single molecules onto the pristine surface and measured coherence times that consistently hit that ceiling — proving the surface environment was quiet enough not to scramble the molecules' quantum state."
