World's Oldest Shipwreck Found Off Turkey — 4,700-Year-Old Cargo Still Inside

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 02:56 IST
Reading options

Archaeologists exploring the seafloor near Kekova Island in Turkey discovered a 4,700-year-old shipwreck — the oldest ever found — carrying amphorae that still held traces of their original cargo.

What did people trade across the Mediterranean 4,700 years ago?

Imagine a boat made of wood and rope, floating on sparkling blue water. Now imagine that boat sank more than 4,700 years ago — and someone just found it. That is exactly what archaeologists did off the coast of Turkey, and the treasures they pulled up still held secrets from the ancient world.

1. A ship that is older than the pyramids

The shipwreck sits just 30 metres (about three swimming pools) beneath the waves, near a tiny island called Kekova in the southern part of Turkey. The archaeologists who found it say the ship was about 10 metres long — roughly the length of a small bus — and was carrying a heavy load across the sea.

Wooden ships almost never survive underwater for thousands of years, so the boat itself had rotted away long ago. But the cargo did not disappear. More than 30 clay pots called amphorae were scattered across the seafloor like a trail of buried treasure.

2. Pots that still hold their secrets

Here is the amazing part: some of the amphorae were still sealed tight after 4,700 years. When the divers opened them, they found a goopy, glossy substance coating the inside. Dr Karin Sowada, an archaeologist from Macquarie University, said she nearly fainted when she saw how much of the residue was still there.

"It wasn't just spots," she said. "A lot of this material was still remaining."

The team thinks the goo might be olive oil, wine, or sticky tree resins — the kinds of liquids that ancient sailors carried across the sea. They are still working on testing exactly what it was.

3. How the pots told the date

Because the ship's wood was gone, the archaeologists could not use the usual radiocarbon dating trick (which needs organic material). Instead, they used something even cleverer: pottery style.

Just like fashion changes over time, the shapes and decorations of ancient pottery change too. The Kekova amphorae have a special look — two handles, a ridge in the middle, and a flat base — that matches pots found in Egypt and the Middle East. Those foreign pots are already known to be about 4,400–4,700 years old.

"Comparing the decoration techniques… we can definitely say our wreck is Early Bronze Age. This is 100%," said Prof Hakan Öniz from Akdeniz University, who led the expedition.

That makes it the oldest shipwreck ever discovered — older even than the famous Uluburun shipwreck found farther north in Turkey.

4. What the cargo tells us about ancient trade

The amphorae were not local to Kekova. Their style links them to the eastern Mediterranean, which means the ship was traveling between busy ancient ports. The researchers believe it set off from somewhere along the eastern coast and was headed either to the city of Troy in north-western Turkey or to the Minoan people on the island of Crete.

For the passengers waiting back home, that journey never ended. The ship simply vanished beneath the waves — taking their goods, their pots, and their story down to the seafloor, where it waited in silence for someone to find it thousands of years later.

Try this at home

Next time you visit a kitchen store, look at the glass jars with two handles (pottery crocks). Ancient people used the same idea 4,700 years ago. Try drawing an amphora on paper — give it two handles, a ridge in the middle, and a flat bottom. Then imagine what you would fill it with for a long sea voyage!

Fun Fact

The amphorae from this wreck are so old that they were being made at roughly the same time as the Great Pyramid of Giza was rising in Egypt. While pyramid builders hauled stone blocks up ramps, sailors were loading olive oil and wine into clay pots for voyages across the Mediterranean.

For the grown-ups

The world's oldest known shipwreck was discovered off Kekova Island, Turkey, in the Early Bronze Age (c. 2700–2400 BCE). The wooden hull did not survive, but over 30 clay amphorae were recovered from the seabed at a depth of about 30 metres. Some amphorae still contained viscous organic residue, which Dr Karin Sowada (Macquarie University) described as remarkably well-preserved. The amphorae style — two handles, central ridge, flat base — matches contemporaneous pottery from Egypt and the Levant, confirming a date of 4,400–4,700 years ago and a ship engaged in long-distance eastern Mediterranean trade. The wreck is owned by the Turkish Ministry of Culture and will be displayed in the new Mediterranean Underwater Archaeology Museum in Demre. Wikipedia's Kekova article independently confirms: "In 2026, an Early Bronze Age shipwreck was discovered off the coast of Kekova. Dating back 4,700 years, to 2700-2400 BC, it is the oldest known shipwrecks in the world."

The short version: archaeologists from the Mediterranean Archaeology Association (Akdeniz University, Turkey) discovered an Early Bronze Age shipwreck off Kekova Island in Antalya Province, Turkey. The wreck dates to approximately 2700–2400 BCE (4,700 years ago) and is the oldest known shipwreck in the world. The wooden hull did not survive, but more than 30 clay amphorae were scattered on the seafloor, some still containing viscous residue that may be olive oil, wine, or tree resins. The amphorae style matches contemporaneous pottery from Egypt and the Levant, confirming the ship was engaged in long-distance Mediterranean trade. The cargo likely traveled from the eastern Mediterranean to Troy or Minoan Crete.
