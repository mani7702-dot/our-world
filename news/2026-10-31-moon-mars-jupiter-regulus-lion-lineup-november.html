"Why Are the Moon, Mars and Jupiter Lining Up in the Lion's Heart?"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at "19:05" IST
Reading options

"In the first nights of November, the waning Moon, red Mars, bright Jupiter and the star Regulus will queue up in a glowing line in the eastern sky. Here's how to spot the 'dance of the planets' — and why it happens."

Imagine looking east an hour after midnight and seeing four bright lights lined up like beads on a string. That is exactly what the sky has planned for the first nights of November.

Look east an hour after midnight on November 1–2 and you will see the Moon, the red planet Mars, giant Jupiter, and a brilliant blue-white star called Regulus — all queued in a glowing row leading down toward the horizon.

The line-up, one step at a time

Start with the Moon. It will be a 45% lit waning crescent — half of its face shining, half in shadow.

Just under 5 degrees away glows Mars, the rusty-red one. If you stretch your arm out and hold up your three middle fingers, that gap is about the size of the space between them. It is a handy trick for measuring the night sky.

Just below Mars, Jupiter shines far brighter — the third-brightest object in the night sky after the Moon and Venus. And beyond Jupiter sits Regulus, the "heart" of the constellation Leo the Lion.

A hidden extra target

Around the same time, look about 10 degrees from the dark, unlit side of the Moon. You may spot a faint fuzzy patch of light: the Beehive Cluster (also called M44), a swarm of around 1,000 stars in the constellation Cancer. It has been known since ancient times, and under dark skies you can just make it out with your own eyes.

A disappearing act

For skywatchers in Australia, Indonesia and nearby island nations, there is a special bonus. On November 2, the Moon will pass directly in front of Jupiter and hide it — an event called an occultation. A whole planet, switched off like a light!

Then, by moonrise on November 3, the Moon will have leapt past both planets. US viewers will see it sitting just 1 degree from Regulus, while people in parts of South America will watch the Moon cover the star itself.

Try this at home

You do not need a telescope for any of this — just your eyes and dark, open sky. Use the finger method: hold your hand out at arm's length. Three middle fingers together roughly cover 5 degrees of sky, and your closed fist covers about 10 degrees. See if you can measure the gaps between the Moon, Mars, Jupiter and Regulus all by yourself.

Fun fact

Regulus looks tiny next to the Moon, but that is only because it is around 79 light-years away. This "little star" is actually a quadruple star system — four stars locked in pairs — and it is up to four times wider than our Sun. Its name means "little king" in Latin, which is why it marks the heart of the Lion.

For grown-ups

"This is an observational skywatching event, not a research claim. A 45%-lit waning crescent Moon sits under 5 degrees from Mars an hour after midnight on Nov 1-2, with Jupiter just below Mars and the bright star Regulus (Alpha Leonis, ~79 light-years away) completing the line toward the eastern horizon; the Beehive Cluster (M44) lies ~10 degrees from the unlit lunar limb in Cancer. On Nov 2 (~3:45 p.m. EST / 2045 GMT) the Moon occults Jupiter for observers in Australia, Indonesia and nearby island nations, and by moonrise on Nov 3 the crescent sits ~1 degree from Regulus for US viewers, with a Regulus occultation beginning ~6:59 EST (1024 GMT) across Brazil, Peru, Argentina and Bolivia. Timings and exact visibility depend on location. Regulus is actually a quadruple star system; the Moon only looks biggest because it is close — it is less than half Mars's diameter, and Jupiter is over 20 times wider than Mars, while Regulus is up to four times the Sun's diameter. Sources: Space.com (Anthony Wood), Star Walk, Popular Science, Wikipedia."
