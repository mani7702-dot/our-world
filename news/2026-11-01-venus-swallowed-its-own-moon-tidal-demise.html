"Why Is Venus the Only Rocky Planet With No Moon?"

By Our World Published 01 November 2026 at "03:45" IST
Reading options

"Venus is almost exactly the same size as Earth, so why does it have no moon? A new computer study suggests a surprising answer: Venus may have had one, then slowly swallowed it."

Our Solar System has a lot of moons. Jupiter has dozens. Saturn has more than a hundred. Even tiny Mars has two little potato-shaped companions. So there is a puzzle sitting right next door to Earth: Venus, a planet almost exactly the same size and mass as our own, has no moon at all.

Scientists have wondered about this for years. Now a new computer study from the University of California, Riverside suggests a surprising idea: Venus may once have had a moon — and then slowly pulled it back in.

A moon that drifts the wrong way

Earth's Moon is slowly moving away from us, about 4 centimetres every year. Astronauts on Apollo 11 left special mirrors on the lunar surface, and scientists still bounce laser beams off them to measure that drift.

Why does our Moon move outward? Because Earth spins quickly — one turn roughly every 24 hours. That spin acts a little like a hand swinging a ball on a string, gently pushing the Moon farther away over time.

Venus is completely different. It turns incredibly slowly: one Venus day lasts about 243 Earth days. Because Venus spins so sluggishly, a moon around it would not drift outward like ours does. Instead, the planet's gravity would slowly reel it inward, until the two eventually collided.

What the simulations showed

Astrophysicist Stephen Kane built computer models of how a planet and its moon tug on each other with gravity. First he tested the model on Earth and the Moon, to make sure it behaved realistically. Then he swapped in Venus and tried moons ranging from half the Moon's mass up to ten times bigger.

Across almost every scenario, the result was the same. "I thought surely the broad range of scenarios I was exploring would lead to a variety of results," Kane said. "But it all went pretty much in the same direction." The moon would crash into Venus — and the heavier the moon, the sooner it happened.

This is still a mystery, not a fact

It is important to say clearly: the study does not prove Venus ever had a moon. It only shows that if one formed, it could not have survived forever. Finding proof would be hard, because Venus has completely repaved its own surface — about 80% of the planet looks the same age, as if a giant volcanic resurfacing event wiped the slate clean around a billion years ago. Any old footprints could be buried deep inside the planet instead.

The idea matters because Venus is our closest neighbour, and it may once have been far more Earth-like. A moon crashing in would have delivered enormous energy and could have reshaped the planet's spin, geology and climate.

Try this at home

You can feel the tug of gravity that shapes moons and planets. Tie a small coin to a piece of string and swing it gently in a circle. If you swing slowly, the coin droops and drifts inward toward your hand. Speed up, and it flies outward into a wider circle. Fast-spinning planets fling their moons outward; slow-spinning ones pull them in. You have just modelled the whole idea in your kitchen!

Fun fact: If Venus's moon really did spiral in, the collision would have been spectacular — a mountain-sized or even Moon-sized object slamming into the planet and possibly changing its entire history.

For grown-ups

"A study led by UC Riverside astrophysicist Stephen R. Kane, with co-authors Franck Selsis, Jérémy Leconte and Sean N. Raymond, published in The Astrophysical Journal (2026, vol. 1009, issue 1, article 31; DOI 10.3847/1538-4357/ae9d6c), titled 'Tidal Demise: The Evolution and Fate of a Hypothetical Venus Moon'. Kane ran N-body/tidal-evolution simulations, first calibrating them against the known Earth–Moon system, then applying them to Venus with hypothetical moons of 0.5 to 10 lunar masses and varying Venus rotation rates. In most scenarios the moon spiralled inward and collided with the planet; more massive moons met that fate faster. Venus rotates retrograde once per ~243 Earth days, far slower than Earth's ~24 hours, which reverses the usual tidal migration. The paper does not claim Venus ever had a moon — only that if it did, a moon could not have survived indefinitely. Earth's Moon recedes ~3.8 cm/yr, measured via Apollo 11 retroreflectors."
