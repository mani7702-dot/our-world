Why Are Room-Temperature Superconductors So Hard?

By Our World Published 18 November 2026 at 18:55 IST
Reading options

Zero-loss electricity sounds like magic. Scientists found that one of the two ingredients already works at room temperature - and the other one is the real bottleneck.

A cable with no waste

Imagine a power line that carries electricity forever without getting warm. No sparks, no heat, nothing lost. That is what a superconductor does, and hospitals already use them in MRI scanners.

The catch: they only work when very cold. The best ones need to be chilled with liquid nitrogen, and some need almost absolute zero, which is colder than deep space.

Two jobs, not one

Superconductivity needs two things at once. First the electrons pair up. Then all the pairs must march in step, like one giant choir singing the same note. Scientists call that phase coherence.

Until now, nobody knew exactly where the pairing starts. That is the question a team in the Netherlands and Germany set out to answer.

Listening to electrons

The team studied a copper-based crystal that becomes superconducting at about 90 kelvin, roughly minus 183 Celsius. They measured tiny electrical fizz, called shot noise, across the material at temperatures close to absolute zero.

Shot noise is a way to count how electrons travel together. If they move in pairs, the charge doubles. The team watched that charge double at one particular energy, the pseudogap. So the pseudogap is where pairing begins.

The surprising part

The pairing energy they measured is about 70 meV. That is far bigger than the energy tied to the 90 kelvin transition. Put simply, the pairs can form at temperatures way above the point where the material actually superconducts.

What stops them is the choir. The pairs cannot all sing the same note once the crystal gets warm. Phase coherence breaks before pairing does, which is why a room-temperature superconductor is hard to build.

Shooting straight: the scientists did not make a room-temperature superconductor. They found out where the first half of the problem is solved, so the second half can be tackled directly.

Try this at home

Try the choir test. Have a group hum one steady note together. Easy, right? Now ask half of them to start on a different note and keep going. The sound turns messy fast. That mess is what losing coherence sounds like.

For grown-ups

Science, 5 Nov 2026, vol 394, pp 682-685, DOI 10.1126/science.adt3444. Utrecht, Leiden and LMU Munich. They used local shot-noise spectroscopy on a bismuth cuprate. The effective charge from the noise rises to 2e at the pseudogap energy. That means the pseudogap marks the onset of Cooper pairing, not a charge-density-wave order. Pairing energy reaches about 70 meV, while Tc is only about 90 K. So pairs can form well above Tc; the bottleneck is phase coherence. Caveat: all data were taken near absolute zero; room-temperature pairing is inferred from the energy scale, and no room-temperature superconductor was made.
