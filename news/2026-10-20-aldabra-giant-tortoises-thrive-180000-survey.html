How 180,000 Giant Tortoises Came Back From Fewer Than 1,000

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 17:05 IST
Reading options

"On a far-off atoll in the Indian Ocean, Aldabra giant tortoises have made a comeback so big that scientists counted nearly 180,000 of them — a triumph of protection that began in the 19th century."

How 180,000 Giant Tortoises Came Back From Fewer Than 1,000

On a ring of coral islands in the Indian Ocean, something amazing walks slowly through the sunshine: 180,000 giant tortoises, minding their own business and proving that protection works.

One of Earth's loneliest giants

The Aldabra atoll is a crescent of islands about 1,000 kilometers (620 miles) east of Madagascar. It is one of only two places left where giant tortoises roam free in the wild — the other is the Galápagos, far to the west across the Pacific.

These Aldabra giant tortoises are true gentle giants. Adults can weigh more than 250 kilograms (550 pounds) and live over 100 years. Their domed shells sweep up like tiny mountains, and their necks stretch out like cranes when they reach for the sweetest leaves.

A story of near-loss and return

In the 1800s, sailors and settlers hunted the tortoises heavily, and cats, rats, and goats arrived as unwanted guests. By the late 1800s, fewer than 1,000 Aldabra tortoises remained.

Then the atoll became a strict nature reserve in 1981 and a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1982. No one lives there permanently. No one hunts there. The tortoises simply got left alone.

The big count

Between 2018 and 2022, scientists led by the Seychelles Islands Foundation and the University of Exeter counted every tortoise they could find — then used math to fill in the spots the dense vegetation hid. Their result, published in the journal Ecology and Evolution: about 183,000 adult and sub-adult tortoises.

That is higher than the entire human population of the Seychelles (about 100,000 people). It is also the highest number ever recorded for the species.

What comes next?

The good news has one challenge: climate is changing, and longer, drier droughts could mean less food and shade. Invasive mammals — especially feral cats and black rats — still sneak onto parts of the atoll and snack on tortoise eggs and babies.

The plan? Remove every cat and rat, then watch the population climb even higher.

Try this at home

Next time you're in a park, count the birds, squirrels, or butterflies you see. Then imagine counting 183,000 animals — all because people decided to keep their world safe. That is the power of a protected place.

Fun Fact

Aldabra giant tortoises can survive for weeks without water. They get most of their moisture from the plants they eat — and when rain is scarce, they can even dig a small hole to reach cooler, damper soil.

For grown-ups

"The Aldabra giant tortoise (Aldabrachelys gigantea) is endemic to the Seychelles. In the 19th century, hunting and introduced species reduced the population to fewer than 1,000 on Aldabra Atoll. After the atoll became a strict nature reserve in 1981 (and a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1982), the population recovered to an estimated ~183,000 adult and sub-adult tortoises — the world's largest free-roaming giant tortoise population. This was confirmed by a five-year survey (2018–2022) led by the Seychelles Islands Foundation and the University of Exeter, published in Ecology and Evolution. Wikipedia independently notes the atoll is home to 'some 180,000 giant tortoises,' and conservationists plan to eradicate invasive mammals (cats, rats) that prey on eggs and hatchlings to help the population keep growing."
