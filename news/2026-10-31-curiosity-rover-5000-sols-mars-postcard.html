What Did NASA's Curiosity Rover Send Home After 5,000 Days on Mars?

For more than 14 Earth years, a car-sized robot named Curiosity has been rolling across Mars. In late August 2026, it quietly passed a huge milestone: its 5,000th Martian day on the Red Planet. To celebrate, the rover turned its cameras toward the landscape and sent home a special picture — a "postcard" of Mars.

A day that lasts a little longer than ours

A Martian day is called a sol, and it lasts about 24 hours and 39 minutes — a little longer than an Earth day. That means 5,000 sols is really more than 5,137 Earth days, or over 14 years of work. Curiosity landed inside a giant bowl called Gale Crater on 6 August 2012, and it has been exploring ever since.

Two pictures, stitched into one

The rover had parked at a sandy ridge nicknamed "Chocolatal." There, it used its navigation cameras to photograph the same view at two different times: once in the morning and once in the afternoon.

Those cameras normally take black-and-white pictures. Back on Earth, engineers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory combined the two panoramas into a single scene and added colour on purpose — blue for the morning view and yellow for the afternoon one. In the foreground you can even spot parts of the rover itself: its dish-shaped UHF antenna that phones home through orbiting spacecraft, and its nuclear power pack at the back.

What five thousand days of driving taught us

Curiosity has now driven roughly 37 kilometres (23 miles) and climbed more than a kilometre up the slopes of Mount Sharp, the 5-kilometre-tall mountain in the middle of the crater. Along the way it found the fingerprints of an ancient, shallow lake — clay minerals, sulfur, nitrogen, phosphorus and carbon, the kinds of ingredients that could have supported tiny microbial life long ago.

Try this at home

Stand in the same spot and take two drawings of the same view — one in the morning, one in the evening. Notice how the shadows point in different directions and the colours look different. That's exactly the trick Curiosity used! Scientists study light and shadow to understand the shape of the land.

Fun fact: Because Curiosity runs on a nuclear power pack instead of sunlight, it can keep working through Martian dust storms that would block solar panels — which is one reason it has lasted so long.

For grown-ups

"NASA's Curiosity rover (Mars Science Laboratory) landed in Gale Crater on 6 August 2012. It reached its 5,000th Martian day (sol) on 29 August 2026 — sols are about 24 h 39 min, so 5,000 sols equals roughly 5,137 Earth days (~14 Earth years). On sol 5,000 (30 Aug 2026, 9:56 a.m. local Mars time) and again on sol 5,003 (2 Sep 2026, 5:39 p.m.), the rover used its black-and-white Navigation Cameras to photograph the landscape from a sandy ridge nicknamed 'Chocolatal'. JPL combined the morning and afternoon panoramas into a single scene and added colour as an artistic interpretation — blue for the morning view, yellow for the afternoon — following the style of a similar 2021 Curiosity 'postcard'. Foreground rover parts visible include the cylindrical UHF antenna (which relays data via Mars orbiters) and the finned Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (MMRTG), Curiosity's nuclear power source. The rover is exploring the lower foothills of Mount Sharp (Aeolis Mons), a 5 km-tall mountain inside Gale Crater; it has driven roughly 37 km and climbed over 1 km since 2014. Curiosity has found extensive evidence that Gale Crater once held a shallow lake with clays, sulfur, nitrogen, phosphorus, carbon and chemical energy sources — conditions that could have supported microbial life."
