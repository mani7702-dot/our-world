"NASA's SPHEREx finds 'goth' brown dwarfs with stormy, colorful skies"

By Our World Published 19 October 2026 at "16:00 IST" IST
Reading options

"NASA's SPHEREx space telescope looked at 37 lonely 'failed stars' called brown dwarfs and discovered they have moody, goth-like atmospheres packed with water, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and methane — all shifting across a temperature range from 4,000 to -10 degrees Fahrenheit."

1. Meet the universe's moody loners

Most stars live in families — born in dusty nurseries alongside hundreds of siblings. But there is a special class of space object that wanders the galaxy completely alone. They are called brown dwarfs, and scientists just gave them a surprising nickname: the universe's goths.

2. Too small to shine, too big to ignore

A brown dwarf is like a star that hit the snooze button on its life. It forms from the same collapsing clouds of gas and dust as a real star, but it never gets quite heavy enough to light the fuse. Where the Sun is about a hundred times more massive than Jupiter, brown dwarfs sit in a sad middle — between 13 and 80 times the weight of Jupiter. That is enough to briefly fuse deuterium (a heavy form of hydrogen) and burn really thick, gassy atmospheres, but not enough to trigger the long-lasting hydrogen fusion that makes true stars shine.

Because they are so faint and cool, brown dwarfs are almost like shadow people gliding through our galaxy. They are nicknamed "cosmic loners" because many of them float completely alone, not orbiting any star at all.

3. NASA's SPHEREx gives them a close-up

So how do you get to know a goth that is drifting in the dark? NASA's SPHEREx space telescope did it by painting with light. SPHEREx — which is short for Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization, and Ices Explorer — looks at the sky in infrared, breaking light into hundreds of colors that our eyes cannot see.

From its perch in low-Earth orbit, SPHEREx studied 37 nearby brown dwarfs across 102 different wavelengths, stretching from red visible light out into infrared. The result is like taking a 102-layer mood ring and reading every shade.

4. What makes them goth?

Here is the goth part: these lonely worlds get a full 4,000 degrees Fahrenheit at their hottest, but cool all the way down to a chilly -10 degrees Fahrenheit — a temperature swing you would never see on any star. That is part of what makes them dramatic, shifting worlds covered in thick, colorful skies.

Their atmospheres are rich with some seriously atmospheric chemicals — literally. SPHEREx found swirling layers of water, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and methane, all of which combine to give brown dwarfs their characteristic hues: deep oranges, dusky reds, and rich purples, like a sky at sunset that never ends.

"They are kind of goth," said Dr. Zafar Rustamkulov, who leads SPHEREx science. And honestly, the name fits. These are moody worlds — cool, dim, and full of deep color, the cosmic equivalent of listening to a long playlist in a dimly lit room.

Try this at home

Make your own brown dwarf light show! Fill a clear glass with water, then add a few drops of food coloring — one blue, one red, one green. Watch how the colors swirl and mix instead of staying neat layers. That is a little bit like what happens when SPHEREx spots different molecules floating through a brown dwarf's cloudy atmosphere — each one leaves its own invisible fingerprint of light that the telescope can read.

Fun Fact

Brown dwarfs were only discovered in 1994 — that is, humans have known about them for less than 35 years. One of the first-ever brown dwarfs spotted, called Teide, is still too small to be a star. But because it glows a little from leftover warmth, it shines reddish-orange in infrared pictures. Imagine discovering a whole new kind of space object in your own lifetime!

For grown-ups

"Brown dwarfs (13-80 Jupiter masses, 0.013-0.08 M☉) form via gravitational collapse but never reach core temperatures of ~3 million K needed for hydrogen fusion (deuterium burns at ~10^6 K, allowing the 13 M_J threshold). SPHEREx — the Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer — launched 12 March 2025 into a sun-synchronous 97° LEO, mapping 3,300 deg² via all-sky near-infrared spectral surveys covering 0.75–5 µm across 102 wavelength channels. Free-floating brown dwarfs (no host star) dominate the local 8 pc volume, making them ideal isolated laboratories. Their atmospheres exhibit pressure-induced collision-induced absorption (CIA) from H2-H2 pairs, plus molecular bands of H2O, CO2, CO, and CH4 whose relative strength traces the T/Y spectral transition near ~1300 K. SPHEREx's broadband near-infrared spectroscopy resolves these features across the full 4000 °F to -10 °F range, revealing how cloud sedimentation efficiency and metallicity shape spectral energy distributions at the stellar-substellar boundary."
