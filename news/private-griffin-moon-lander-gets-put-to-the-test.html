What are the critical tests that the Griffin-1 lunar lander must pass before its Moon mission?

By Our World Published 23 October 2026 at 14:57 IST
Reading options

The Griffin-1 lunar lander has passed a gauntlet of extreme tests at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, simulating the vibrations, temperatures, and sounds of launch and landing — all before its first trip to the Moon's south pole later this year.

A lander that faces the harshest ride in space

Imagine strapping yourself into a car that will shake, rattle, and roll through temperatures that swing from oven-hot to freezer-cold — all while blasting with the deafening roar of a rocket launch. That is exactly what Griffin-1 just survived, and it passed with flying colors.

At NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, engineers put Griffin-1 through environmental testing, simulating every brutal condition the spacecraft will face: the violent shaking of launch, the extreme heat of sunlight and the bitter cold of space, and even the thunderous sound of rocket engines. The lander was tested inside JPL's 25-foot-wide (7.6-meter) space simulator — a giant vacuum chamber that recreates the vacuum of space and the searing heat and deep freeze of the lunar environment.

Why the Moon's south pole?

Griffin-1's destination is the lunar south pole — a region where NASA and commercial partners are racing to explore. The south pole holds permanently shadowed craters that scientists believe contain water ice, a treasure that could provide drinking water, oxygen, and rocket fuel for future astronauts.

Voyager's team studied the terrain from space, mapped where sunlight hits and shadows fall, and ran thousands of computer simulations to choose a landing site near Mons Mouton. Their tools — DEMkit and LunaRay — predict exactly how the lunar surface will look at any time of day, which matters because landing in total darkness would blind the cameras that help steer the lander.

A stepping stone to a Moon base

Griffin-1 is part of NASA's CLPS program — the Commercial Lunar Payload Services initiative. CLPS teams up with private companies to deliver scientific experiments to the Moon's surface. The lander will carry over 1,100 pounds (455 kilograms) of cargo — science experiments and new technologies that will test how future Moon bases could work.

Try this at home

Build a lunar lander test rig. Get a raw egg (your "astronaut"), tape, and soft padding like cotton balls. Drop it from different heights and design a cushioning system that keeps the egg safe. Now imagine doing this from thousands of miles away, on a world with no atmosphere — that is what Griffin-1 has to master!

Fun fact: The Moon's south pole is one of the coldest places in the Solar System, with some shadows that have never seen sunlight in billions of years.

For grown-ups

The short version for curious grown-ups: Voyager Space's Griffin-1 lunar lander — built by Voyager Technologies (formerly Astrobotic) — has completed NASA JPL's environmental testing campaign, which simulates the extreme vibrations, thermal swings, and acoustic noise of launch and descent. Griffin-1 is part of NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program and is scheduled to launch to the Moon's south pole later in 2026, carrying more than 1,100 pounds (455 kg) of scientific payloads. Its landing site near Mons Mouton at the lunar south pole was chosen through detailed terrain analysis, illumination mapping, and hazard avoidance studies conducted by Voyager's Lunar Systems team using in-house tools DEMkit and LunaRay. NASA's CLPS program partners with commercial companies to deliver small payloads to the lunar surface as a stepping stone toward the Artemis Moon Base. The south pole region is of particular interest because permanently shadowed craters there are thought to contain water ice, a resource critical for future human exploration.
