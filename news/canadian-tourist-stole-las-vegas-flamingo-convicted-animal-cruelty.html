Why can't visitors take a selfie with zoo animals? A flamingo theft shows why

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 09:05 IST
Reading options

A tourist in Las Vegas picked up a flamingo for a photo and was convicted of animal cruelty — here's why zoos and habitats have rules about touching.

Why can't visitors take a selfie with zoo animals? A flamingo theft shows why

You might think a flamingo is just a pink, friendly-looking bird. But a tourist in Las Vegas learned the hard way that wild animals are not photo props — and a court agreed.

The selfie that went too far

In March 2026, a Canadian visitor at the Flamingo Las Vegas Hotel & Casino walked into a wildlife habitat and picked up a Chilean flamingo named Peachy. He carried the squawking bird to his hotel room. Hotel staff found him in the hallway with the flamingo in his arms.

The tourist later told police he had drunk 30 to 40 beers and thought it would be "funny." A jury watched hotel camera footage showing the bird being carried by the neck. After five hours of deliberation, the jury convicted him of felony and misdemeanor animal cruelty.

Why wild birds are not pets

Flamingos are wild animals, not domesticated pets. Even zoos that house them keep visitors behind barriers because:

  • Stress kills quickly. A wild bird handled by a stranger can go into shock — its heart races and it stops eating. That is often fatal within days.
  • Feathers matter. Plucking or tearing feathers during a struggle leaves the bird unable to keep warm or stay waterproof.
  • Beaks and wings are fragile. What looks like a soft pink plush toy has a beak designed to dig in mud and wings built for long-distance flying — both break easily under panic.

Animal experts at the trial confirmed that Peachy lost feathers and suffered blood loss. The good news: with vet care, Peachy recovered — but now reacts more anxiously around humans than before.

The rules that protect everyone

Every zoo, sanctuary, and wildlife habitat has barriers for a reason. They protect the animals from stress and injury, and they protect visitors from bites, scratches, or diseases animals can carry. Some animals are even legally protected — harming one can mean fines, jail time, or both.

The convicted tourist now faces up to five years in prison. His case is a reminder that "just a photo" can seriously hurt a creature — and that the law treats wild animals as worth protecting.

Try this at home

Next time you visit a zoo or petting farm, notice the barriers. They are not just for show. Ask an adult to explain why each animal needs its space — it is usually about keeping both of you safe.

Fun Fact: A single flamingo can eat up to 200 shrimp a day, which is what gives its feathers that bright pink color! Without the barrier, that shrimp-eating beak could also give you a painful peck.
