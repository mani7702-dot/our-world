A floating forest broke loose on a Canadian lake — and nobody knew where it came from

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 18:42 IST
Reading options

A boat captain on Williston Lake, Canada, filmed a whole patch of trees floating like a tiny island. Satellites confirmed it was real — a rare "floating forest" that forms when high water lifts mats of roots and soil from the shore.

Some lakes keep their secrets quiet. Others wear them on the surface — and every once in a while, one of those lakes decides to set a secret adrift.

That is exactly what happened on Williston Lake in British Columbia, Canada. In August 2026, a boat captain named Rocky Avery was out on the water when he spotted something that looked like a small island — except it was moving on its own.

An island that swam away

The floating object wasn't a real island at all. It was a thick mat of soil, roots, plants, and fallen branches — a "floating forest" — that had broken loose from the shoreline. From a distance, the surviving trees made it look like a genuine little island drifting through the reservoir.

At first, even the experts were suspicious. "When we have full reservoirs, these higher water levels can lift material that may have been lodged along the shoreline, in a sheltered bay, and then allowing it to float free," explained a BC Hydro spokesperson. The reservoir had reached full capacity for the first time in fourteen years, thanks to heavy snowmelt and summer rains.

A mystery confirmed by satellites

Because internet videos these days are often fake, the team reached for something harder to fool: space. Scientists checked old images from the European Space Agency's Sentinel-2 satellites. A picture from July 21 showed a small green mass floating about 100 kilometers west of the W.A.C. Bennett Dam. The blob was not there in earlier photos, and it vanished again later — proving Avery's video was real.

The floating forest is the kind of thing that usually sinks without notice, soaking up water until it becomes one with the lakebed. But this one stayed intact long enough for people to film it and for satellites to spot it.

Two, four, six, eight…

Floating forests are not unique — similar mats have appeared on lakes in Sweden and even on the Amazon. But Williston Lake is enormous, the seventh-largest reservoir in the world, which makes a drifting island harder to miss.

  1. Quick floodwaters lift a chunk of shoreline.
  2. Roots and soil stick together like a green carpet.
  3. The mat floats away, trees still standing.

Try this at home

Next time you fill a sink with water, scatter a few fresh leaves and a small twig on the surface. If you tilt the sink just right, the leaves will clump into a little raft that moves with the current — a tiny model of the same force that freed a whole forest.

Why we care

Besides being delightfully strange, floating forests tell us something useful: when water levels rise quickly, the edges of a lake can let go. Knowing that helps scientists and dam operators predict what might wash loose during big flood seasons.

Fun Fact: Williston Lake was created in the 1970s by damming the Peace River — the very kind of project that can leave behind buried forests, which sometimes resurface decades later as floating islands.

Sources

The floating forest was first reported by ScienceAlert, which cross-checked the video against satellite data and interviews with BC Hydro. The story also appears in Listverse's roundup of weird lakes.
