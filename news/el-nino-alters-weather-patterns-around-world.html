How El Niño's warming ocean is rewriting the weather map across Earth

By Our World Published 20 October 2026 at 18:15 IST
Reading options

"This year's El Niño — now officially the strongest on record — is sending ripples around the planet: wildfires in Indonesia, a supercharged Pacific hurricane season, a hush over the Atlantic, and a monsoon that forgot to show up in India. Satellites and weather stations from NASA to the WMO are tracking every twist."

Far away in the Pacific Ocean, a patch of water is quietly running a fever. It is called El Niño — Spanish for "little boy" — and every few years it warms up the tropical Pacific enough to nudge the whole planet's weather. This year, it is doing it BIG.

1. Fires paint the sky red in Indonesia

When El Niño turns on, it usually dries things out in the western Pacific. That means places like Indonesia get hotter and thirstier. Since January 2026, fires have burned through more than 895,000 hectares — that is nearly six times the size of Greater London — making it the worst fire season there since 2015.

Thick, toxic haze has hung over cities for weeks. Schools have closed. Hospitals are full of people — especially little ones — coughing from the smoke. The sky turned orange, and motorists drove with headlights on at noon.

2. Hurricanes dance to a new beat

El Niño changes the rules for storms. In the eastern Pacific, warmer ocean water gives hurricanes extra fuel. This year already saw 22 named storms and 12 hurricanes — including 10 major ones — before the season's halfway point. That is more than what usually forms in a whole average year, and there are still two months left.

Hurricane Polo was one of the stars — it went from nothing to 180 mph winds in less than a day, one of the fastest intensifications ever recorded. At one point, three hurricanes were spinning at the same time.

Meanwhile, across the Atlantic Ocean, it is almost the opposite. Warmer Pacific winds create high-altitude winds that tear storms apart before they get strong. So far, the Atlantic has had just one hurricane — Isaias — and it formed unusually late. Some places that usually get hit every season have barely seen a cloud.

3. India's monsoon plays hooky

Every summer, a giant wall of wet air sweeps in from the south-west over India — the monsoon — and most of the country's rain falls during a few short months. Farmers plant their crops waiting for it.

This year, El Niño whispered in the monsoon's ear and it seemed to forget. Rainfall came in 13 percent below average overall. But it was worse in some places: the northeast got 26 percent less rain, and the south got 24 percent less. Even where the total was close to normal, the rain no longer fell steadily — instead it swung between weeks of dry dust and sudden, shocking downpours.

That means crops are stressed. Water reservoirs are lower. And the timing — one of the things farmers rely on most — is off.

4. Tiny ocean plants fade as big storms form

High above, satellites are watching something invisible to our eyes: the green of the ocean. That green comes from phytoplankton — tiny plant-like microbes that are the base of the whole marine food chain.

When El Niño warms the Pacific, it quiets the winds that normally stir up cold, nutrient-rich water from the deep. Without those nutrients, phytoplankton numbers crash — and the maps from NASA show it clearly. Around the equator, huge swaths turned from bright green to ghostly blue in just a few months.

Less food at the bottom means trouble all the way up. Peru has suspended its anchoveta fishery — the world's biggest single-species fishery — to protect the fish stocks. And in the ports, pelicans have been seen waddling around city streets, searching for food that used to be swimming right offshore.

Try this at home: Fill a clear glass with water, add a few drops of food coloring, and blow gently across the top with a straw. Watch how the color spreads in swirls — just like warm ocean water pushes the cold, dense layers down and changes what floats on top.

What comes next?

Scientists at the World Meteorological Organization say sea-surface temperatures in the tropical Pacific are forecast to climb even higher through late 2026. El Niño typically peaks during winter in the Northern Hemisphere, then slowly fades — but NOAA now says there is a 97% chance it will stick around until spring 2027.

That means the weather map will stay rewritten for a while longer. The good news? It cannot last forever, and our planet has survived many an El Niño before. The better news? Each one teaches us more — and satellites like NASA's PACE are now watching the ocean's green pulse in real time for the very first time during a full El Niño event.
