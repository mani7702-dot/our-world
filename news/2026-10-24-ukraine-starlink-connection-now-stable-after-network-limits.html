Why was Ukraine's Starlink internet slow for a week — and how did it get fixed?

The internet that comes from space

Imagine getting Wi-Fi from a blanket of tiny satellites flying above your head — that is exactly what Ukraine uses! The system is called Starlink, and it belongs to Elon Musk's company SpaceX. Instead of one big satellite parked far away over the equator (like a TV dish), Starlink uses thousands of small satellites that zoom in low orbits, only 550 kilometers (about 340 miles) above Earth.

Because they fly so close, the signal has less far to travel — so it is faster than old-style satellite internet. That makes it perfect for places like front lines and remote farms where laying cables is impossible.

When the satellites hit the brakes

Last week something strange happened. Ukraine's military — which depends on Starlink to pilot drones and stay in touch — suddenly found the connection slowing to a crawl. Drones that flew faster than 160 km/h (100 mph) started losing their signal, like a video call that keeps freezing.

Ukraine's defence minister said the problem came from network limits placed on the system. He did not name who set the limits, but the slowdown last only about a week, and it clearly mattered: Starlink is how Ukrainian forces call in drone strikes far from cell towers. As one official put it, a stable connection is "important for our front, for our soldiers, for the operation of drones, for logistics and communications."

Back to full speed

By Saturday the system was fixed. Defence Minister Yevhenii Khmara announced that Starlink was now "stable", and speeds above 400 km/h were possible again — faster than a race car on a highway! That means drones, trucks, and even fighter jets can stay connected while moving at top speed.

The fix is a relief because Starlink also has a secret twin called Starshield — a more secure version used for sensitive military work. Together the two keep Ukraine online even when cell towers and power lines are damaged.

The big picture: 10,000 satellites working together

A single Starlink satellite is only about the size of a kitchen fridge, but the whole network is enormous. As of late 2026 the constellation includes roughly 10,000–11,000 satellites working as a team — each one handing off your connection to the next as they race around Earth every 90 minutes (that is 16 orbits a day!). A user terminal on the ground — a small dish that swivels automatically — talks to whichever satellite is overhead, and that satellite beams your data to a ground station connected to the normal internet. It is like a cosmic game of hot potato, except instead of a potato it is your video call or drone control signal.

Try this at home

You can model a satellite network with a flashlight and friends! Have one person shine a flashlight (your "satellite") across a dark room to another person holding a mirror (the "ground station"). The second person tilts the mirror to "bounce" the light to a third person (the "user on the ground"). Now have the first person slowly rotate the flashlight so the beam passes from one mirror to the next, like satellites handing off a signal. The faster and smoother the handoff, the clearer the connection — just like Starlink!

Fun Fact

One Starlink launch can carry 60 satellites at once tucked inside a Falcon 9 rocket fairing. When the fairing door opens, they all tumble out like a cluster of metallic ping-pong balls, then each one fires tiny ion thrusters to climb to its assigned orbit. SpaceX has launched so many that at certain times of day you can see the "train" of Starlink satellites crossing the night sky as bright moving dots!

for-grownups: Starlink is SpaceX's low-Earth-orbit satellite internet constellation (about 11,150 satellites in orbit as of October 2026, per Wikipedia). Ukraine uses both the civilian Starlink service and the military-grade Starshield system for drone operations and battlefield communications. The constellation uses ~10,000 LEO satellites that relay data to ground terminals and gateway stations, enabling high-speed broadband where no fiber cables exist. Starshield is the secure U.S.-military variant of the same technology.
