Could the World's Most Accurate Clock Be Powered by an Atomic Nucleus?

Every clock needs something that repeats perfectly. A pendulum swings, a quartz crystal vibrates, and today's atomic clocks watch electrons inside atoms jump between energy levels. Now scientists in Vienna have built a new kind of timekeeper — one that listens, not to electrons, but to the nucleus at the very heart of an atom.

What makes this clock different?

Inside an atom, electrons buzz around a dense central nucleus. Regular atomic clocks rely on those electrons. The new nuclear clock skips them and uses an energy jump inside the nucleus itself. That matters because a nucleus is more than 10,000 times smaller than the whole atom around it. Being so small and tucked away, it is much harder for temperature changes or stray vibrations to disturb it — which is exactly what makes a clock steady.

Why thorium is the magic ingredient

Most nuclei need gigantic amounts of energy to change state, far more than a laser can deliver. But one rare atom — thorium-229 — is different. Two of its nuclear states sit unusually close together, so a laser can trigger the switch. Scientists chased this transition for decades before pinning it down experimentally in April 2024.

A clock that keeps itself on time

Even a great laser can drift. The Vienna team solved this with a neat trick: thorium nuclei only absorb laser light when the frequency is just right. If the laser wanders, the nuclei absorb less light — and the system automatically nudges it back on track. This feedback loop lets the clock run for more than 24 hours without help, making it the first self-stabilizing nuclear clock.

How accurate is it?

Measured over a single day, the prototype hit a precision of about one second in 30 million years. That is impressive for an early version, though today's best atomic clocks are still a bit sharper. Better lasers and purer thorium crystals could push nuclear clocks past them — opening the door to testing the deepest laws of physics.

Try this at home

Build a "feedback clock" with a grown-up. Have a helper tap a steady beat on a table. Clap just after each tap — if they speed up, you speed up too. That is a feedback loop, exactly how the nuclear clock keeps its laser steady.

Fun fact: At one second every 30 million years, the clock would drift by only about 460 seconds across the entire 13.8-billion-year age of the Universe.

For grown-ups

"Researchers at TU Wien (Vienna University of Technology), led by Prof. Thorsten Schumm, have demonstrated the first self-stabilizing nuclear clock — a device that locks a laser to an energy transition inside a thorium-229 nucleus rather than to an electron transition in an atom's shell. Thorium-229 is uniquely suited because two of its nuclear energy states are separated by an unusually small gap (about 8.36 eV), low enough to be excited by laser light. After the transition was first identified experimentally in April 2024 and connected to a conventional optical clock later that year, the missing piece was autonomous stabilization: here the camera detects how much laser light the nuclei absorb and feeds corrections back to hold the laser frequency steady, letting the clock run for more than 24 hours without outside help. Measured over a day, the prototype reached a relative precision of about 10^-15 — roughly one second in 30 million years. That is not yet a match for the world's best optical atomic clocks, but Schumm calls it 'a fantastic result' for a first prototype. Because nuclei are more than 10,000 times smaller than atoms, they are far less disturbed by their surroundings, so nuclear clocks could eventually improve not just timekeeping but tests of fundamental physics. The work was published in Nature; a parallel team in Beijing reported related results."
