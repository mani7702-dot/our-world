"Who Moved Into the Tree Apartments Built for Ants? A 10-Million-Year Deal Is Breaking"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at 17:56 IST
Reading options

"In the rainforests of Borneo, trees have rented out tiny hollow rooms to ants for 10 million years. Now predatory wasps are moving in — and turning the ant apartments into nurseries."

Who Moved Into the Tree Apartments Built for Ants? A 10-Million-Year Deal Is Breaking

1. A tree that rents out tiny rooms

Imagine a tree that grows tiny hollow apartments inside its stems — and rents them out. That's real. In the rainforests of Malaysian Borneo, a tree called Macaranga pearsonii grows hollow chambers where ants can live. As a sort of rent, the tree also makes little food parcels packed with nutrients, called food bodies.

The ants pay back by acting as bodyguards. They swarm any caterpillar or beetle that tries to chew the leaves. This friendly partnership — where both sides help each other — has lasted for more than 10 million years.

2. Wait — someone else moved in

Scientists from Queen Mary University of London, the Royal Botanic Gardens Kew, and the Czech Academy of Sciences went into the forest to check on these tree apartments. When they opened the hollow stems, they found something surprising: predatory wasps had moved in.

Instead of ants, many chambers were packed with flies. But the flies weren't alive and well — they had been caught, paralysed, and stored there as food for baby wasps.

Lead author Dan Lestina explains what he saw: "While surveying these ant-plants, I noticed many stems had been hollowed out in an unusual way. When we opened them, they were full of flies being eaten alive by wasp larvae."

3. Why does it matter?

The team found that trees growing in oil palm plantations were far more likely to house wasps than trees in logged forests. Trees taken over by wasps also had smaller ant colonies — a hint that the insects may be pushing the ants out.

"We don't yet know whether this wasp is native or introduced," says co-author Dr Kalsum M. Yusah, "but its spread is clearly linked to disturbed landscapes."

Without their ant bodyguards, these trees could lose some of their natural leaf protection — a small change that ripples through the whole forest.

4. Try this at home

Be a neighbourhood nature detective. Pick one garden plant or tree and check a few leaves every day for a week. Tally how many leaves have been nibbled and write down any insects you spot — ants, ladybirds, bees. You're doing exactly what the Borneo scientists did: watching who lives where and who is helping whom.

Fun Fact

Some ant-plants give ants more than a home — they grow special "food bodies" that are basically packed lunches. And ants are so good at guarding their tree that scientists call the whole partnership a mutualism, from the word mutual, meaning "both sides win."

For grown-ups

"Published in PeerJ (2026, DOI 10.7717/peerj.20984), researchers from Queen Mary University of London, the Royal Botanic Gardens Kew, the Biology Centre of the Czech Academy of Sciences and partner institutions studied Macaranga pearsonii, a myrmecophyte ('ant-plant') in Malaysian Borneo. For at least 10 million years these trees have housed protective ant colonies in hollow chambers called domatia, paying 'rent' in nutrient-rich food bodies; the ants in turn defend the leaves from caterpillars and other herbivores. Dissecting young trees in logged forest and oil-palm plantations, the team found predatory wasps using the hollow stems to store paralysed flies as food for their larvae. Wasp occupancy was markedly higher in oil-palm plantations than in logged forest, and infested trees hosted smaller ant colonies — though the authors caution that further experiments are needed to confirm the wasps are displacing ants outright. Lead author Dan Lestina and co-author Dr Kalsum M. Yusah note it is not yet known whether the wasp is native or introduced, but its spread is clearly linked to human-disturbed landscapes. The story is a vivid, non-frightening example of how habitat change can quietly rewire long-standing species partnerships. No sensitive content. The wasp is a predatory insect, not a stinging threat to people; keep the framing factual and ecological."
