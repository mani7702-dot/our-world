"What Did the Oldest Telescope in the Southern Hemisphere Capture in Its Final Glimpse?"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at "14:00" IST
Reading options

"The Swedish-ESO Submillimetre Telescope (SEST) may have retired from active duty, but its giant 15-meter mirror still 'sees' the stars of the Southern Hemisphere — reflecting the cosmos like a cosmic eye in a quiet Chilean desert."

The Telescope That Still Watches the Stars

Perched on a high plateau in Chile's Atacama Desert, a giant aluminum dish silently "reflects" the night sky like a mirror left out under the stars. This is the Swedish-ESO Submillimetre Telescope — or SEST for short — and even though it stopped collecting data in 2003, it still stands guard, gazing at the universe like a sleeping giant.

Astronomer and astrophotographer José Rodrigues recently visited the European Southern Observatory's La Silla site and captured SEST's silhouette against the Milky Way. The photo shows the telescope's 15-meter (49-foot) dish catching "airglow" — a soft orange glow that happens when sunlight touches molecules high above Earth's surface.

Why Airglow Makes the Sky Glow

Airglow might sound like something from a science fiction movie, but it's very real! It happens about 90 kilometers (56 miles) above our heads, where sunlight splits apart oxygen and sodium atoms. When those atoms stick together again, they release tiny amounts of light — so faint that you might think someone left a nightlight on somewhere far above. The University of Lancaster explains that this gentle glow blankets the whole sky, even on the darkest nights.

The Telescope That Hunted Hidden Light

When SEST first opened its "eye" in 1987, it was the only large telescope in the Southern Hemisphere built to catch submillimetre light — wavelengths that are too long for our eyes to see and too short for ordinary radio antennas. These invisible rays come from cold clouds of gas and dust scattered across the Milky Way, where new stars are born inside mysterious cocoons called "proplyds."

SEST's high mountaintop home (2,375 meters above sea level) gave it a nearly perfect view of the heavens, free from the fog and water vapor that block submillimetre waves. Scientists used its observations to study comets wandering through our solar system, map distant galaxies, and peer into star-forming regions like the famous Carina Nebula.

A New Generation Takes Over

Although SEST retired in 2003, its mission continued through two successor telescopes. The Atacama Pathfinder Experiment (APEX) — built from a modified ALMA prototype antenna — now scans the same submillimetre skies from an even higher mountaintop (5,104 meters). And further up the cosmic ladder, the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) uses 66 dishes working together to create the sharpest submillimetre pictures ever taken.

Try This at Home

On a clear, dark night, look for the Milky Way arching across the sky. That milky band is the combined light of billions of stars too faint to see individually — much like the "universe in the eye of a telescope." Can you spot any of the dark patches? Those are clouds of cosmic dust blocking the light behind them — the same kind of stuff that SEST spent decades studying in invisible wavelengths.

Fun Fact

SEST's name sounds like "sestet" (a group of seven) or even "sauce" — but it stands for "Swedish-ESO Submillimetre Telescope." Sweden and the European Southern Observatory built it together, and it was one of the first telescopes designed specifically for submillimetre astronomy!
