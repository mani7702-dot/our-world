Who runs the science lab on the ISS while orbiting 250 miles above Earth?

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 22:05 IST
Reading options

NASA astronaut Jessica Watkins spends her days aboard the ISS running life-science experiments inside the giant Kibo lab — the biggest scientific workhorse orbiting Earth.

Floating inside the biggest science lab in space

About 250 miles above Earth, where night falls and rises every 45 minutes and everything weighs nothing, NASA astronaut Jessica Watkins is hard at work. She is inside the International Space Station's Kibo laboratory — a silver cylindrical room the size of a basketball court that is packed with windows, robot arms, and the kind of tools that only make sense in zero gravity.

On an ordinary Thursday in early October, Watkins floated into Kibo's Life Science Glovebox. Behind its clear plastic walls, she began setting up an experiment that would be impossible back on Earth: watching how living tissue behaves when it never has to fight gravity.

What is a "glovebox" in space?

A glovebox sounds small, but space's version is a sealed, sealed-off room built right into the lab. It keeps tiny floating droplets, dust, and even gases from drifting into the rest of the station — which matters when "away" might mean your pipette tip flies out the airlock. Astronauts press their hands into rubber gloves built into the box and work as if underwater, except the water is air and everything floats.

Inside, Watkins and the ground team could watch cells, seeds, or small organisms grow and change in perfect stillness — the way they do on Earth, except gravity never pulls them down to settle, never makes them stretch or squash. Scientists on Earth get videos live, and the results teach us how life really works when one of nature's biggest forces is turned off.

Why watch biology in weightlessness?

On Earth, gravity is always tugging. Roots always know which way is down. Hearts always pump against gravity. Blood always pools a little in the feet. In space, biology runs on a different set of rules — and that makes it a giant natural experiment.

Tissue that grows in microgravity often turns out thinner, more spread-out, and oddly organized. Muscles weaken. Bones thin. But some processes — like how blood vessels form, or how a seed first learns to sprout — become clearer when gravity's noise is silenced. That is why Kibo runs dozens of biology experiments every year, from growing lettuce to studying how the brain changes on long trips.

What does it look like?

Imagine trying to do delicate science while everything you touch gently floats away. There are no "up" shelves in the glovebox; tools are taped to trays. Liquids form glowing spheres. And when an astronaut sneezes? Everyone nearby politely holds their breath for a few seconds.

Watkins, who trained as a geologist before becoming an astronaut, is one of just a handful of people who has operated the Life Science Glovebox. She arrived at the station in early October 2026 as part of SpaceX Crew-13 and joined Expedition 75 of the space station program.

Try this at home

Build a tiny "space lab" under a bright lamp: tape a clear jar to a table, put a few drops of food coloring in some water, and shine the lamp from the side so you can watch how the colored drops swirl and mix. In space there is no sinking or rising — try tilting the jar gently and see how the drops behave differently. Ask a grownup to help you float a raisin in a glass of soda: it will bob up and down between the top and bottom like it's weightless, just like the experiments floating inside Kibo.

Fun Fact

The International Space Station orbits Earth every 90 minutes, which means the crew sees about 16 sunrises and sunsets every single day. For most of those sunrises, someone inside Kibo is already at work, because science doesn't take breakfast breaks in space.

For grown-ups

NASA astronaut Jessica Watkins, part of Expedition 75 aboard the International Space Station, spent October 9, 2026 working inside the Kibo laboratory module's Life Science Glovebox. Kibo is the largest pressurized module on the ISS and has a dedicated science bay where crew run biology, chemistry, and physics experiments in microgravity. Expedition 75 began on July 26, 2026 and was joined by the four-person SpaceX Crew-13 crew (including Watkins) arriving October 1. Wikipedia's Expedition 75 article confirms Watkins arrived with Crew-13 on October 1 and that the expedition has conducted multiple spacewalks installing iROSA solar arrays. The Life Science Glovebox is a sealed enclosure that lets astronauts safely study biology and human physiology samples in orbit without contaminating the station.
