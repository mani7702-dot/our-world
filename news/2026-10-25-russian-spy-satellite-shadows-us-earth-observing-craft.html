"Why did a Russian spy satellite play cat-and-mouse with a commercial US spacecraft?"

By Our World Published 25 October 2026 at 23:05 IST IST
Reading options

"A Russian reconnaissance satellite named Kosmos 2612 followed a commercial US radar satellite called ICEYE-X36 through low Earth orbit — getting as close as just a few meters at one point in a silent, non-consensual game of orbital chicken."

How close can two satellites get before space becomes a game of chicken?

When a Russian Satellite Followed a US Spy-Eye

Imagine two cars on a highway — one going 17,000 miles per hour, both invisible to the drivers, and neither one able to slow down. That is what happened in orbit last week, when a Russian military satellite quietly shadowed a commercial American radar satellite through the silent darkness of space.

The Russian satellite is called Kosmos 2612. The American one is ICEYE-X36. Between October 15 and October 19, 2026, Kosmos 2612 followed right behind its target — sometimes separated by just a few meters. Satellite trackers called it a "worrisome cat-and-mouse game," and for good reason.

Why Was the Close Approach So Unusual?

Most of the time, when one satellite inspects another, it happens far away from Earth — in what is called geostationary orbit, a parking spot 22,236 miles above the equator where satellites appear to hover over the same spot. It is like a highway with plenty of room between cars.

But this close encounter happened much lower — in low Earth orbit (LEO), the busy neighborhood where the International Space Station and most Earth-observing satellites live. Here, satellites zoom around the planet at 17,500 miles per hour, and there is far less room to maneuver.

That matters because ICEYE-X36 is not a US government spy satellite. It belongs to a private company called ICEYE, which builds tiny radar satellites that can see through clouds and darkness using a technology called synthetic aperture radar (SAR). These satellites bounce radio waves off Earth's surface and listen for the echo — like shouting in a cave and timing how long the reply takes. The echo reveals the shape and movement of everything below, even in total darkness or thick storm clouds.

The Spy in the Shadows

Why would Russia follow a commercial radar satellite? The answer may be tied to a conflict far below.

ICEYE satellites have been helping Ukraine monitor the war zone since Russia invaded in 2022. In January 2026, ICEYE signed a contract to share high-resolution radar imagery with Ukraine's military, giving them an eye in the sky that works day and night, rain or shine. That makes ICEYE-X36 especially valuable — and especially tempting to watch.

Russia has tried this kind of orbital shadowing before. In January 2020, a satellite called Kosmos 2543 came within 19 miles of a US reconnaissance satellite. In August 2022, Kosmos 2558 came within 43 miles of another target. But those happened far above Earth, and the targets were classified US military satellites. This time, the target was a private company's satellite, and the dance happened in the crowded low orbits where ordinary satellites — including scientific and weather craft — also fly.

Who Is Watching the Watchers?

Every country keeps satellites in orbit for different reasons. Some take pictures, some send internet signals, some measure weather, and some do things that are harder to explain. When one satellite gets very close to another without asking permission, space agencies worry about two things:

  1. Accidents — even a paint flake traveling at orbital speed can punch through a spacecraft. If two satellites get too close, a small mistake could create dangerous debris that threatens every other satellite.
  2. Spying or sabotage — a close neighbor could try to read signals, block sensors, or jam communications without anyone noticing.

The good news is that hundreds of people around the world — amateur astronomers, satellite trackers, and government agencies — keep careful tabs on what happens in space. The US Space Surveillance Network tracks every sizable object, and hobbyist sky-watchers often spot things first. That is exactly how the cat-and-mouse game between Kosmos 2612 and ICEYE-X36 was discovered — not by governments, but by citizen scientists peering through telescopes and sharing their findings online.

Try this at home

Tie a string to a marble and swing it around your head in a big circle (outdoors, and only if it is safe!). The string is like gravity — it keeps the marble from flying away. Now imagine a second marble trying to follow your first one around the circle without touching it. How close can you get before the strings get tangled? That is what satellites have to manage in orbit — all the while moving at 17,500 miles per hour!

Fun Fact

Satellites in low Earth orbit complete an entire trip around Earth in just 90 minutes — that means they see about 16 sunrises and sunsets every single day. The astronauts onboard the International Space Station watch the sun come up, go down, come up, go down... 16 times before they finish one ordinary Earth day of sleep and meals!

For grown-ups

"Kosmos 2612 is a Russian military reconnaissance satellite launched in April 2025 as part of a dual-use civilian/military payload stack. Between October 15 and October 19, 2026, it conducted a close rendezvous with ICEYE-X36 — a commercial synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellite operated by ICEYE US. ICEYE's SAR constellation (approximately 80 satellites in LEO as of October 2026) provides all-weather, day-and-night Earth imaging and has been sharing data with Ukraine's Ministry of Defence since a contract signed in January 2026. Low Earth orbit (LEO) is defined as an orbit with a period of 128 minutes or less and an altitude below 2,000 km (1,200 mi), where orbital velocity is about 7.8 km/s (17,000 mph). Kosmos-class satellites are a long-running Soviet/Russian military designation for military payloads; the Kosmos series has included hundreds of satellites since 1962. This close approach was notable because it occurred in LEO (where most prior inspection incidents have been in geostationary orbit at 22,236 mi / 35,786 km altitude) and targeted a private, non-spy satellite. Prior Russian LEO inspection incidents include Kosmos 2543 approaching USA 245 in January 2020 (within 30 km) and Kosmos 2558 approaching USA 326 in August 2022 (within 70 km). The motivation remains unclear: possibilities include assessing the satellite's capabilities, testing interference techniques, or demonstrating military prowess in response to the ongoing conflict in Ukraine. Space situational awareness relies on the US Space Surveillance Network to monitor objects in orbit to prevent collisions. Cross-checked: Space.com (Oct 21, 2026 by Mike Wall); Wikipedia 'Low Earth orbit'; Wikipedia 'Geostationary orbit'; Wikipedia 'Reconnaissance'.\""
