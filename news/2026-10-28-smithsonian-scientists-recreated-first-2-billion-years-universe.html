"Scientists Recreate the Universe's First 2 Billion Years in Giant Computer Simulation"

By Our World Published 28 October 2026 at "18:23 IST" IST
Reading options

"Researchers built Lumina, one of the largest cosmic simulations ever, showing how dark matter, gas, and radiation shaped the first stars, galaxies, and black holes in our universe's first 2 billion years."

1. One Simulation to Rebuild the Early Cosmos

How did everything in space form? With powerful telescopes like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the new Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope peering deeper into the cosmos than ever before, astronomers are seeing the universe's first stars, galaxies, and black holes in stunning detail. But to understand these observations, scientists need more than pictures — they need a virtual universe they can explore on a supercomputer.

That is exactly what a team has done with Lumina, one of the largest and most detailed simulations of the early universe ever produced.

2. Rewriting the First 2 Billion Years

Lumina recreates the universe's first 2 billion years after the Big Bang, calculating how dark matter, gas, and light influenced one another as the cosmos evolved. The virtual universe spans a cube roughly 1.5 billion light-years on each side — more than 100 times larger than previous simulations. This enormous size lets scientists watch whole regions of space at once, while still seeing individual galaxies form.

<div class="try"> <h4>Try this at home!</h4> <p>On a clear night, look for the Milky Way arching overhead — that is our entire galaxy, containing hundreds of billions of stars. The universe is so big that light from the very first stars took 13 billion years to reach Earth. Even a tiny speck of light you see through a telescope may be one of the earliest galaxies that Lumina helps us understand.</p> </div>

3. A Supercomputer Larger Than Any Lab

Recreating billions of light-years takes enormous computing power. The team used more than 15,000 graphics processing units (GPUs) on Frontier, one of the world's fastest supercomputers, housed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee. This processing power let Lumina simulate an enormous region of cosmic history without losing the fine detail needed to study individual galaxies.

4. Solving the Mystery of Invisible Light

One of Lumina's breakthroughs is tracking helium reionization — a phase when the radiation from early stars and galaxies stripped electrons away from helium atoms. This was the same kind of process called "reionization" that transformed the early universe from a foggy haze to a transparent cosmos. By comparing Lumina's predictions with real JWST and Roman Space Telescope data, scientists can test whether their theories hold up, or whether new physics is hiding in the data.

<div class="fun-fact"> <h4>Fun Fact:</h4> <p>Dark matter — invisible stuff that does not shine, emit, or absorb light — makes up about 85% of all matter in the universe. In Lumina, dark matter clumps into a cosmic web of filaments, and galaxies form along these invisible scaffolding strands, like beads on a string.</p> </div>

for-grown-ups: The Lumina simulation, described by lead author Oliver Zier (ITC Fellow, Center for Astrophysics | Harvard & Smithsonian) and co-author Aaron Smith (University of Texas at Dallas), is a cosmological radiation hydrodynamics simulation covering the universe's first 2 billion years. It models interactions between ordinary matter, dark matter, and radiation across a cube ~1.5 billion light-years per side, using 15,000+ GPUs on the Frontier exascale supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. A key advance is its treatment of helium reionization, where radiation strips electrons from helium atoms — analogous to the broader "Epoch of Reionization" driven by the first stars and galaxies, as described on Wikipedia's "Reionization" page. The simulation also tracks how galaxy formation and black hole growth are connected. Wikipedia confirms that the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (launched Aug 30, 2026) studies galaxy formation and cosmic structure growth, while JWST observes the first stars and galaxies. Wikipedia's "Dark matter" article confirms dark matter constitutes ~85% of total matter and acts as gravitational scaffolding for cosmic structures. Wikipedia's "Galaxy formation and evolution" article confirms hydrodynamics simulations of both baryons and dark matter are the standard tool for studying these processes. Published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society.
