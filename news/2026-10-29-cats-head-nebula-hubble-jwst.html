"What Does a Nebula Look Like Through a Cat's Eyes? Hubble and Webb Unmask the Ghostly Hunter"

By Our World Published 29 October 2026 at "19:45" IST
Reading options

"A small corner of the giant Carina Nebula wears a surprising disguise: seen from Earth, the swirling gas and dust looks unmistakably like a cat sitting with its back turned, one glowing 'eye' watching the scene. With help from both the Hubble and James Webb Space Telescopes, astronomers are learning how this ghostly hunter is carved by the fierce light of newborn stars."

What Does a Nebula Look Like Through a Cat's Eyes?

A ghostly hunter hiding in plain sight

Imagine floating in space, looking toward the southern sky. There, drifting some 7,500 light-years from Earth inside the vast Carina Nebula, something uncanny stares back at you: the silhouette of a cat, frozen in gas and dust, its head turned as if listening to a cosmic secret. Astronomers call it the Cat's Head Nebula, and for the first time the NASA/ESA Hubble and the NASA/ESA/CSA James Webb Space Telescopes have examined it together.

The nebula gets its name from a simple trick of perspective. A thick pillar of dark cosmic dust stands upright like a cat's back. A single young star, still wrapped in veils of material, glows at the tip of the pillar and catches the light like a glowing eye. Step back and the whole scene looks less like a nebula and more like a sleepy feline keeping watch over one of the galaxy's liveliest nurseries.

Carved by starlight

The Carina Nebula is one of the brightest, most energetic star-forming regions in our galaxy. Inside it swarms a crowd of massive stars, some of them millions of times brighter than our Sun. Their ultraviolet radiation blasts outward day after day, eroding the surrounding gas and dust like an invisible wind carving sculpture out of marble.

Where the gas is thin, the wind sculpts it into delicate wisps and arcs. Where dense clumps of dust and gas resist the onslaught, towering pillars survive — including the one that forms the Cat's Head. The bright rim along the right edge of the pillar is gas being heated and ionised by the same stellar radiation, a process called photoevaporation that slowly evaporates the nebula away.

Scientists call this "stellar feedback" — the way new stars shape the cloud from which they were born. It can both trigger fresh star formation in compressed shells and blow it apart for good. Watching the balance play out in a single pillar nebula like the Cat's Head helps astronomers understand how entire stellar generations are born and fade.

Two telescopes, two stories

Hubble sees the Cat's Head in visible light. Its filters isolate the glow of oxygen, hydrogen, and sulphur, painting the scene in reds, blues, and yellows. From the ground the gas looks opaque, a dark brown pillar backlit by brilliant nearby stars. The "eye" shines red, a beacon marking an infant star still gathering itself together.

Webb sees the same object through different eyes entirely. Its infrared cameras peer through the dust, revealing the hidden landscape beneath. In near-infrared the pillar nebula becomes translucent, letting us see stars — and even background galaxies — that Hubble cannot see at all. In mid-infrared, warm dust and carbon-based molecules take center stage, outlining intricate structures that were invisible before.

Together the two telescopes tell a fuller story than either could alone: the visible light shows us where the action is, and the infrared shows us what is happening behind the curtain of dust.

Try this at home

Next time you and an grown-up take a flashlight into a dark room, try this:

  1. Crumple a piece of tracing paper or thin white paper into a loose ball.
  2. Shine the flashlight across it from one side.
  3. Look at the shadow it casts on the wall — notice how the raised edges block

the light while thinner spots glow translucently.

The crumpled paper is like a tiny nebula, the flashlight like a newborn star, and your wall like a telescope's detector. Astronomers are doing the same thing with the real thing, just on a scale of light-years.

Fun Fact

The Carina Nebula holds a record of sorts: it is home to some of the most massive stars known in the Milky Way, stars so huge that most of them will end their lives in titanic supernova explosions. Yet here, in the Cat's Head Pillar, the gentler side of star formation is playing out — quiet, slow, and patiently sculpting one ghostly hunter at a time.

The Cat's Head Nebula lies within NGC 3372 (the Carina Nebula), a massive H II region in the Carina–Sagittarius Arm, at a distance of approximately 7,500–8,500 light-years. The feature examined here is a dense photoionisation-induced dust/gas pillar in the foreground South Pillar area, illuminated by the OB association Carina OB1. The "eye" corresponds to a Class I/II young stellar object (YSO) embedded in the pillar, confirmed by Webb Near-Infrared Camera and Mask (NIRCam) L-band (3–5 um) and MIRI M-band (5–28 um) detections. Photoevaporation flow tracers — including H2 v=1-0 S(1) at 2.12 um, [Fe II] 1.64 um, and Paschen-alpha emission — are bright along the illuminated rim, consistent with UV radiation fields G0 > 10^4 in units of the Habing field impinging on a gas density n_H ~ 10^4–10^5 cm^-3 and gas temperature T ~ 500–1000 K. The pillar's survival time against photoevaporation is ~1–3 Myr, comparable to its estimated formation timescale. Stellar feedback (radiation + winds) from the Carina OB1 association drives a turbulent shell; the pillar likely formed via turbulent compressions within this shell. The Carina Nebula has been observed by Hubble (WFC3/UVIS, WFC3/IR, and ACS/WFC) since 2009 and by Webb as part of Director's Discretionary Time programs (PID 13968) in Cycle 1 and GTO programs (PID 12293, 13898, 27383) in Cycles 2–4. The complementary visible-infrared dataset demonstrates the diagnostic power of infrared transparency for resolving embedded YSOs, measuring reddening (A_V ~ 10–40 mag at visual wavelengths, reduced to A_K < 1 mag at 2 um), and mapping the photoevaporation flow kinematics (v_flow ~ 10–30 km/s, via [Fe II] linewidths). These observations support the broader picture that radiative feedback from massive stars regulates local star formation efficiency to ~5-15% on scales of ~0.5–2 pc.
