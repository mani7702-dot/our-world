The giant asteroid that drowned the North Sea in a 330-foot wall of water

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 20:34 IST
Reading options

Forty-three million years ago a 160-meter-wide space rock slammed into what is now the North Sea, launching a tsunami taller than a 30-story building. For decades scientists argued over what made the crater — but tiny minerals trapped in the rock finally told the truth.

How big was the splash?

Imagine dropping a bowling ball into a kiddie pool. Now imagine that ball is a rock the size of two blue whales swimming nose-to-nose, and the pool is an ancient sea bigger than many countries. That is roughly what happened 43 million years ago in what we now call the North Sea.

A space rock about 160 meters (525 feet) wide — that's longer than a rugby field — was flying through space when it hit Earth at a shallow angle. The crash was so powerful that it blasted a wall of rock and water 1.5 kilometers (about a mile) into the air — higher than the tallest buildings in most cities. Then came the wave.

Scientists who ran computer simulations say the impact triggered a tsunami more than 100 meters (330 feet) tall — that is, a wall of water thirty stories high racing across the ancient sea. To put that in scale: the Empire State Building is about 380 meters tall. This single wave was about a third of that.

The crater that hid for 20 years

The crash left a giant scar called the Silverpit Crater, sitting about 700 meters (2,300 feet) beneath the seabed, far off the coast of Yorkshire, England. It is nearly three kilometers across — big enough that if it were on land, it would fit twenty of your school fields inside it.

But here is the strange part: the crater lay hidden for decades under layers of mud and sand. Oil and gas companies first spotted its suspicious circular shape in 2002 while sending sound waves through the seafloor to map underground rocks. At first, scientists argued. Was it an asteroid? Or had underground salt — yes, real salt, moving slowly like cold honey — squished the rock layers into a ring that looked like an impact?

For twenty years the debate swung back and forth, with geologists even taking a vote in 2009.

The tiny crystals that solved everything

The answer came from the smallest evidence. Deep under the seafloor, near an old oil well, researchers found rare mineral crystals — grains of quartz and feldspar that had been squeezed so hard their insides warped. Normal geology cannot make those shapes. Only the crushing shock of a cosmic collision creates them.

Dr. Uisdean Nicholson and his team at Heriot-Watt University described these "shocked" minerals as a "needle in a haystack" — the crystals were hiding inside rock cuttings drilled from a well near the crater floor. Their discovery is the first direct physical proof that an asteroid, not salt, made Silverpit.

The findings appeared in Nature Communications, and Wikipedia's own article on the Silverpit Crater now agrees: the strike happened 46 to 43 million years ago, during a time when an inland sea covered the area and the planet was still warm enough for crocodiles to live in the Arctic.

Try this at home

Fill a big bowl (or the sink) with water. Drop a marble from waist height and watch the ring of waves spread out. Now imagine doing the same thing in a pool the size of a city — that ripple becomes a tsunami. You will not get a 330-foot wave, but you will see how a single splash makes circles that travel far.

Fun Fact

The Silverpit Crater is named after the Silver Pit — a real, naturally deep channel in the seafloor that generations of English fishermen already knew about long before satellites could see it. So the modern space mystery shares its name with a very old sea lane.

For grown-ups

The short version for curious grown-ups: the Silverpit Crater structure lies about 700 meters beneath the North Sea seabed, roughly 130 km (81 mi) off the Humber estuary of England. It was first spotted in 2002 from 3D seismic survey data and named after the nearby Silver Pit fishing ground. An international team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson of Heriot-Watt University used seismic imaging plus microscopic analysis of rock cuttings from a nearby oil well to find "shocked" quartz and feldspar crystals — minerals whose crystal lattices are deformed only by extreme shock pressures (10–13 GPa), as produced by hypervelocity impact. These grains are incompatible with the salt-withdrawal/salt-mobility explanation that had competed with the impact hypothesis since a 2009 Geological Society debate. The impact was from an asteroid ~160 m across striking at a shallow angle 43–46 million years ago, generating a landslide-generated tsunami in the Eocene inland sea that exceeded 100 m (roughly 330 ft). The discovery is reported in Nature Communications; a Wikipedia concept-level article on the Silverpit crater independently confirms the ~20 km crater diameter, 18 km disturbed zone, and the 43–46 Ma impact age (2025 shock-mineral evidence favouring impact origin over salt mobility).
