"How Did Old Recordings Bring a Nearly Lost Language Back to Life?"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 02:45 IST
Reading options

"A language once heard all over New Orleans nearly vanished within a single lifetime. Then a researcher found forgotten 1935 recordings hidden in an archive — and suddenly people could hear how Louisiana Creole really sounded."

How did old recordings bring a nearly lost language back to life?

Imagine a language spoken every single day in a busy port city — on the docks, in the markets, and in the songs drifting out of music halls. Then, within one lifetime, almost everyone stops speaking it. That is exactly what nearly happened to Louisiana Creole, a language once heard all across New Orleans in the United States.

Now, thanks to some dusty old recordings, people can finally hear it again.

A box of mysterious discs

In 1935, a trailblazing Black scholar and linguist named Lorenzo Dow Turner travelled to Louisiana with a recording machine. He asked everyday people — a former police officer, dock workers, local storytellers — to simply talk into it. He saved their voices on metal aluminium discs, each one labelled with nothing but a single word: "Louisiana."

Those discs were stored away in an archive at Indiana University and then almost forgotten. Decades later, Dr Oliver Mayeux from the University of Cambridge came across a mention of Turner's trip while reading about his life. He went looking for the discs — and found far more than he expected.

What is Louisiana Creole?

Louisiana Creole grew up in the 1700s. Most of its words come from French, but it is a language all its own, with its own grammar and its own colourful sayings. Dr Mayeux calls the bustling melting pot that created it a "linguistic pressure cooker": when people who spoke many different languages had to talk to each other every day, a brand-new language was born. Many people today call it Kouri-Vini.

Other recordings from the 1930s had captured only short snatches of sung Creole. Turner's discs were different — the only long stretch of ordinary conversation, and the only recordings of how people spoke it in the city of New Orleans.

Why this discovery matters

By the 1950s, Creole had mostly faded from the city, and only a few thousand speakers remain today, mostly older people in rural areas. But something hopeful is happening: young Louisianans have started teaching themselves Kouri-Vini, sharing lessons online so the language can travel to a new generation. Being able to hear the old voices makes that revival richer — learners can now copy real sounds, not just words written on a page.

Try this at home

Languages are like living things — they change as people use them!

  1. Interview a grandparent or older neighbour. Ask them to say a phrase in a language they grew up with, and record it (with their permission!).
  2. Compare one word across languages. How do you say "water" in English, Spanish, French, and Hindi? Do any sound alike?
  3. Start a family word list. Invent a fun code word only your family uses — congratulations, you just helped a tiny language grow.

Remember: the best way to keep a language alive is simply to use it.

Fun Fact

Louisiana Creole is a creole language — a brand-new language that forms when speakers of different languages need a shared way to talk. Creoles are spoken all over the world, from Haiti to Hawaii, and each one is a real, complete language with its own rules — never a "broken" version of another!

For grown-ups

"Louisiana Creole (called Kouri-Vini by many revivalists today) is a French-lexifier creole that emerged in 18th-century Louisiana as a lingua franca among French colonists, enslaved West Africans and their descendants, and free people of colour. Although most of its vocabulary is French, it has its own grammar, structure and idioms. Field recordings were made in 1935 by Lorenzo Dow Turner (1890-1972), a pioneering Black American linguist who later wrote 'Africanisms in the Gullah Dialect' (1949). Folklorist Alan Lomax had captured only short fragments of sung Creole in 1934 and 1937; the Turner discs are the only extended spoken audio and the only New Orleans-variety recordings from a period when the language was still widely spoken. Turner's aluminium discs, labelled simply 'Louisiana', were preserved and digitised by the Archives of Traditional Music at Indiana University. Dr Oliver Mayeux, of Cambridge's Faculty of Modern and Medieval Languages and Linguistics, matched the recordings to Turner's fieldwork by tracing his address book. Creole declined in New Orleans by the 1950s; only a few thousand native speakers remain today, and a youth-led revival is teaching the language again via social media."
