Why are Switzerland's glaciers melting so fast?

By Our World Published 23 October 2026 at 23:19 IST
Reading options

Switzerland's glaciers are losing ice faster than ever. In just five years, nearly 20% of the country's glacier ice has vanished — and some glaciers have disappeared entirely. Here is why the mountains are turning bare.

Report filed at 23:19 IST from the Our World news desk.

1. The ice that is slipping away

Switzerland is famous for its snow-capped peaks and long, winding rivers of ice called glaciers. But those glaciers are melting faster than scientists have ever measured. In just the single year 2026, the country's glaciers lost more than 5% of their total ice volume — and across five years that adds up to nearly 20%.

The trouble starts with two things working together. First, winter snow — the white blanket that protects glaciers through the cold months — has been disappearing. The winter of 2025 to 2026 was one of the ten least snowy on record for Switzerland. Without that fluffy shield, the hard, blue glacier ice is exposed to the Sun's rays all year round. Second, summer heat has been climbing higher than usual.

2. When the thermometer climbs above 4,000 metres

Here is a number that surprised even the scientists: in summer 2026, the altitude where snow turns to rain (called the freezing level) rose above 4,000 metres on 76 separate days. That is more than double the normal number. For comparison, the highest Swiss peaks — the Matterhorn and the Jungfrau — are just under 4,200 metres tall. On those 76 days, it was literally too warm for snow even at the tops of most mountains.

The Aletsch Glacier — Switzerland's largest, stretching nearly 23 kilometres through the Bernese Alps — took the hardest hit. It lost so much ice that scientists called it the worst melting season in living memory. Two smaller neighbours were even less fortunate: Bella Tola in the canton of Valais and Griessfirn in Glarus both vanished completely, leaving behind only bare rock where ice once stood.

3. The water that runs down

You might think that losing billions of tonnes of ice is only bad news — but meltwater is also a resource. Between July and September 2026, Switzerland's shrinking glaciers released about 2.2 trillion litres of water. That is enough to fill 880,000 Olympic-size swimming pools!

For now, that extra water eases summer droughts. But the glaciers are borrowing from tomorrow's supply to pay for today. Once the ice is gone, the trickle will stop — and the dry summers of the future will feel a lot hotter and thirstier.

Try this at home

Make a mini glacier model: fill a clear container with water, add a pinch of dirt or cocoa powder to make it look like rock, then freeze it overnight. Place a small marble or coin on top before freezing. When you take it out, run warm water over it and watch how the "glacier" melts unevenly — some parts drip away fast (the edges), while others linger (the thick parts). That is exactly how real glaciers behave as temperatures rise!

Fun Fact

The word "glacier" comes from the French word for "frozen," but in Switzerland they have been watching the ice for centuries. The Aletsch has been shrinking since records began in 1882 — but the rate of loss has tripled since the year 2000. Before it disappears entirely, it still holds enough ice to fill more than 15 million Olympic swimming pools... but that number is getting smaller every summer.

The annual GLAMOS (Glacier Monitoring Switzerland) mass-balance report is compiled by the Swiss Academy of Sciences (SCNAT) from field measurements taken at more than 140 glaciers by ETH Zurich, the University of Fribourg, and the University of Zurich. In the 2025/2026 balance year, glaciers lost 5%+ of their ice volume nationally — the third-worst year after 2022 and 2023. Average thinning was 2.5–4 metres, with up to 10 metres at lower elevations. The Aletsch Glacier (Wikipedia confirms ~23 km length, 15.4 km³ volume as of 2011) lost its most ice on record. Bella Tola and Griessfirn vanished entirely — a fate that Wikipedia's Glacier article notes is increasingly common as lower-elevation glaciers melt away first. The freezing level stayed above 4,000 m on 76 days in summer 2026 — more than double the normal count — because of a persistent high-pressure ridge that brought clear skies and intense daytime heating. Snow disappeared at 3,500 m by September, exposing glacier ice that is normally protected. The 2.2 trillion litres of meltwater released (July–September 2026) equals roughly 4× annual Swiss household consumption, providing short-term relief but accelerating long-term loss. For grown-ups

Switzerland's glaciers lost more than 5% of their total ice volume in 2026 alone — the third-worst year on record after 2022 and 2023. Between 2021 and 2026 the country lost nearly 20% of its glacier ice volume. The Swiss Academy of Sciences (SCNAT) compiles annual observations from the GLAMOS (Glacier Monitoring Switzerland) network at ETH Zurich, the University of Fribourg, and University of Zurich. In 2026, glacier thinning averaged 2.5–4 metres nationally, with up to 10 metres lost at lower elevations. The Aletsch Glacier — the Alps' largest, confirmed by Wikipedia to be ~23 km long with a 2011 volume of 15.4 km³ — experienced its most severe melting on record. Two smaller glaciers, Bella Tola (Valais) and Griessfirn (Glarus), disappeared entirely. The drivers were an exceptionally snowless winter (2025–26 ranked among the ten least snowy since records began) plus 76 days where the freezing level climbed above 4,000 m — more than double the normal number, setting a new Swiss record. By September, seasonal snow had vanished even at 3,500 m elevation. Meltwater released between July and September 2026 totalled approximately 2.2 trillion litres — enough to fill 880,000 Olympic swimming pools.
