"What is the golden tree coral that glows underwater off Costa Rica?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 16:37 IST
Reading options

"Scientists exploring underwater mountains off Costa Rica found a bright yellow coral so unusual that they had to invent a brand-new family name for it. What makes this 'golden tree of the stars' so special — and why did its genes refuse to agree?"

1. What is the golden tree coral that glows underwater off Costa Rica?

The yellow tree that grows on the ocean floor

Picture a tree made of glowing gold branches, swaying on the seafloor nearly half a kilometer below the waves. It is not a plant at all — it is a coral, and scientists exploring underwater mountains off Costa Rica just discovered it is so special that they had to create an entirely new family name just for it.

The coral was found on seamounts — underwater mountains formed from ancient volcanoes that rise up from the ocean floor without breaking the surface. Some colonies grow more than a meter tall, their bright yellow branches spreading wide like the limbs of an underwater tree. Around them, fields of brittle stars (relatives of starfish) cover the rocky seafloor, their spindly arms stretching out like tiny stars against the dark.

A Tolkien name for a starry treasure

When Dr. Odalisca Breedy of the Universidad de Costa Rica first saw the coral in 2019, she was struck by its beauty. Collecting specimens with a remotely operated vehicle called SuBastian during expeditions aboard the research ship Falkor, she realized this was no ordinary coral.

The coral's vivid yellow color and tree-like shape inspired its name. Scientists called the new genus Laurinque — from "Laurinquë," meaning "golden tree" in Quenya, the Elvish language invented by J.R.R. Tolkien. The species name elenya means "stellar" or "of the stars," because of the brittle stars glowing around the coral like a sky full of stars.

When genes disagree, use hundreds at once

Figuring out where this coral belonged on the family tree turned out to be trickier than expected. Scientists first sequenced three genes — the usual shortcuts for identifying coral families. But each gene told a different story! One gene said it was related to family A, another suggested family B. The results were so conflicting that the team suspected something very unusual was happening.

To solve the mystery, they used phylogenomics — a technique that reads hundreds of genes at the same time instead of just three. With this wider view, the picture became clear: the golden coral belonged to no known coral family at all. It was a brand-new evolutionary branch, most closely related to the Eunicellidae family, but different enough that it deserved its own — Laurinqueidae.

Try this at home

Make your own "seamount" ecosystem! Fill a clear bowl with water and add a tall yellow pipe cleaner (or strips of yellow felt) to represent the golden coral. Scatter small starfish-shaped stickers or drawings around the base to stand in for brittle stars. Ask yourself: if you found a new creature in your neighborhood, what Elvish or made-up name would you give it? Write a short "scientific name" with two parts — a genus name and a species name — just like real scientists do.

Fun Fact

Deep-sea corals like this golden tree coral are sometimes called the "forests of the deep." Unlike the colorful reef-building corals of shallow tropical waters, deep-sea corals live in perpetual darkness, often in shades of yellow, orange, or red. They do not rely on sunlight or algae — instead, they catch tiny plankton drifting past them with their tentacles, like underwater spider webs.

The coral was found at depths of 360-529 meters. Initial attempts to classify it using three standard genes produced conflicting results — each gene pointed to a different, unrelated family. For grown-ups

"A new species of deep-sea coral, Laurinque elenya, was discovered on seamounts off Costa Rica at depths of 360-529 meters during Schmidt Ocean Institute expeditions in 2019 and 2023. The coral's vivid yellow, tree-like branches and its unusual genetic makeup were so distinct that researchers created an entirely new family, Laurinqueidae. Scientists initially sequenced three genes commonly used for coral classification, but each gene pointed to a different, unrelated family. Only by using phylogenomics — comparing hundreds of genes at once — could they determine that the species belongs to a distinct evolutionary lineage most closely related to Eunicellidae, but different enough to warrant a new family. The genus name Laurinque comes from "Laurinquë," meaning "golden tree" in Quenya (the Elvish language created by J.R.R. Tolkien), and the species name elenya means "stellar" or "of the stars," referencing the brittle stars surrounding the colonies. Deep-sea corals (Octocorallia) are colonial marine invertebrates with eight-fold symmetry, found in deeper, colder ocean waters. Seamounts are underwater mountains formed from extinct volcanoes rising from the ocean floor.
