"Scientists discovered that ancient ice from a mountain glacier in Tibet is more than 128,000 years old — the oldest mountain ice ever found outside the polar regions. It preserves a rare record of Earth's climate from the last ice age."

Why did ice in Tibet survive 128,000 years? A frozen time capsule

Ice that remembers the last ice age

High in the mountains of northwestern Tibet, there is a glacier that has quietly kept a secret for a very, very long time. Deep inside its frozen heart, scientists have found ice that is more than 128,000 years old — older than any mountain ice ever recovered outside the polar regions. That means this ice was already frozen while woolly mammoths still roamed, and long before modern humans spread across the planet.

It is like finding a giant ice-cube time capsule that has been storing Earth's ancient climate for over a hundred thousand years.

Two drillings, one identical story

Here's the clever part: scientists did not drill this ice just once — they did it twice, in 1992 and again in 2015, 23 years apart. When they compared the two sets of ice cores (long cylinders of ice pulled up from deep inside the glacier), the layers of oxygen isotopes matched almost perfectly. Both samples told the exact same story about what the climate was like thousands of years ago.

That match matters. It proves the ice is a reliable, reproducible record — not a one-time accident — and that the glacier has kept its memory intact through dramatic changes in Earth's climate.

How do you date a 128,000-year-old ice cube?

You cannot just look at ice and guess its age. So scientists used a clever trick: they measured tiny amounts of radioactive beryllium and chlorine trapped inside the ice. These elements decay at a steady, known rate, like a natural clock. That clock revealed a special marker — the Laschamp Geomagnetic Excursion, a brief wobble in Earth's magnetic field that happened more than 41,000 years ago and left its chemical fingerprint in the ice.

By combining this clock with oxygen-isotope records from nearby Tibetan caves, the team concluded the Guliya ice may extend well past 128,000 years. It's the only record of its kind from the world's mountaintops, outside the great polar ice sheets. The research was published in the journal Science Advances.

Try this at home

Next time you see an ice cube, imagine it as a tiny record keeper. Every layer of ice — like the rings of a tree — holds clues about what the air and weather were like when that layer formed. A big glacier is like a whole library of these records, each page written in frozen water. What do you think the ice near your house would "remember" about today?

For grown-ups: the real mechanism

The Guliya ice cap's record was previously believed to reach back only ~12,000 years (the Holocene). This study uses cosmogenic radionuclides (¹⁰Be and ³⁶Cl) to detect the Laschamp Geomagnetic Excursion horizon, anchoring the core chronology beyond the Holocene. Reproducibility across the 1992 and 2015 drillings and correlation with Tibetan speleothem oxygen-isotope records supports a timescale exceeding 100,000 years — showing high-altitude Asian ice persisted through the last glacial-interglacial transition. This makes Guliya the oldest non-polar ice core record, providing a long-term window into low-latitude climate dynamics.
