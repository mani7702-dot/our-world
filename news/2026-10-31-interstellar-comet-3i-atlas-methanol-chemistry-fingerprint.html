"What Can the Alcohol in a Flying Comet Tell Us About Alien Oceans?"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at "11:02" IST
Reading options

"A space visitor from another star system is full of methanol — a type of alcohol — more than any comet we've ever studied in our own Solar System. By sniffing its comet's 'fingerprint,' astronomers are learning how alien planets and oceans might form."

What can the alcohol in a flying comet tell us about alien oceans?

A comet from another star is cruising through our Solar System — and when astronomers point one of the world's biggest radio eyes at it, they hear a surprising chemical secret: it's full of alcohol.

Not the kind you drink (and please don't try any). The alcohol here is methanol — the simplest kind, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. It is one of the first molecules that forms on icy dust grains in the cold dark between stars, and finding lots of it in a single visitor is like receiving a letter written in a foreign alphabet.

A space postcard with a strange recipe

When sunlight warms a comet, its ice turns straight into gas and leaves behind a glowing fuzzy "cloud" called a coma. By the time Earth's telescopes watched 3I/ATLAS approach the Sun in late 2025, that coma was glowing with two simple molecules the team could pick out like musical notes: methanol and hydrogen cyanide.

Here is the twist. In almost every comet from our own Solar System, hydrogen cyanide beats methanol by a long way — methanol is the quiet guest. But in 3I/ATLAS, methanol is the star of the show, appearing roughly 70 to 120 times more often than hydrogen cyanide. That ratio puts it among the most methanol-rich comets ever measured.

The fingerprint of another world

Because this comet was born around a different star, that strange ratio is a fingerprint — a record of the recipe of its birthplace. Methanol is fragile: ultraviolet light from a nearby star tends to break it apart. So to have so much of it today, 3I/ATLAS must have formed in a cold, shadowed region of its parent star system, where icy grains had time to build up methanol on their surfaces — a very different neighbourhood from where our own comets were cooked.

ALMA's sharp vision also revealed something else: the methanol does not all come from the comet's solid nucleus. Some is released by tiny icy grains floating in the coma itself, each one acting like a miniature comet of its own. As they are warmed by sunlight, they burst open and add more methanol to the glow.

Why should we care?

Every interstellar visitor is a sample-return mission we never had to launch. 3I/ATLAS is the third such object ever confirmed (after `Oumuamua in 2017 and Borisov in 2019), and each one lets us compare our cosmic home with another planetary system — without ever leaving Earth.

Try this at home

Make a "coma cloud" with a glass of fizzy water and a drop of milk. Shine a torch through it from the side. The milk particles swirl through the drink just like gas escapes from a comet's nucleus, scattering the light so you can see the beam. The brighter the glow, the more stuff is leaking out — just like astronomers measuring how much methanol a faraway comet is releasing.

Fun fact

Methanol ice is a key ingredient for building more complex organic molecules. In the right conditions it can help form the very compounds that, billions of years later, end up in the soups where life experiments begin. So in a very real sense, this little travelling comet is carrying a pre-packed starter kit from another star.

For grown-ups

"3I/ATLAS is the third known interstellar object to pass through our Solar System (after 'Oumuamua in 2017 and Borisov in 2019). It was discovered on 1 July 2025 by NASA's Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System (ATLAS) in Hawaii; its hyperbolic trajectory proves it did not originate in our Solar System and it poses no impact threat to Earth (closest approach was 1.8 AU). ALMA's Atacama Compact Array observed the comet's coma in late 2025 and measured submillimetre signatures of methanol (CH3OH) and hydrogen cyanide (HCN). The methanol-to-HCN production-rate ratio was measured at roughly 70 and 120 on two separate dates, making 3I/ATLAS among the most methanol-rich comets ever recorded — Solar System comets typically have the inverse ratio (HCN > CH3OH). ALMA's high-resolution imaging showed methanol is released both from the nucleus and from icy grains in the coma, each acting like a 'mini-comet' as sunlight warms them. The result is interpreted as evidence that 3I/ATLAS's ice formed, or was chemically processed, under conditions different from those that shaped most Solar System comets, where molecular nitrogen ice is abundant; the presence of abundant CH3OH implies a formation environment richer in water ice and shielded from the ultraviolet light that breaks methanol apart. The James Webb Space Telescope independently reported the comet's coma was dominated by carbon dioxide when it was farther from the Sun. The ALMA study appears in Astrophysical Journal Letters (Roth et al.). Methanol in the interstellar medium forms primarily on icy dust-grain surfaces at temperatures of ~10-20 K, so its strong presence in 3I/ATLAS points to a formation zone in the outer, colder regions of its parent star system, rather than the warmer inner disk. The finding is part of ALMA's broader program of chemically fingerprinting individual interstellar objects, a field that has expanded rapidly since the first detection of such a visitor."
