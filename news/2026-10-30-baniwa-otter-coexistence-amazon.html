"How the Baniwa People and Otters Share the Amazon Rivers"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 02:45 IST
Reading options

"In Brazil's Amazon rainforest, the Baniwa Indigenous people and wild otters have learned to share the same rivers — not through avoiding each other, but through a quiet dance of mutual respect that lets both thrive."

How the Baniwa People and Otters Share the Amazon Rivers

1. Two neighbors in the same river

Deep in the Amazon rainforest, something remarkable happens every day. Along the Içana River in Brazil, Indigenous Baniwa fisher families and wild otters both head out to catch fish — and they do it without chasing each other away. How did they learn to share the same water?

The Baniwa people have lived along these rivers for generations. They know every bend, every quiet pool where fish gather. The giant otters — sleek, social hunters that live in family groups of up to twelve — know the same rivers well too. Both need fish to survive, so you might expect a fight. But instead, they found a way to make space.

2. A dance of respect

The researchers watched giant otters hunting near the riverbank, their heads popping up and diving like an arcade game of Whack-A-Mole. Meanwhile, Baniwa men checked their fishing traps further downstream. The secret? Timing and territory.

The Baniwa fish for different species at different times of day. They leave the otters' favorite hunting spots alone, and in return, the otters do not tear up their nets or steal their catch. It is not just practical — it is cultural. Many Baniwa see the otters as "pajés das águas," spiritual guardians who protect the fish and keep the rivers healthy.

3. Lessons from a near-loss

This harmony was almost lost in the mid-1900s. Commercial fur traders nearly wiped out both the giant otter and the neotropical otter for their velvety pelts. Otter populations crashed, and with them, the delicate balance the Baniwa had built.

But both species are recovering. The giant otter, once listed as endangered, is slowly returning to the rivers. The neotropical otter — a more solitary cousin that prefers clear, fast-flowing streams away from its boisterous giant relative — is also making a comeback. The Baniwa's knowledge of how to live alongside these animals helped the recovery, and today both otters and people thrive side by side.

4. Try this at home

Next time you visit a pond, stream, or even your local park, try otter-style observation:

  • Watch for signs of animal paths and favorite spots
  • Notice when different animals use the same space at different times
  • Think about how people and wildlife might share the area you live in

Even a small garden or balcony can become a place where humans and animals find their own quiet ways to coexist.

Fun Fact

The giant otter is the noisiest otter in the world! Scientists have documented distinct vocalizations that mean different things — alarm calls, greetings, and even reassurance sounds parents make to their pups. Their families work together like a well-rehearsed team, hunting and playing and even taking turns babysitting the youngest otters while the adults rest.

For grown-ups

"A 2026 study by Jansen Zuanon and colleagues tracked Baniawa Indigenous fishers along the Içana River in Brazil's upper Rio Negro Basin. The researchers observed how both giant otters (Pteronura brasiliensis) and neotropical otters (Lontra longicaudis) hunt fish in the same waterways. The Baniwa have developed an intricate system of coexistence: they fish different species at different times, respect the otters' feeding areas, and in return the otters do not damage their nets or steal their catch. The study found that coexistence depends on more than just avoiding conflict — it is shaped by history, behavioral adaptation, cultural beliefs, and local governance. Historically, the mid-1900s commercial fur trade nearly wiped out both otter species, which disrupted the relationship between the Baniwa people and the otters for generations. The Baniwa regard otters as 'pajés das águas,' or spiritual guardians who protect fish and the aquatic world. This cultural belief, combined with practical knowledge passed down through generations, has helped both species recover."
