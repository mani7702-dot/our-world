How a soybean compound may shield the brain from too much salt

By Our World Published 21 October 2026 at 09:35 IST
Reading options

Eating too much salt can harm memory and social behavior, not just blood pressure. Japanese researchers found that a natural compound from soy helps restore balance in the body's signaling chemicals.

Why salt does more than raise blood pressure

We all know too much salt raises blood pressure. But scientists are discovering it may do something else, too: it can scramble the brain chemistry that helps us remember things and get along with others.

Eating a very salty diet doesn't just stress the heart and blood vessels. It can also leave mice less good at recognizing objects they have already seen, and less interested in playing with other mice. That is a real problem — because remembering and socializing are jobs the brain's prefrontal cortex works hard to do.

A soybean superhero molecule

A research team in Japan, led by Professor Akihiro Mouri and Dr. Hisayoshi Kubota, had an idea. They looked at LPC70, a natural compound from soy. It is a special kind of phospholipid (a fat the body uses to build cell membranes) that soybeans make in large amounts.

Past work had hinted that LPC70 might ease salt problems. The new study, published in Neurochemistry International, looked deep inside the biology to ask: how does it actually work?

The body's tiny mail system

The brain and kidneys talk to each other using signaling chemicals called prostaglandins. Think of them as tiny biochemical "mail" — local hormones made from a fatty acid called arachidonic acid that tell cells what to do.

Here is the messy part. When mice ate a super-salty diet:

  • In their kidneys, salt turned up two parts of the mail system — a protein called COX-2 and a receiver called EP3 — that push blood pressure higher.
  • In their brains, the same salty diet scrambled the prostaglandin signals that help with memory and social behavior.

But when the salty-eating mice also got LPC70 in their food, the signaling chemicals returned to a healthier balance — both in kidney and in brain.

The proof in the experiments

The numbers told a clear story:

  • Blood pressure went down.
  • Mice remembered objects better in memory tests.
  • Their social behavior bounced back — they were more interested in interacting with other mice again.

So one molecule, working through the same prostaglandin system, helped fix three different problems caused by salt. It is like finding a single key that fixes a door, a window, and a gate, all at once.

What this means for you

This is early science — mice, not people. But it fits a big pattern: what we eat changes the body's chemistry every single day, and tiny signaling molecules matter more than we used to think. The real message for curious readers is simple — balance is the body's superpower, and it is a good reason to taste your food before you reach for the salt shaker.

Try this at home

Make two clear cups of water. Dissolve one teaspoon of salt in one and leave the other plain. Taste both. The salty one should taste stronger and "sharper." That sharpness is salt changing how your tongue send signals — the same kind of signaling system the brain scientists studied, just in a much simpler form you can try safely.

Fun Fact: Prostaglandins don't just work in the brain and kidneys — they are involved in inflammation, fever, and even how a baby's body gets ready for birth. One family of molecules, dozens of jobs. Nature reuses good ideas.

For grown-ups

The short version for curious grown-ups: the study is a mouse experiment — effects in people are not known. The compound LPC70 is a soy-derived form of lysolecithin (>70% lysophosphatidylcholine), tested by Mouri/Kubota at Fujita Health University (Neurochemistry International, July 1 2026, Vol 197). Mechanism: high salt intake raises blood pressure and impairs memory by dysregulating prostaglandin (PG) signaling — PGs are arachidonic-acid derivatives that act as local hormones. In kidneys, salt increases COX-2 and the PGE2-EP3 receptor (raising BP); LPC70 reduced that. In the prefrontal cortex it rebalanced PG signaling and restored memory and social behavior in mice. This is basic neuroscience of signaling pathways, not human treatment advice.
