How were zebra sharks almost lost forever? Scientists rewilding them with a bold new plan

The stripes that saved a species

Meet the zebra shark — not actually a zebra, and not really a "shark" in the dangerous sense. These gentle, spotted fish are the ocean's gardeners, gobbling up dead matter from the seafloor and keeping coral reefs healthy. But their name comes from their babies: when a baby zebra shark hatches from its egg, it is covered in bold black stripes on a yellowish-brown back — like a tiny tiger-striped caterpillar of the sea.

When only 20 were left

By 2019, in the crystal waters around Indonesia's Raja Ampat archipelago, fewer than 20 adult zebra sharks remained. Locals had been hunting them for their fins, and the species' slow reproduction — they take 5-12 years just to reach sexual maturity — meant they could not bounce back on their own. The IUCN Red List classified the zebra shark as Endangered. Something bold was needed.

The world's first shark rewilding project

A group called ReShark teamed up with zoos and aquariums across 15 countries to hatch a plan that had never been tried before: rewilding sharks. Usually, rewilding is for land animals like wolves or bears. But zebra sharks are special — they lay tough eggs that can be safely shipped across the ocean, giving conservationists a rare advantage.

Dozens of aquariums sent their extra zebra shark eggs to special nurseries in Raja Ampat. There, trained "shark nannies" (aquatic animal husbandry specialists) carefully reared the pups until they were big enough — about 1 meter, or just over 3 feet — to release. After three years of planning, the first 82 pups were set free into a massive 46,000-square-kilometer sanctuary where they are protected from illegal fishing. Each pup was fitted with a tiny satellite tag so scientists can track their adventures.

Will they start families in the wild?

The real test won't come until 2028, when scientists hope to see something never recorded before: captive-bred zebra sharks mating and producing wild-born babies in Indonesia. If it works, it could open the door for rewilding other shark — and ray — species around the world.

ReShark aims to release 200 to 300 zebra sharks over the next decade, giving this striped sea hero a real second chance.

Try this at home

Draw a spiral galaxy of dots on a piece of paper — that is your "reef." Using a fork or stiff brush, drag paint from the center outward in wavy lines. Those lines are like reef currents. Drop a cotton ball (your "zebra shark") into the middle and tilt the paper. Watch how the shark's movement — stirring up the swirling currents — distributes the "food" evenly across the reef. That is one reason sharks are called "ecosystem engineers": they keep ocean life balanced just by swimming!

Fun Fact

Despite their name, zebra sharks have nothing to do with zebras. They were named by ancient mariners who thought the striped babies looked like baby zebras. But as they grow, their spots multiply and their stripes fade — adults look totally different from their younger selves, a phenomenon called ontogenetic color change. No wonder "Indo-Pacific leopard shark" is their other, more accurate name!

For grown-ups

"The zebra shark (Stegostoma tigrinum), also known as the Indo-Pacific leopard shark, is the only living member of the family Stegostomatidae and is listed as Endangered (EN) on the IUCN Red List. Overhunting for their fins had driven wild populations in Indonesia's Raja Ampat to as few as 20 adults by 2019. The StAR Project (Stegostoma tigrinum Augmentation and Recovery), led by ReShark and the West Papua Provincial Government, launched the world's first endangered shark rewilding program. Over three years, an international consortium of 60+ aquariums across 15 countries bred zebra sharks in captivity and shipped viable eggs to protected nurseries in Raja Ampat and Phuket, Thailand. There, 'shark nannies' (trained aquatic animal husbandry specialists) reared 82 pups before releasing them into a 46,000-square-kilometer shark and manta ray sanctuary. The pups, released at about 1 meter long, were satellite-tagged to monitor their foraging behavior and survival rates. The project aims to release 200-300 individuals to restore a self-sustaining population within 6-10 years, with early results expected by 2028."
