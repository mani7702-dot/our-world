"Why Did a Skull-Shaped Space Rock Fly Past Earth on Halloween?"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at "19:50" IST
Reading options

"Eleven years ago tonight, a dark, 650-metre asteroid nicknamed the 'Great Pumpkin' swept safely past Earth — and radar pictures revealed a spooky skull-like face. Here's how astronomers tracked it."

On the night of 31 October 2015, a dark lump of rock about 650 metres wide swept past Earth — closer than the Moon. Astronomers had spotted it only three weeks earlier, and when radar pictures came back, the face staring out of the data looked unmistakably spooky. NASA nicknamed it the "Great Pumpkin."

There was never any danger: it passed about 1.27 lunar distances away and kept going. But the story of how we saw it is a great example of how scientists track the space rocks near us.

A rock that showed up late

The object, officially called 2015 TB145, was first spotted on 10 October 2015 by the Pan-STARRS survey telescopes in Hawaii. At the time it was incredibly faint — about magnitude 20, far too dim for your eyes. The survey caught it because its telescopes photograph the whole sky over and over, hunting for anything that moves.

By Halloween, the asteroid was racing past at 17:01 UTC (about 10:31 p.m. India time). Even at its brightest it stayed a tough target, washed out by an 80% Moon.

The "skull" in the radar data

Optical telescopes see sunlight bouncing off an object, but radar astronomy works differently: big dish antennas shout radio waves at the asteroid and listen for the echo. This lets astronomers measure its shape, spin and surface in the dark.

Three giant facilities joined in — Arecibo in Puerto Rico, the Green Bank Telescope in West Virginia and NASA's Goldstone antenna in California. Arecibo's images, taken on 30 October 2015, sharpened to about 7.5 metres per pixel. That is where the famous skull-like shape appeared, giving the rock its other nicknames: the "Halloween Asteroid" and the "Skull Asteroid."

A dead comet?

Here is the fun twist. 2015 TB145 is a very dark, charcoal-like world with an albedo of just 0.06 — it reflects only about one part in sixteen of the light that hits it. Its orbit is also steeply tilted (about 40°) and stretched into a long oval.

That combination makes astronomers think it may be an extinct comet: a comet that made too many trips around the Sun, ran out of the ices that create a glowing tail, and quietly settled down to look like a bare asteroid.

Try this at home

You can model radar astronomy with sound. Stand at one end of a big room and clap once. Listen to how the echo changes when you face a wall, a sofa, or an open doorway. Radar astronomers do the same thing with radio "claps" — reading the shape of something far away from the echo that bounces back.

Be a sky survey: pick a patch of night sky and sketch the bright dots you see. A week later, sketch the same patch. Most stars will be exactly where you left them — but if a dot has moved, you may have found something wandering, just like Pan-STARRS does with a camera.

Fun Fact

2015 TB145 comes back, but it likes its space. After being hidden for almost three years, it was recovered in October 2018 and passed again on 11 November 2018 — this time a safe 0.267 AU away, roughly a hundred times farther than its Halloween visit.

A bigger one is coming

The last object this large and bright to brush so close was asteroid 2004 XP14, back in 2006. The next one is 1999 AN10, about 800 metres across, expected to pass roughly 1 lunar distance from Earth on 7 August 2027. It, too, is not on a collision course — and thanks to surveys like Pan-STARRS, we will be watching.

For grown-ups

"On this day in space: 2015 TB145, a sub-kilometre near-Earth asteroid of the Apollo group, made its closest approach on 31 October 2015 at 17:01 UTC, passing 1.27 lunar distances (about 486,000 km / 302,000 mi) from Earth and 0.75 lunar distances from the Moon — no impact threat. Discovered 10 October 2015 by Pan-STARRS in Hawaii at magnitude 20, it is roughly 650 m across with a very dark albedo of about 0.06. NASA nicknamed it the 'Great Pumpkin'; radar imaging by the Arecibo Observatory (7.5 m per pixel, 30 Oct 2015) produced the skull-like frame that earned it the 'Skull Asteroid' and 'Halloween Asteroid' names. Its high orbital inclination (39.7°) and eccentricity (0.86) suggest it may be an extinct comet that has shed its volatiles. The last object this bright (absolute magnitude brighter than 20) to pass so close was (612901) 2004 XP14 in 2006; the next comparably large object, (137108) 1999 AN10, will pass about 1 lunar distance from Earth on 7 August 2027. The asteroid was recovered in October 2018 and passed again on 11 November 2018, but at a safe 0.267 AU. Radar studies used NASA's Goldstone antenna, the Green Bank Telescope and Arecibo. No impact-risk claim is made anywhere in this piece."
