"How a Zombie Star Creates a Cosmic 'String of Pearls': The Supernova That Astronomers Saw in 1181"

How a Zombie Star Creates a Cosmic "String of Pearls"?

The ghost star that should not exist

About 850 years ago, astronomers in China and Japan looked up and saw a "guest star" blazing in the constellation Cassiopeia — so bright it was visible during the day for weeks. That star was SN 1181, one of only five supernovas that humans have seen in our own Milky Way galaxy before telescopes existed.

Now, with the Gemini North telescope in Hawaii, modern astronomers are peering into the wreckage of that ancient explosion. What they found looks like something from a dream: a cosmic "string of pearls" — dandelion-shaped knots of gas and dust stretched across space, each one wider than our entire Solar System. And at the very center of it all sits a zombie star — a dead star that somehow survived its own explosion.

What is a "zombie star"?

Normally, when a white dwarf (the dense, Earth-sized remnant of a star like our Sun) pulls matter from a nearby companion star, it balloons toward a critical mass. When it crosses that line, a runaway nuclear fusion reaction tears the white dwarf apart in a brilliant Type Ia supernova — a stellar detonation so thorough that no part of the original star survives.

But sometimes nature takes a gentler path. In a Type Iax supernova, the explosion is weaker — like a misfiring engine instead of a roaring one. It blasts off only part of the white dwarf's mass, leaving behind a shattered but still gravitationally bound core. That survivor — still glowing from leftover heat, still spinning, still breathing out stellar wind — is called a zombie star.

Pa 30's zombie star (catalogued WD J005311) is one of the most extreme examples ever found. It is shedding material at a staggering 16,000 kilometers per second, and its surface glows at a temperature near 200,000 degrees Celsius — hotter than almost any known star.

The pearls: gas knots wider than our Solar System

The Gemini North telescope captured the Pa 30 supernova remnant in unprecedented detail. What it revealed is breathtaking: long, thin filaments of gas and supernova debris stretch outward from the explosion site like the stems of a dandelion. Along those stems sit perfect, round knots — each one a glowing "pearl."

"The planetary region of our solar system could fit in each knot about ten times with room to spare," explains astronomer Ilaria Caiazzo. These pearls are so large and uniform that they look almost artificial — a cosmic necklace strung by a stellar explosion.

Why the zombie stayed centered

Here is another mystery: when a supernova explodes, the leftover core should get a kick — a gravitational shove that sends it flying away from the blast center. Yet Pa 30's zombie star sits almost perfectly at the heart of the remnant, as if the explosion never happened at all.

This tells astronomers that the original supernova was remarkably symmetric — the blast energy flowed outward in nearly all directions, giving the star almost no sideways push. It is the stellar equivalent of a perfectly balanced firework that lifts straight up without wobbling.

Try this at home

You can make your own cosmic "dandelion" using a balloon and glitter:

  1. Blow up a balloon and let it sit for a minute.
  2. Sprinkle a pinch of glitter on a flat surface.
  3. Hold the balloon just above the glitter and rub it on your hair or a wool sweater to build up static electricity.
  4. Bring the balloon close to the glitter — the static charge will pull the glitter toward the balloon without touching it, and the "pearls" will jump and stick.

The electric force here is like the supernova's blast: it shapes and rearranges particles into new patterns.

Fun Fact

Pa 30 is the only known supernova remnant that contains a visible zombie star — making it a one-of-a-kind laboratory for studying how stars are born, explode, and sometimes, refuse to die. The remnant lies about 7,500 light-years away in Cassiopeia, and the "guest star" of 1181 AD that ancient astronomers observed would be 850 times older by now — a blink of an eye on cosmic timescales.

For grown-ups

"Pa 30 (IRAS 00500+6713) is the remnant of SN 1181, a Type Iax supernova observed by Chinese and Japanese astronomers between August 4 and August 6 in the year 1181 AD — one of only five pre-telescopic supernovae confidently identified in the Milky Way. Like all Type Ia supernovae, SN 1181 began when a white dwarf in a binary system accreted matter from a companion star until it approached the Chandrasekhar limit (~1.44 solar masses), triggering runaway carbon fusion. But unlike a normal Type Ia supernova that fully unbinds the white dwarf, Type Iax supernovae can leave behind a bound remnant — the so-called 'zombie star.' Pa 30's central star (WD J005311) is one of the most extreme white dwarfs known: it has record-breaking wind speeds of 16,000 km/s, a surface temperature near 200,000 K, and may be a rapidly-rotating super-Chandrasekhar white dwarf (mass >1.5 M☉) that could itself collapse into a neutron star within ~10,000 years. Gemini North telescope observations revealed dandelion-shaped filaments of gas and dust radiating outward like pearls on a string, each knot wide enough to fit the entire planetary region of our Solar System ten times over. The remnant lies ~7,500 light-years away in the constellation Cassiopeia. The research was published in The Astrophysical Journal."
