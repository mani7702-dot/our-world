"How did cryo-electron microscopy reveal why statins cause muscle pain?"

By Our World Published 25 October 2026 at 10:34 IST
Reading options

"Scientists used cryo-electron microscopy to see, almost atom by atom, how statin molecules latch onto a muscle cell's calcium channel and hold it open — causing the calcium leak that leads to muscle pain."

1. How did cryo-electron microscopy reveal why statins cause muscle pain?

A tiny channel gone rogue

Inside every muscle cell is a protein channel called the ryanodine receptor (say: ry-an-O-deen). Think of it as a tiny gate in a storage tank. The tank holds calcium — a mineral that muscle cells need to contract and relax. When your muscle needs to flex, this gate swings open and releases a controlled burst of calcium. Then it closes again, keeping everything balanced.

But what happens when something jams that gate open? Scientists at the University of British Columbia and the University of Wisconsin-Madison used one of the most powerful imaging tools in biology to find out — and they discovered that statin molecules are the culprits. Statins are cholesterol-lowering medicines taken by millions of people worldwide. For most users they work perfectly. But for some, they cause a side effect: muscle pain.

Seeing proteins with an electron microscope

To understand why statins cause muscle pain, the researchers needed to see exactly what the molecules were doing at the scale of individual atoms — far too small for ordinary light microscopes to resolve. They turned to cryo-electron microscopy, or cryo-EM.

Cryo-EM works like this: scientists flash-freeze protein samples in ultra-thin ice (so fast that water freezes without forming crystals, preserving the molecules in their natural shape). Then they blast the samples with a beam of electrons, taking thousands of pictures from every angle. Finally, powerful computers stitch all those images together into a 3D map at near-atomic resolution.

The reason this was so tricky is that the ryanodine receptor is one of the largest protein channels ever discovered — weighing in at over 2 million Daltons. Traditional methods like X-ray crystallography struggle with such massive, flexible proteins. But cryo-EM excels at them, which is why it has revolutionized structural biology in recent years.

Three molecules, one big problem

When the researchers examined the structure, they saw something remarkable. Three statin molecules — specifically atorvastatin — had latched onto a specific pocket inside the ryanodine receptor. It was like three tiny keys getting stuck in a lock:

  1. The first molecule binds when the channel is closed, nudging it to open.
  2. The second and third molecules then rush in, jamming the channel into its open position.
  3. With the gate forced open, calcium leaks continuously out of storage, building up to harmful levels inside the cell.

Under normal conditions, the ryanodine receptor releases calcium in controlled bursts, then seals itself back up. But when statins hold it open, the calcium flood triggers muscle damage and pain — explaining why some people experience soreness, weakness, or even severe complications.

This was the first time anyone had seen, almost atom by atom, exactly how statins interact with the muscle calcium channel. The detailed structure gives drug developers a clear blueprint for redesigning statins that keep the cholesterol benefits without the muscle side effects.

Why this matters

More than 200 million people worldwide take statins. Most experience no problems, but the muscle pain side effect is one of the most common reasons people stop taking their medication. Now that scientists can see the precise molecular interaction — down to three molecules wedged in a single pocket — they know exactly which parts of the statin molecule are responsible for the unwanted effect.

By modifying those specific features, researchers may be able to redesign statins that lower cholesterol just as well, without jamming open the muscle calcium channel. That means future patients could take the benefits without the aches.

Try this at home

You can't operate a cryo-electron microscope at home, but you can explore how molecules fit together — like keys in locks! Find three differently shaped objects around the house (a pen, a spoon, and a key, for example). Try to get them to all hang on or grip the same spot on a wire coat hanger at the same time. If one shape makes it easier for the others to "stick," you're modeling how the first statin molecule makes it easier for the other two to force the gate open!

Fun Fact

The 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank, and Richard Henderson "for developing cryo-electron microscopy." Before their breakthroughs, scientists thought biological molecules would be destroyed by electron beams in a vacuum. Cryo-EM proved that if you freeze samples to liquid-nitrogen temperatures and image them fast enough, you can capture near-perfect snapshots of life's tiniest machines.

for-grown-ups

Researchers at the University of British Columbia (UBC), working with colleagues at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, used cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) to determine the atomic-level structure of the ryanodine receptor (RyR1) in complex with atorvastatin, a commonly prescribed statin. Cryo-EM works by flash-freezing protein samples in vitreous ice and imaging them from thousands of angles with an electron beam, then computationally reconstructing a 3D density map at near-atomic resolution. The ryanodine receptor is one of the largest known ion channels (over 2 megadaltons), making it a challenging but ideal target for cryo-EM — traditional X-ray crystallography struggles with such large, flexible membrane proteins. The team found that three atorvastatin molecules simultaneously occupy a specific pocket within the RyR1 channel. The first molecule binds when the channel is closed, making it more likely to open. Two additional molecules then enter and hold the channel open permanently, allowing calcium to continuously leak out of the sarcoplasmic reticulum into the muscle cell cytoplasm. Under normal conditions, RyR1 releases calcium only when muscles need to contract, then closes to restore calcium balance. When statins force the channel open, calcium accumulates in the cell, triggering muscle damage and pain. This is the first time scientists have had a clear, near-atomic picture of how statins interact with the muscle calcium channel. Published in Nature Communications (2026).
