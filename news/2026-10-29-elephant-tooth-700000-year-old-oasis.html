"What can a 700,000-year-old elephant tooth tell us?"

By Our World Published 29 October 2026 at "23:13" IST
Reading options

"A giant elephant's fossil tooth has helped scientists rebuild a watery landscape of woods, grasses and marshes that early humans also used."

What can a 700,000-year-old elephant tooth tell us?

A tooth with a landscape hidden inside

Imagine holding a tooth bigger than your hand and using it to picture a whole lost world. Scientists have done almost exactly that with the fossil molar of a straight-tusked elephant from Gesher Benot Ya'aqov, an archaeological site in northern Israel.

The elephant lived slightly less than 700,000 years ago. Its molar and pieces of tusk were found in the same layer as stone handaxes and cleavers made by early humans. That does not show that people hunted this particular elephant, but it does place giant animals and human toolmakers in the same ancient setting.

How do you read a fossil tooth?

The team did not simply measure the tooth and stop. They used CT scans, looked at tiny marks on its surface, and tested chemical signatures preserved in its enamel. Plants and water leave different chemical clues, so an animal's teeth can carry hints about what it ate and the places where it drank.

The elephant's carbon signature changed very little across samples representing several years. That suggests it kept eating similar plants in a reliably watered place, such as woodland or the green edges of a river. It was not wandering through an empty, dry wasteland.

Pollen adds the scenery

Tiny fossil pollen grains in nearby sediment supplied another set of clues. Reeds, sedges and pondweed pointed to marshes and shallow water. Other pollen indicated open oak woodland and grasses, with willow and tamarisk also present.

Put together, the clues reveal a patchwork of woods, grassland, rivers and wetlands. This resource-rich corner of the Jordan Valley sits on a natural route between Africa and Eurasia. Reliable water and food could have made it a useful stopping place for animals and early human groups on the move.

Try this at home

Become a landscape detective in a park or garden. Write down every clue to water you can spot: mud, reeds, smooth pebbles, water-loving insects or a stream. Then list the plants growing in sunny and shady spots. You are doing the same basic job as the researchers — using several small clues to describe a habitat — without disturbing anything.

For grown-ups: the real mechanism

Tooth enamel preserves stable carbon and oxygen isotope signatures that reflect diet and drinking water. Serial sampling can reveal variation across the years while a large molar formed; in this case, stable carbon values indicate persistent use of well-watered vegetation. Microscopic wear adds dietary evidence, while fossil pollen independently reconstructs local plant communities. The researchers combined these records with CT imaging and 3D reconstruction. Their estimate of slightly under 700,000 years comes from the dated volcanic layer beneath the fossil-bearing deposit, not from directly dating the tooth. The nearby tools establish association, not a hunting event.
