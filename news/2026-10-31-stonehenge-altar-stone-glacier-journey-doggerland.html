How Did a 6-Ton Stone Travel 700 Kilometres to Stonehenge?

Stonehenge is one of the world's most famous ancient monuments — a ring of giant stones on a plain in England, built thousands of years before the invention of the wheel. But one of its stones holds a puzzle that has kept scientists guessing for years.

That stone, called the Altar Stone, didn't come from anywhere nearby. It travelled roughly 700 kilometres (435 miles) from north-east Scotland. So how did a six-tonne rock (about as heavy as a large elephant, twice over) get so far?

A rock from a lost lake

The Altar Stone is made of sandstone that formed long ago in the Orcadian Basin, an ancient lake in northern Scotland. Somehow it ended up in southern England, where it became the centrepiece of Stonehenge.

Some people wondered whether glaciers did the hard work. During the last ice age, huge rivers of ice covered much of Britain and slowly pushed rock and debris along with them. Could a glacier have carried the Altar Stone south?

What the new study found

A team from Sheffield Hallam University and Curtin University in Australia tested that idea. They combined mineral dating of the stone's grains with computer models of the ancient ice sheet.

Their result: glaciers could have carried the stone part of the way — from Scotland as far as Dogger Bank, an area in the North Sea. That region was once dry land called Doggerland, a landscape that is now underwater.

But the models showed there was no realistic ice route all the way to Stonehenge. After Dogger Bank, people would still have had to move the stone hundreds of kilometres themselves.

A journey in stages?

This is where the story gets exciting. If the stone rested in Doggerland for a while, the people living there might have valued it long before Stonehenge existed. As sea levels rose at the end of the ice age, they may have moved it to safety — and later, again, toward its final home.

"Rather than being carried naturally by ice, the evidence points to a deliberate, carefully planned movement across a challenging and varied landscape," said Dr Anthony Clarke, a co-lead author.

Moving such a giant rock would have taken planning, teamwork and deep knowledge of the land — proof that these early communities were far more organised than we once imagined.

Try this at home

Be an ancient engineer! Find a heavy book and a smooth floor. Try sliding it on a towel (like a sledge, dragged overland) and then on two rolling pencils (like logs used as rollers). Which way is easier? Ancient builders may have used similar tricks to move stones, wood, and even boats.

Fun fact: Doggerland was full of rivers, marshes and forests — a real home for people thousands of years ago. Today it lies hidden beneath the North Sea, and fishing boats sometimes pull up ancient tools and bones from it.

For grown-ups

"A study published in the Journal of Quaternary Science (DOI 10.1002/jqs.70080) by researchers co-led by Sheffield Hallam University and Curtin University, with the University of Sheffield, Wessex Archaeology and the University of Bristol, has proposed a new, plausible route for part of the Altar Stone's journey to Stonehenge. Combining mineral grain dating with numerical models of Britain's last ice sheet (the British–Irish Ice Sheet), the team found that glaciers could have carried the six-tonne sandstone megalith from the Orcadian Basin in north-east Scotland as far as Dogger Bank in the North Sea — part of the now-submerged landmass called Doggerland — but found no viable glacial pathway into southern England. The researchers therefore conclude that human transport of at least several hundred kilometres was still required, likely in stages combining overland hauling with river or coastal movement, possibly via the Berkshire Ridgeway. Co-lead authors: Dr Remy Veness (Sheffield Hallam) and Dr Anthony Clarke (Curtin University's Timescales of Minerals Systems Group). The authors hypothesise that Doggerland communities may have valued the stone culturally and moved it twice: first to save it from rising seas at the end of the last ice age, then again toward Salisbury Plain."
