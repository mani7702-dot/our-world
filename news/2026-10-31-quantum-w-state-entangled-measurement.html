"How Do You Measure a Team of Three Photons? The Quantum Puzzle That Took 25 Years"

By Our World Published 31 October 2026 at 08:45 IST
Reading options

"Three tiny particles of light can be linked so deeply that they act like one team. For 25 years, scientists could not check on that team — until now."

How do you measure a team of three photons?

Imagine three friends who always finish each other's sentences. You cannot describe one of them on their own — you have to describe the team. In the tiny world of quantum physics, particles of light called photons can be linked in exactly that way. The link is called entanglement, and it famously puzzled even Albert Einstein.

Once photons are entangled, they stop having separate "realities". Their properties are shared, so the group has to be treated as one whole thing.

Two famous quantum "team photos"

Physicists describe these three-photon teams with maths, and two patterns show up again and again. One is called the GHZ state; the other, the stranger one, is the W state.

They are not the same, even though both link three photons. Think of the GHZ state as three friends who all shout the same word at once, and the W state as three friends where one is speaking while the other two listen — but you never know in advance which one will speak.

Making these states is one challenge. Checking which one you have made is another.

Why checking is so hard

The usual way to inspect a quantum state is a method called quantum tomography. It works by taking lots of measurements and piecing the picture back together, like reconstructing a photo from thousands of tiny clues.

The problem is that for every extra photon, the number of measurements needed explodes. Add a few photons and the job quickly becomes impossible.

There is a smarter option: an entangled measurement, which can identify the state in a single shot instead of thousands. Scientists had built one for the GHZ state more than 25 years ago — but never for the W state.

Cracking the W state

A team at Kyoto University and Hiroshima University took on that missing piece. They noticed that the W state has a neat hidden pattern called cyclic shift symmetry — you can rotate the arrangement of photons in a repeating cycle and the important pattern stays the same.

Using that symmetry, the researchers designed a photonic quantum circuit that performs a mathematical trick called a quantum Fourier transformation. Then they built the device using very stable optical circuits — so steady that they needed no active adjustment while running.

By sending three single photons in carefully chosen polarisation states into the circuit, they showed it could tell different three-photon W states apart.

"More than 25 years after the initial proposal concerning the entangled measurement for GHZ states, we have finally obtained the entangled measurement for the W state as well," said Shigeki Takeuchi, who led the work.

Why it matters

Being able to identify W states opens the door to quantum teleportation — transferring quantum information from one place to another — as well as new ways of sending multi-photon entangled states and a style of computing that runs on careful measurements.

It is a small device with a big promise: teaching quantum computers to read the "team" instead of the players.

Try this at home

  1. Make a secret team. Give three friends a rule: one of you says "beep" when the leader claps. Nobody knows who until the clap. That is a shared, linked team — just like the W state.
  2. Shrink your clue hunt. Time yourself finding one object in a messy room. Now imagine needing twice as long for each new object you add. That exploding effort is why scientists wanted a one-shot method.
  3. Spin a pattern. Draw a triangle with a dot at each corner. Rotate the paper 120° — the shape looks the same. That repeating sameness is the "symmetry" the researchers used.

Fun Fact

Entanglement is so strange that Einstein called it "spooky action at a distance". Today, physicists use it every day — but they still cannot use it to send messages faster than light, because a normal, speed-limited message has to tag along.

For grown-ups

"Quantum entanglement links particles so their shared state cannot be described one-by-one. Two famous three-photon entangled families are the GHZ state and the W state. To use them, physicists must identify which state they have produced. The standard method, quantum tomography, needs exponentially more measurements as photons are added, so it does not scale. An 'entangled measurement' can identify a state in a single shot, and such a measurement existed for GHZ states but never for W states. Researchers at Kyoto University and Hiroshima University (corresponding author Shigeki Takeuchi) exploited the W state's cyclic-shift symmetry to design a photonic quantum circuit performing a quantum Fourier transformation, then built a high-stability optical circuit and demonstrated it with three single photons in selected polarisation states. Announced September 2025 (Kyoto University research news), covered by Physics World (14 Oct 2025). It opens routes to quantum teleportation, multi-photon state transfer and measurement-based quantum computing."
