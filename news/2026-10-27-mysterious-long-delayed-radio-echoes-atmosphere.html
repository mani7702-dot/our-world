"What Are Long-Delayed Radio Echoes? The Century-Old Mystery of Signals That Return Seconds Late"

By Our World Published 27 October 2026 at "22:35 IST" IST
Reading options

"In 1927, a Norwegian radio operator heard a strange echo returning three seconds after the original signal — from the other side of the world. Nearly a century later, scientists still cannot explain these 'long-delayed echoes,' which seem to bounce off invisible structures in Earth's upper atmosphere."

What Causes Radio Signals to Echo Back Seconds After They Should Be Gone?

A mystery that started with a crackling receiver

Picture this: it is October 1927, and Jørgen Hals — a Norwegian civil engineer who loved tinkering with radios in his spare time — is sitting by his receiver near Oslo. He is listening to a shortwave radio station broadcasting from Eindhoven, The Netherlands, thousands of miles away. The music and voices come through clearly.

Then, about three seconds later, something impossible happens: the signal comes back again. Not a reflection bouncing off the ionosphere on a normal schedule — but a ghostly echo that arrives long after it should have faded into silence.

Hals could not explain where it came from. He wrote to physicist Carl Størmer, who teamed up with Balthasar van der Pol to investigate. They managed to reproduce the strange effect in their lab — but neither could say what caused it. That mystery has lasted nearly a century and still has no final answer.

What are long delayed echoes?

When you shout in a canyon, the echo comes back almost instantly. Radio waves bouncing off Earth's ionosphere normally return in about one-seventh of a second — fast enough that you barely notice.

But long delayed echoes (LDEs) are different. Scientists define them as echoes that return more than 2.7 seconds after the original transmission. Some have been recorded returning as late as 30 seconds later. And here is the weird part: these signals have bounced, in effect, dozens of times around the entire planet in the time it takes you to blink.

Since radio waves travel at nearly the speed of light (about 300,000 kilometers per second), a three-second delay means the signal traveled roughly 900,000 kilometers — more than twice the distance from Earth to the Moon. What kind of invisible pathway could send a radio signal on such a long, looping journey?

What could be bouncing these signals?

Scientists have proposed five main theories to explain long delayed echoes:

  1. Magnetospheric ducts. Our planet is wrapped in a giant magnetic bubble called the magnetosphere. Radio waves might get trapped inside invisible "ducts" of electrically charged particles, hitching a ride along Earth's magnetic field lines until they reach the opposite hemisphere and bounce back. This could explain shorter delays of a few hundred milliseconds — but not the longer ones.
  1. Signals circling Earth. A radio wave could theoretically travel around the entire planet seven times in a single second. Some researchers have recorded echoes with delays up to nine seconds — that is roughly 65 trips around Earth. But what would keep the signal on such a perfectly guided path?
  1. Plasma-wave mode conversion. As radio waves travel through the ionosphere (the electrically charged layer of our upper atmosphere), they might briefly convert into plasma waves — a different kind of wave that moves much more slowly. If these slow waves later convert back into radio waves and bounce home, that would explain the long delays. Experiments have recorded echoes with delays up to 40 seconds using this mechanism.
  1. Solar plasma clouds. Streams of charged particles from the Sun could form invisible clouds in space that act like floating mirrors, catching our radio signals and sending them back on a delayed schedule.
  1. Iono-spheric ducting. Signals might get caught between two ionized layers in the atmosphere and guided around the world like a cosmic fiber-optic cable, slipping out of gaps in the layers only when it is ready to return.

University of Oslo researcher Sverre Holm considers the magnetospheric duct theory the "best understood" of the bunch. But he also points out its biggest weakness: the historical echoes from 1927 were detected at about 9.55 MHz, well above the 1-to-4 MHz range where ducting works best — and their delays were 3 to 30 seconds long, far too slow to be explained by magnetism alone.

Why does it matter today?

Even in our age of internet and smartphones, long delayed echoes remind us that Earth's invisible atmospheric and magnetic environment is far stranger than we realize. Every day, our planet's ionosphere acts as a mirror for radio waves — bouncing, bending, and sometimes trapping them in ways we are still learning to understand.

For scientists studying radio astronomy and space weather, understanding these echoes helps improve communication with satellites and spacecraft. And for everyone else, it is a humbling reminder that just beyond our everyday senses — carried on invisible waves through the sky above us — the universe is still full of quiet, unsolved mysteries.

Try this at home

You can hear your own delayed echo using just your voice and a wall:

  1. Stand about six feet away from a large, hard wall or building.
  2. Clap once or snap your fingers sharply.
  3. Listen carefully — you should hear a faint second sound, the echo, returning to you.

Your echo comes back almost instantly (sound travels about one foot per millisecond). But a radio echo that takes three whole seconds to return would mean the signal traveled halfway to the Moon and back. To imagine that journey, picture whispering into a pipe so long it wraps twice around the entire Earth — and someone, or something, is waiting at the other end to whisper back.

Fun Fact

The 1927 discovery by Jørgen Hals happened just a few years before humanity even knew radio waves could bounce off the ionosphere at all. Today, radio astronomers study similar mysterious signals from deep space — like the puzzling Fast Radio Bursts — using the same basic phenomenon of waves traveling through Earth's atmospheric layers. The century-old mystery of echoes from our own sky may one day help us understand whispers from the edge of the universe.

For grown-ups

"Long delayed echoes (LDEs) are radio echoes returning more than 2.7 seconds after transmission. First observed in 1927 by Norwegian amateur radio operator Jørgen Hals, who heard a delayed echo ~3 seconds after signals from a Dutch station in Eindhoven. Five main hypotheses are studied: (1) magnetospherically ducted echoes — radio waves trapped in ionized regions and guided along magnetic field lines to the opposite hemisphere; (2) signals traveling many times around Earth; (3) mode conversion — radio energy coupling into slower plasma-wave modes in the ionosphere; (4) solar plasma clouds; (5) ionospheric ducting between layers. University of Oslo researcher Sverre Holm identifies MDE as best-understood but notes it cannot explain historical echoes at 9.55 MHz with 3–30 second delays, since MDE typically produces only a few hundred milliseconds. No single theory currently explains all observed LDE characteristics."
