"Could Satellite Bonds Prevent a Space Junk Disaster?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at "06:01" IST
Reading options

"A new idea proposes making satellite companies put down a $200,000 refundable bond before launch. If they bring their old satellites back safely, they get their money back. If they leave space junk behind, they lose it — and the money goes toward cleaning up orbit!"

Could Satellite Bonds Save Our Orbit from a Junkyard Future?

What happens when space becomes a junkyard?

Imagine if, every time you flew on an airplane, hundreds of tiny metal scraps were left floating behind — pieces so small you can't see them, but so numerous that soon any new plane would face a storm of invisible collisions. That is exactly what is happening above our heads right now.

Every year, thousands of satellites are launched into low Earth orbit — the crowded "near-Earth highway" where the International Space Station and most weather, phone, and internet satellites live. But when these satellites run out of fuel or finish their jobs, most of them never come back down. They just stay up there, becoming space junk — tiny shards of metal, old rocket bodies, and broken pieces, all zooming around Earth at 17,500 miles per hour.

Scientists have a name for the dangerous idea that this junk could spiral out of control: the Kessler syndrome (also called the "Kessler effect"). It was first dreamed up in 1978 by NASA scientist Donald Kessler, who realized that if there get too many pieces of junk in orbit, they will bump into each other, creating even more shards — a chain reaction that could turn low Earth orbit into an impenetrable wall of flying metal.

A clever fix: make companies "pay to play"

Here is the clever part: a team of researchers led by Daniel Kaffine at the University of Colorado Boulder has proposed a simple rule. Before any satellite is allowed to launch, the company must put down a refundable bond of $200,000.

  • If the company brings its satellite back safely — either by guiding it to burn up in Earth's atmosphere or by parking it in a "spacecraft graveyard" orbit — they get their money back, plus interest.
  • If they just abandon it? They lose the $200,000, and that money goes into a shared fund that can pay for future junk-cleanup missions.

The researchers published their idea in the scientific journal Nature Communications. What makes it smart is that it works without needing every country in the world to agree first — any single nation could start requiring this bond for satellites registered in its name.

Why is the problem so big?

Right now, international space rules ask satellite companies to remove their old spacecraft from orbit within 25 years after their mission ends. The United States goes a step further, asking for 5 years. But here is the catch: 60% of satellites stay in place even after they are dead, because there are no real consequences for failing to deorbit.

With companies like SpaceX, Blue Origin, and Amazon planning to launch hundreds of thousands of new satellites in the coming years, the space around Earth could get dangerously crowded. A single collision creates thousands of new shards — each one capable of smashing into another satellite, which creates even more debris in a runaway chain reaction.

Will it work?

The researchers are honest that some big space nations (like the United States) have historically been reluctant to adopt new space rules. But by making the bond a national-level requirement rather than waiting for international agreement, the plan could get started faster. Any country can say: "No bond, no launch."

And the payoff could be huge — the researchers say the system could raise billions of dollars for cleaning up the space junk that is already up there, turning a shared problem into a shared solution.

Try this at home

Build a Kessler cascade in a jar! Fill a clear plastic bottle with glitter (representing working satellites), then add a few pieces of metallic confetti (representing space junk). Shake the bottle gently — this is orbit today. Now shake it hard — when the junk starts bumping into each other, you will see a sudden burst of chaos. That is the Kessler syndrome in action! Try adding a "net" (small strainer) over the bottle opening to catch pieces — that is like the deorbit plan, catching the junk before it all goes wild.

Fun Fact

The idea of space being treated like a shared "commons" is similar to how national parks or oceans are protected. In fact, one researcher compared low Earth orbit to "lots of other resources that we have trashed" — and said we might finally be learning from that mistake before it is too late.

The scheme requires satellite operators to post a $200,000 refundable deposit before launch, returned with interest upon verified deorbit, forfeited to a debris-removal fund if the satellite is abandoned.
