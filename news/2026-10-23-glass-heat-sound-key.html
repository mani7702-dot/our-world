What Makes Glass a Good Conductor of Heat? The Surprising Role of Sound

By Our World Published 23 October 2026 at 20:46 IST
Reading options

Glass may seem like an insulator, but scientists have discovered that the speed of sound traveling through silicate glass is the dominant factor controlling how well it conducts heat — a finding that could help design better smartphone screens and electronic components.

What Makes Glass a Good Conductor of Heat? The Surprising Role of Sound

Why glass doesn't insulate as well as you think

When you touch a glass of ice water, the outside feels cool even though glass is supposed to be a good insulator. But glass is not a simple on/off switch for heat — it is an amorphous solid, meaning its molecules have no regular crystal structure. Heat still sneaks through it, carried by tiny vibrations called phonons. These phonons are really just packets of sound — the same kind of waves that carry music through air, except they jiggle the glass's atoms instead.

For decades, scientists thought three factors mattered equally: how much heat the glass can store, how fast sound travels through it, and how far vibrations can go before bumping into something. But which one is the boss? A team at Tohoku University set out to settle the debate by testing 13 different kinds of silicate glass — the kind used in windows, phone screens, and electronic chips.

Sound speed turns out to be the boss

The researchers measured every factor they could think of: thermal conductivity, sound velocity, how much heat the glass can store, and how far phonons travel before scattering. Their surprising result, published in Applied Physics Letters (2026), was clear: the speed of sound through the glass is the dominant factor controlling its thermal conductivity.

Think of it like a hallway race. If sound waves sprint faster down the hallway, they carry heat energy with them more efficiently. But the team also found a trade-off: when one factor went up, another tended to go down, so their product stayed about the same. Only sound velocity broke the pattern — it alone determined how well heat moved through the glass.

Designing better materials for tomorrow's gadgets

Smartphones, tablets, and computer chips all need glass parts that handle heat differently. Some areas need to shed heat fast to avoid overheating; others need to keep heat contained. Knowing that sound velocity is the key lets engineers tune glass recipes from the start, mixing in different elements to change how fast sound travels through the material.

Instead of trial-and-error, manufacturers could design glass with the exact thermal properties they need — whether for a phone screen that stays cool or a sensor that traps every bit of warmth.

Try this at home

Fill two identical cups with hot water. Wrap one's rim with a thick rubber band (a poor heat conductor) and leave one bare. Touch each rim with the same finger at the same time. You will feel the difference heat makes — and now you know that invisible sound waves are partly responsible!

Fun Fact

The word "glass" comes from an old Germanic word meaning "a thin sheet" — but the oldest human-made glass, found in ancient Mesopotamia, dates back nearly 4,000 years. The first glass windows were so thick at the bottom because glass is not actually a liquid that flows over centuries (a common myth!) — it is simply an amorphous solid that was hand-blown unevenly.

For the grown-ups

A research team led by Sohei Sukenaga at Tohoku University investigated 13 silicate glass compositions to identify the dominant factor governing thermal conductivity. They measured thermal conductivity, sound velocity, volumetric specific heat, and phonon mean free path at room temperature. The results, published in Applied Physics Letters (2026), reveal that thermal conductivity is proportional to sound velocity, identifying it as the dominant factor. A trade-off exists between volumetric specific heat and phonon mean free path — as one increases, the other tends to decrease, keeping their product approximately constant. Only sound velocity breaks this pattern, making it the key design parameter. The findings provide a guideline for designing glass materials with specific thermal properties for next-generation electronic devices, allowing engineers to predict thermal conductivity by simply measuring sound velocity rather than conducting complex heat-flow experiments.
