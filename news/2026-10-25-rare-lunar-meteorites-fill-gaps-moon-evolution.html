"How do rare Moon rocks that fall from space fill gaps in our story of the Moon?"

By Our World Published 25 October 2026 at 15:02 IST
Reading options

"Some of the best Moon rocks never came from a spacecraft — they are meteorites that were blasted off our nearest neighbor eons ago and rained down on Earth, bringing us pieces of the Moon we never visited."

How do rare Moon rocks that fall from space fill gaps in our story of the Moon?

Moon Rocks That Travel to Earth

The Moon looks peaceful hanging in our sky, but billions of years ago its surface was pockmarked by violent impacts. When a giant space rock slammed into the Moon, it blasted chunks of lunar rock into space — and some of those pieces eventually fell to Earth as meteorites.

These special space rocks are called lunar meteorites. They are literally pieces of the Moon's surface that traveled through space for millions of years before landing in places like Antarctica, the deserts of Africa, and the sands of Australia. Scientists have found only about 835 of them so far — and that is a tiny number compared to all the meteorites that fall to Earth. But to lunar scientists, they are pure gold.

Why They Matter More Than Apollo Rocks

The American Apollo missions between 1969 and 1972 brought back about 382 kilograms (842 pounds) of Moon rocks. China's Chang'e 5 and Chang'e 6 missions recently returned more precious samples. But here is the catch: every Apollo landing site is in one small corner of the Moon — a region scientists now know is geochemically unusual. It is a bit like if every book in a library came from only one shelf.

Lunar meteorites come from almost random places across the Moon — including perhaps the mysterious far side, which no human has ever visited. That means they could hold clues about how the Moon's oldest crust formed, and whether a giant global ocean of molten rock once covered the entire Moon (a theory called the Lunar Magma Ocean).

A Mystery Waiting to Be Solved

Here is what still puzzles scientists: each lunar meteorite is a tiny piece of the Moon, but we do not know exactly where it came from. It is like finding a page from a library book with no title or number — fascinating, but we cannot place it. By studying the chemistry and age of these stones, researchers hope to crack the code of the Moon's earliest days.

Finding a meteorite that holds an unusual, super-old piece of the Moon's mantle would be extraordinary. Such a discovery could tell us secrets about our nearest neighbor that even the Apollo astronauts never brought home.

Try this at home

On a clear night, find the Moon through a small telescope or even with your naked eye (ask an adult first). Look for the dark patches called maria (Latin for "seas") and the bright, rough highlands. Imagine that every once in a while, a meteorite from somewhere in the solar system might be heading toward the Moon — and one day, a piece of our Moon might end up crossing Earth's path, too.

Fun Fact

The very first lunar meteorites were discovered in Antarctica in 1979 — nearly 15 years after the last Apollo mission! They were named Yamato-791197 and Yamato-793167, after the Japanese ice field where the Japanese meteorite-hunting program found them.

for-grownups: Lunar meteorites (≈835 verified, 0.7% of all meteorites) are fragments of the Moon's crust blasted into space by impact events and later found on Earth. They come from random lunar locations — not the geochemically atypical Procellarum KREEP Terrane sampled by all six Apollo landings — so they may reveal how the Moon's oldest crust and mantle formed and test the Lunar Magma Ocean hypothesis.

For grown-ups

"Lunar meteorites are fragments of the Moon's crust that were ejected by impact events and later fell to Earth. Unlike Apollo (6 missions, 1969-1972) and Chang'e 5/6 sample-return missions — which visited the geochemically atypical Procellarum KREEP Terrane on the lunar near side — lunar meteorites originate from random locations across the Moon, including possibly the far side. Only ~835 lunar meteorites have been verified (0.7% of all meteorites), but they offer a more representative sampling of the Moon's surface. They are critical to testing the Lunar Magma Ocean (LMO) hypothesis — the idea that the early Moon was once covered by a global ocean of molten rock — because they may contain pieces of the ancient lunar crust and mantle that the Apollo samples, all from one region, cannot reveal. The first lunar meteorites (Yamato-791197, Yamato-793169) were identified in Antarctica in 1979.
