How can two teeth reveal a rodent as heavy as a big dog?

By Our World Published 13 November 2026 at 01:30 IST
Reading options

Scientists in Thailand found just a molar and a bit of a front tooth - and worked out the animal was a rodent weighing about 30 kilograms. Here is how you weigh a fossil.

Imagine finding two teeth in a pile of ancient mud. No bones, no skull, no skeleton. Just a back tooth and a piece of a front tooth.

From that, a team of scientists worked out they had found a new species of rodent that weighed about 30 kilograms (66 pounds) - roughly as heavy as a big dog. It lived in what is now southern Thailand around 34 million years ago, long after the dinosaurs had gone.

They named it Yakamys sirindhornae. The second part of the name honours Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn of Thailand, who has long supported science and fossil research there.

Where the teeth were hiding

The two fossils were collected back in 2004, in a clay layer inside a coal seam at the Bang Mark pit of the Krabi lignite mine, in Krabi Province. Lignite is a soft, brown kind of coal - not the place you would expect to meet a giant.

They sat in a museum collection until this international team of palaeontologists - from Thailand, France and Canada, including McGill University - studied them properly. The results were published in the journal Scientific Reports.

The back tooth is about 11.6 millimetres long and 11.3 millimetres wide. That sounds small, but the biggest teeth of its closest known Asian relatives were less than half that size.

How do you weigh an animal from one tooth?

You cannot put a fossil on a scale. So the team used seven different maths formulas that link tooth size to body weight in living rodents, and ran all of them.

The answers spread quite widely, from about 21 kilograms up to about 47 kilograms. The average came out at around 31.6 kilograms, so the scientists report the animal as "approximately 30 kilograms" - and they say clearly that this is a ballpark number, because small fossils can never give an exact weight.

Thirty kilograms is about twice as heavy as other rodents known from the same ancient time period, such as a species called Hulgana. For a size picture: a big capybara, the largest rodent alive today, can reach around 60 kilograms - so this fossil rodent was roughly half a capybara, and many times heavier than a pet rat.

Why two teeth are still a big deal

There is a lot we still do not know. The animal's face, fur, diet and habits are all a mystery until more of it is found. What the teeth do show is the shape of the chewing surface and the microscopic structure of the front tooth's enamel, and those details place it in the Ischyromyidae - one of the earliest big families of rodents, now extinct.

The surprise is the timing and the place. Giant rodents were thought to have turned up later, and mostly in other parts of the world. Yakamys shows that some rodents were already enormous in Southeast Asia during the late Eocene, the stretch of prehistory that followed the age of dinosaurs.

Try this at home

  • Measure a tooth. Find a ruler and look at the mark for 11 millimetres. Now look at your own back tooth in a mirror. Fossil teeth are not always as huge as the animal sounds.
  • Draw it to scale. Draw a dog, a cat, a pet rat and a 30-kilogram rodent side by side, all to the same scale. Which one would you not want to meet in your kitchen?
  • Play detective. If you only had one tooth from an unknown animal, what could you guess about it? Size, food, age? What could you never guess? That is exactly the problem palaeontologists face.
  • Look at your own front teeth. Rodent front teeth keep growing all their lives. Feel the edge of yours - and remember you only get two sets.

For grown-ups: Yakamys sirindhornae is described from a lower second molar and a lower incisor fragment from the Bang Mark locality of the Krabi Basin, southern Thailand, dated to the late Eocene (approximately 35.1-33.7 million years ago) on the basis of the mammal assemblage and reversed magnetic polarity (Chron 13r). Body mass was estimated with seven published allometric regressions from dental dimensions, giving a range of 21.4-47.3 kg, mean 31.6 kg, median 28.6 kg, summarised by the authors as approximately 30 kg; the paper is explicit that dental allometry carries substantial uncertainty. Referral to Ischyromyidae rests on molar morphology plus incisor enamel microstructure. With only two teeth, diet, locomotion and ecology remain unknown, and the value of the find is biogeographic: it is the earliest well-documented evidence of a very large ischyromyid in Southeast Asia, extending the known range of early rodent gigantism in both time and space. For context, the largest living rodent, the capybara, typically reaches 35-66 kg.
