"How did Earth's ice ages begin? A hidden ocean chain reaction may hold the answer"

By Our World Published 30 October 2026 at 09:15 IST
Reading options

"For decades, scientists have wondered how growing ice sheets in the Northern Hemisphere could trigger climate changes thousands of miles away around Antarctica. New computer simulations suggest the answer is an invisible ocean highway: cold water from the north sinks and travels south, eventually changing how the Southern Ocean absorbs carbon dioxide — a key step that helped push Earth into ice ages."

How did Earth's ice ages begin? A hidden ocean chain reaction may hold the answer

The mystery of two poles, one problem

For a long time, scientists were puzzled by a big question: how could giant ice sheets growing in the Northern Hemisphere cause the climate around Antarctica — thousands of miles away — to change at the same time? After all, ice ages are not just about one cold spot. They involve a chain reaction that links the whole planet. And the key piece of that chain turns out to be the ocean.

Step 1: Ice sheets cool the northern seas

When Earth's orbit changes over thousands of years, less sunlight reaches the northern lands. Snow piles up and turns into thick ice sheets. As these massive walls of ice grow, they chill the air above them — and the seawater right next to them in the North Atlantic.

Cold water is heavier than warm water. So this chilled, dense water sinks down into the deep ocean, starting a slow underwater river that flows south.

Step 2: The deep-ocean conveyor belt

This sinking water doesn't just sit there. It joins a global system called the thermohaline circulation — a network of deep, slow-moving currents that act like a giant conveyor belt around the planet. Driven by differences in temperature and salt, these currents carry cold northern water all the way down into the deep sea and then southward, past Europe, Africa, and eventually into the Southern Ocean that circles Antarctica.

Step 3: The Southern Ocean absorbs more carbon dioxide

When the extra-cold northern water reaches the Southern Ocean, something important happens: it changes how the ocean mixes. Normally, strong winds bring deep, carbon-rich water to the surface around Antarctica, where carbon dioxide (CO₂) can escape back into the air. But the influx of northern water alters this pattern, so more CO₂ stays locked away under the sea.

CO₂ is a greenhouse gas — the more of it in the air, the warmer the planet gets. Taking it out of the atmosphere means Earth cools down a little more.

Step 4: The feedback loop that freezes the world

Here is where it becomes a loop:

  1. Colder northern waters flow south.
  2. The Southern Ocean absorbs more CO₂.
  3. Less CO₂ in the air means cooler global temperatures.
  4. Cooler temperatures grow the ice sheets even bigger.
  5. Bigger ice sheets cool the northern oceans even more.

Computer simulations by Dr. Madison Shankle and colleagues showed that cooling just the far-northern regions produced 74% as much CO₂ drawdown as cooling the entire planet — proving that the northern ice sheets had a surprisingly strong effect on the faraway Southern Ocean.

This feedback loop is what scientists think helped push Earth into full blown ice ages, with vast glaciers stretching across continents.

Try this at home

You can see a mini version of ocean circulation in your kitchen! Fill a clear cup with water, add a few drops of blue food coloring, and then gently drop in a small cube of ice. Watch how the cold meltwater (which is denser) sinks and swirls through the warmer water below, carrying color with it. That swirling flow is a tiny model of the deep currents that helped shape our planet's deepest freezes!

Fun Fact

The most recent full ice age — called the Last Glacial Maximum — happened about 26,000 to 20,000 years ago. At its peak, enormous ice sheets miles thick covered much of North America, northern Europe, and Asia. Sea levels were about 120 meters (400 feet) lower than today, because so much water was locked up in those giant glaciers.

For grown-ups

"A study led by Dr. Madison Shankle from the University of St Andrews, published in the Journal of Climate, used computer simulations to show how expanding northern ice sheets could trigger a chain reaction that helped Earth plunge into ice ages. The key mechanism is ocean circulation: as ice sheets grew in the north, they cooled nearby North Atlantic waters. Those dense, cold waters then sank and flowed southward as part of the global ocean conveyor belt, eventually reaching the Southern Ocean around Antarctica. Once there, the changed circulation allowed the Southern Ocean to absorb more carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Since CO2 is a greenhouse gas, less of it in the air meant cooler global temperatures — which in turn grew the ice sheets even more, creating a feedback loop. The simulations showed that cooling just the far-northern latitudes produced 74% as much CO2 drawdown as cooling the entire planet, proving that Northern Hemisphere ice sheets had a powerful influence on the Southern Ocean despite being thousands of miles apart. The Last Glacial Maximum, roughly 26,000 to 20,000 years ago, represents the most recent time this process ran its full course, with massive ice sheets covering North America, Europe, and Asia. By identifying this ocean-linked mechanism, researchers hope to better understand Earth's natural carbon cycle — knowledge that is especially urgent today as human-caused CO2 emissions reverse the process, warming the planet instead of cooling it."
