"Why did Blue Planet III keep the cloudy, 'imperfect' shots? It's all real!"

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 11:39 IST
Reading options

"The BBC's new ocean documentary leaves in cloudy water and floating debris on purpose — to prove every animal is real and no AI fakery snuck in."

Why did Blue Planet III keep the cloudy, “imperfect” shots? It’s all real!

1. Blurry water, real animals

When the cameras of Blue Planet III sink beneath the waves, something might look odd to your eyes. The water is sometimes cloudy. There might be floating bits of seaweed, bubbles, or plankton drifting past. In the old days, the film crews would have cut those shots entirely — too messy, too imperfect.

But not anymore. This time, the BBC is keeping the cloudy, “messy” footage on purpose. Why? Because it proves every creature swimming across your screen is real — no computer tricks, no AI-made animals hiding in the deep.

2. The AI worry that changed everything

“In the past, we might have framed out a boat or a buoy,” said the series’ producers. “The water conditions were not really good enough.” But now, as AI makes it easy to fake almost anything on a screen, the team decided to lean into the opposite: let everything in, exactly as it happened.

They call it being “organic.” The more real, the more convincing the story. And the story here is that the ocean is full of creatures so strange and wonderful, they don’t need any help from a computer.

3. Cameras that think like octopuses

To catch these creatures, the film crews invented brand-new tools. There is the “stonefish” — a tiny underwater CCTV camera that can sneak into the smallest caves and crevices. There is a mini-dome camera that rides along with an octopus, seeing the world from eight arms’ worth of perspective. There is even a “deep-sea selfie stick” and laser cameras that paint pictures of life in the pitch-black deep ocean.

With these tools, the team filmed never-before-seen behaviour: dolphins who stuff sea sponges into holes in coral to trap fish (like using a kitchen strainer underwater), 200,000 crabs that line up in perfect military formation to feast, and sea urchins that balance whole shells on their heads like tiny sun hats.

Fun Fact

The ocean covers more than 70% of our planet — but humans have explored less than 5% of it in person. Every episode of Blue Planet III shows places that almost no human has ever seen.

Try this at home

Make a “cloudy ocean” experiment. Fill a clear glass or jar with water, then add a few drops of milk and some glitter or tiny pieces of coloured paper. Shine a flashlight through the side — the light will scatter, turning the water cloudy white (just like real ocean water). Now drop in a small toy sea creature and watch how it looks clearer or blurrier depending on how “cloudy” your ocean is. It’s the same reason Blue Planet III leaves the cloudy shots in — sometimes the murk is what makes the magic visible.

For grown-ups

"The BBC's Blue Planet III — the third in the famous ocean documentary franchise that began in 2001 — premieres on 1 November 2026 on BBC One, BBC iPlayer, and BBC America. It was co-produced by the BBC Natural History Unit alongside ZDF (Germany), France Télévisions, and Skai TV, in partnership with The Open University. David Attenborough, born 8 May 1926 and therefore 100 years old in 2026, returns as narrator. In May 2026 the BBC confirmed his return to narration, coinciding with his 100th birthday. The series has six hour-long episodes, each covering a different underwater habitat: Tropical Seas, Open Ocean, Seasonal Seas, Polar Waters, The Deep, and a final conservation-themed episode called Future Seas. Jack Bootle, the BBC's specialist factual commissioning head, officially stated: 'No gen AI was used in this series. Not a single frame.' Executive producer Elizabeth White explained that the rise of AI 'makes us want to be even more authentic' — which is why shots that would have previously been cut for murky water, plankton particles, or stray buoys are now left in. Over four years of filming across five oceans, the team developed new cameras including the world's first underwater CCTV camera (nicknamed 'stonefish'), a mini-dome camera that followed an octopus, a 'deep-sea selfie stick', and used lasers to film creatures in the dark. Novel animal behaviours filmed include dolphins using sea sponges to block holes in coral to trap fish; 200,000 crabs forming marching lines to feed; and sea urchins balancing shells on their heads like sun hats. The series also addresses climate threats — showing coral reef damage and plastic pollution — alongside the ocean's own resilience. Blue Planet III was confirmed by BBC in 2022, with filming spanning four years. Source: Guardian (primary article), Wikipedia (Blue Planet III article, David Attenborough biography)."
