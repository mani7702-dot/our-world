How Did 53 Orangutans Survive Borneo's Fires and Find a New Home?

Fires sweep through Borneo's forests

Forest and peatland wildfires have been burning across the island of Borneo, threatening the air quality for both people and wildlife. These blazes are especially dangerous for orangutans — gentle great apes that call Borneo's rainforests home. When fires torch the forest canopy, the orangutans lose their food (mostly fruits and leaves) and their shelter, leaving them stranded and vulnerable.

A dedicated rescue team springs into action

An 18-year-old nonprofit called YIARI has been racing against time. Since August, their team has responded to urgent calls, rescuing 53 orangutans from fire-affected areas. Some were found stranded, others dehydrated and thin. The team didn't just save them — they also rewilded many into protected forest preserves across Indonesian Borneo, including Gunung Palung National Park.

One photo shows Dr. Karmele Llano Sanchez, YIARI's CEO, gently comforting a young female orangutan named Irene. She was so thin and stressed that she may have gone days without food. Thanks to round-the-clock care from veterinarians and volunteers, baby Irene is now on the road to recovery.

Why orangutans need our help

Orangutans are found only in the rainforests of Borneo and Sumatra. Both the Bornean and Sumatran orangutans are listed as Critically Endangered — the highest risk level before extinction. The biggest threats are habitat loss from palm oil farming, logging, and forest fires.

These clever apes are excellent climbers and spend most of their lives in trees. They can swing from branch to branch using their long arms, and they build a new sleeping nest in the treetops every night from leaves and branches. Baby orangutans stay close to their mothers for up to 8 years, learning which fruits are safe to eat and how to navigate the forest.

What can we learn?

When wildfires hit the forest, every orangutan counts. The fact that 53 were saved and many rewilded shows that dedicated people can make a real difference. But YIARI's rescue center is now full — they need support to keep helping these amazing animals.

Try This at Home

Make a paper orangutan and learn about their habitat! Cut out an orangutan shape from red-brown craft paper. Add big eyes, a round nose, and long arms. Glue it to a branch-shaped stick. Now imagine: if your bedroom was a forest and you had to sleep in a tree every night, what would your perfect nest look like?

Fun Fact

Orangutans share about 97% of their DNA with humans — making them our closest living relatives along with chimpanzees, gorillas, and bonobos. They can recognize themselves in mirrors and remember the locations of hundreds of fruit trees for months!

for-grownups: The YIARI Foundation (Yayasan IAR Indonesia) has been rescuing and rehabilitating orangutans since 2008. Their work focuses on Borneo, where forest and peatland fires — worsened by seasonal drought and land-clearing practices — displace and endanger local wildlife. Wikipedia confirms orangutans are endemic to Borneo and Sumatra, with all three species (Bornean, Sumatran, Tapanuli) listed Critically Endangered by the IUCN Red List due to habitat destruction, deforestation for palm oil, and fires. Baby orangutans typically stay with their mothers for 6–8 years, one of the longest dependency periods among great apes. The rewilding of rescued orangutans into protected areas like Gunung Palung National Park is a key conservation strategy to maintain genetic diversity.
