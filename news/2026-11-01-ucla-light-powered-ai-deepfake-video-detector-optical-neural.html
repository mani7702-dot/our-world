Can Light Travel Through a Chip to Spot Fake Videos?

Every day, AI tools can now make a video of almost anyone saying almost anything. The fake clips can look real, which makes it hard to tell what is true and what is made up. But what if the answer is not more software, but a clever use of light itself?

Why deepfakes are so tricky to catch

Most deepfake detectors are run on digital computers. They look at millions of tiny numbers (pixels) in a video, one frame at a time. The trouble is that the best AI fakes are improving faster than the detectors, and attackers can nudge a fake in small ways that fool the software. On top of that, checking video this way is slow and uses a lot of energy — like reading every book in a library one word at a time.

A detector that thinks with light

A team at UCLA, led by Professor Aydogan Ozcan, tried something different. Instead of doing all the work inside a computer's processor, part of the detection happens in real light, guided by a tiny chip.

Here is how it works, step by step:

  1. A small digital program first grabs the important features from each video — its shapes,

colours, and how it moves.

  1. Those features are turned into a phase pattern and shown on a programmable light

modulator (a screen for light waves).

  1. As light passes through that pattern, it changes in a way that is unique to real or

fake video.

  1. At the other end, simple optical detectors read the light and produce a **yes-or-no

score**: real, or probably a deepfake.

Because many videos can be analysed during a single pass of light, the chip can check 15 videos at the same time — like a security guard who can watch 15 doors at once instead of only one.

Numbers that matter

In tests using 15 celebrity deepfake videos (a standard test set called Celeb-DF), the light-powered detector got 97.8% accuracy. That is already strong, but the most important number is its sensitivity: 99.86% — meaning almost every fake video was caught, and only about 1 in 700 fakes slipped through unnoticed. When the team added two extra diffractive (light-bending) layers to the chip, its accuracy on the hardest fakes climbed to 95.8%.

Best of all, it still worked when the researchers tested videos made with the latest AI tools, including Google's Gemini. The physics of light is hard to trick, so the system is naturally resistant to the sneaky "adversarial" edits that often fool digital-only detectors.

What it could mean

Right now, websites and phone apps struggle to check billions of videos by hand. A light-speed first pass that flags likely fakes in real time could help platforms catch problems before anyone sees them. It could also help people feel more confident about videos they watch — a useful superpower in an age where a clip can look convincing even when it is not real.

Try this at home

You will need an adult. Shine a phone flashlight through a crumpled, greasy transparency sheet (or a clean CD case) onto a wall. Move and tilt the sheet and watch how the light pattern changes. That is a tiny glimpse of what the UCLA chip does at a microscopic scale — using shapes to control light and read a result. Real deepfakes look the same as real videos, but this kind of physical check looks at differences a hidden AI might not be able to hide.

Fun fact: The name "deepfake" was born in 2017, when an Reddit user (going by the name "deepfakes") started swapping celebrity faces onto videos. Today the technology runs on much bigger and more powerful AI models — which is exactly why physical, light-based detectors are racing to keep up.

For grown-ups

"Researchers at UCLA (University of California, Los Angeles), led by Professor Aydogan Ozcan, built a hybrid digital-optical processor that detects deepfake videos using light itself. A lightweight digital encoder first pulls compact features from each video frame, then transforms them into a phase pattern displayed on a programmable spatial light modulator. As light passes through, paired optical detectors directly score whether each video is real or manipulated. In experiments the system scanned 15 deepfake videos at once (from the Celeb-DF benchmark) and reached 97.8% detection accuracy, with 99.86% sensitivity — meaning almost no fakes slipped past unnoticed. Adding diffractive optical layers lifted accuracy to 95.8% on harder to detect forgeries, and the chip still worked on AI content made with newer tools such as Google's Gemini. Because the detection happens through the physics of light rather than only through digital math, the approach is fast, low energy, and naturally resistant to adversarial attacks that try to confuse detectors. The study was published in the journal eLight (DOI 10.1186/s43593-026-00143-y). The deepfake problem itself: deepfakes are AI-edited images and videos that swap faces or synthesize new scenes, and they are a form of synthetic media."
