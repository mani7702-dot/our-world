"How can invisible magnetic whirlpools help computers think like brains?"

By Our World Published 26 October 2026 at 21:14 IST IST
Reading options

"Inside every computer chip, tiny tornadoes of magnetism called skyrmions swirl by the billion. Scientists are learning to watch these invisible whirlpools melt and reform — and one day they might let computers save energy the way our brains do."

How can invisible magnetic whirlpools help computers think like brains?

The whirlpools inside your gadgets

Deep inside every computer chip, beneath the glare of electric current, there are storms no eye can see. Not made of wind or water — these tiny tornadoes are made of magnetism. They are called skyrmions, and they are among the tiniest whirlpools in the universe.

Imagine a dot of magnetic north at the center of a swirl, surrounded by atoms whose magnetic arrows spin around it like water circling a drain. That is a skyrmeon — a stable knot of magnetism so small that billions could fit on the head of a pin. Scientists have been hunting them for years, but now, for the first time, a research team led by Asle Sudbø at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology has filmed them melting in real time — like watching ice turn to water, but with invisible magnetic whirlpools instead of water molecules.

When magnetic lattices melt in slow motion

Left to themselves, skyrmions do not float around alone. Under the right conditions, they line up into tidy rows — a repeating pattern called a lattice, like atoms in a crystal but made of magnetism instead of atoms.

Sudbø's team, working with researchers at Johannes Gutenberg University in Mainz, Germany, did something no one had done before: they watched this lattice melt while they gently poked at each individual skyrmion with magnetic fields. It is like filming a beehive while carefully moving each bee one by one. They could change the skyrmion's shape, its size, even make it stretch and shrink — all captured in real time.

"This is the first time researchers have managed to film how a magnetic skyrmeon lattice melts in real time, while simultaneously being able to manipulate each individual skyrmeon directly," explained Sudbø. "Both their shape and size can be changed on the fly."

Watching the melt lets scientists see how these tiny vortices behave when their perfect order breaks apart — the same moment when chaos and creativity meet in nature.

Computers that think like brains

Why does this matter? Because skyrmions might help computers work more like the human brain.

Today's computers use transistors — tiny switches that turn electricity on and off. It takes a lot of space and a lot of energy to make them smarter. But skyrmions use a different trick called spintronics, where the twist of an electron (not just its charge) carries information. It is like the difference between pushing a ball down a hill versus spinning a top — both move things, but one uses a lot less fuel.

"The skyrmions could help develop computers that resemble biological brains," said Sudbø. These brain-like computers would process information with magnetism instead of pure electricity, which means they could do complex thinking while sipping energy instead of gulping it down like current chips.

Think of a hummingbird's brain — smaller than a blueberry, yet able to hover, dart, and remember dozens of flowers. A skyrmeon-powered computer chip could one day match that: powerful, tiny, and frugal with power.

Try this at home

Grab a bar magnet and a compass. Slowly move the magnet around the compass needle. Watch how the needle wobbles, spins, and snaps back — that is a tiny magnetic field interacting with another. Now imagine millions of these invisible dances happening at once inside a sliver of metal. That is the world of skyrmions. You can also try a magnetic sand art kit (available at science toy stores) — pour the metal filings between two plastic sheets and a magnet underneath, and you will see whirlpool patterns form and flow, just like skyrmions in a crystal.

Fun Fact

The name "skyrmeon" comes from physicist Tony Skyrme, who in 1961 proposed a theoretical model of protons and neutrons. Decades later, scientists discovered that real, physical skyrmions could be created in labs — turning a piece of abstract math into something you can see swirl in a magnetic material. They are one of the rare particles predicted by equations that turned out to be real.

For grown-ups

"Magnetic skyrmions are tiny, vortex-like magnetic structures studied in spintronics — a field that uses electron spin instead of just electric charge to store and process data. Published in Nature Nanotechnology, a team led by Asle Sudbø at NTNU (Norway) and researchers at Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz (Germany) achieved the first real-time filming of a skyrmion lattice melting while simultaneously manipulating individual vortices, changing their shape and size on the fly. Unlike conventional transistors that need ever more space and electricity as chip complexity grows, skyrmion-based 'racetrack' memory could pack data into swirling magnetic textures that are topologically protected — meaning they resist disruption and stay stable even at the nanoscale. This makes them promising candidates for brain-like neuromorphic computing and low-power quantum devices, though practical applications remain years away."
