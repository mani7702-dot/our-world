"How Does Hubble Train Its Younger Sister Roman to Map Dark Matter?"

By Our World Published 28 October 2026 at "06:32 IST" IST
Reading options

1. Why Does the Universe Need a Cosmic Magnifying Glass?

Imagine holding a regular magnifying glass over a page of tiny letters — the glass bends the light so the letters look bigger and easier to read. In deep space, a whole galaxy can act like that magnifying glass, but instead of glass, it uses something even more powerful: gravity itself.

This incredible trick is called gravitational lensing. It happens when a super-heavy object — like a galaxy full of stars and invisible dark matter — floats between Earth and an even more distant galaxy. The heavy object's gravity warps the fabric of spacetime, and that warp bends the light coming from the faraway galaxy. To us, the distant galaxy looks brighter and stretched into curved streaks, sometimes forming perfect rings or graceful arcs.

Albert Einstein predicted this over a century ago, when his general theory of relativity showed that mass and spacetime are tangled together — more mass means more warping. When the alignment between Earth, the foreground galaxy, and the background galaxy is just right, we get some of the most beautiful and useful pictures in astronomy.

2. How Hubble Trains Roman to See the Invisible

Not all the light-bending a galaxy does can be explained by the stars and gas we can actually see. The extra bending comes from something invisible — the mysterious dark matter that holds galaxies together like an invisible skeleton.

By measuring how stretched and smeared the background galaxy's light appears, astronomers can work out exactly how much total mass the foreground galaxy carries, and how much of that is dark matter. But here's where it gets interesting: each galaxy's gravity bends light in its own slightly messy way, creating a unique pattern called the point-spread function — the telescope's "fingerprint" of how it blurs a pinpoint of light.

Hubble can study one gravitational lens at a time with stunning clarity, working out that fingerprint for each one. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, arriving soon at the Sun–Earth L2 point, will survey thousands of these lenses at once. But Roman's images will be 200 times larger than Hubble's and spread across multiple detectors, making it harder to untangle the PSF on its own. So Hubble draws the "answer key" — detailed models that Roman will use to read its much bigger pictures.

3. 160,000 Lenses and a Race Against Time

Roman's High-Latitude Wide Area Survey (HLWAS) is expected to cover about 5,000 square degrees of the sky — roughly 12% of the entire heavens — and should detect 160,000 strong gravitational lenses. But only about 500 of those will be sharp enough for detailed follow-up observations that can reveal the hidden substructure of dark matter.

That's why Hubble's preparatory work matters so much. Astronomers are building computer models using Hubble's sharp, one-at-a-time lensing observations to help decipher Roman's wider-field images. Hubble will also perform follow-up imaging of the most interesting lenses that Roman discovers, like a seasoned detective helping a rookie partner solve cases.

Even though the Roman is more advanced in some ways, Hubble is far from retired. The veteran telescope is gradually losing its stabilizing gyroscopes, and with no repair mission planned, it may not last forever — but until then, it still has important work to do supporting its younger sister.

Try this at home: You can make a mini version of gravitational lensing with a magnifying glass and a bright light. Hold the glass between your eye and a light bulb (never look directly at the sun!) and tilt it slightly — watch how the bulb's reflection stretches and distorts. Every time you see a rainbow in a garden hose spray or a dewdrop on a blade of grass, you are seeing light being bent and redirected — a tiny reminder that the same rules shaping your garden also shape the entire cosmos.

Fun Fact: The term "gravitational lens" was coined because gravity acts like an invisible lens in space — but unlike a glass lens, it has no single focal point. It focuses light along a whole line, which is why lensed galaxies often appear as rings or arcs rather than simple dots.
