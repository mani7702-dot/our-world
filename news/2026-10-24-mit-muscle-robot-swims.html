5. How MIT built a paper-thin robot that swims using living muscle cells

By Our World Published 24 October 2026 at 09:17 IST
Reading options

MIT engineers built a robot just as thin as a sheet of paper that swims by flapping two tiny fins — each powered by a layer of living muscle cells thinner than a strand of hair. The cells twitch when zapped with light, meaning no batteries or motors are needed.

5. How MIT built a paper-thin robot that swims using living muscle cells

A robot powered by living cells

Imagine a robot so thin it could slip through the tiniest cracks underwater — not with clunky metal gears or buzzing motors, but with nothing but living muscle cells. That is exactly what a team at MIT has built. The robot is about the size and shape of a stick of gum, and it swims by flapping two paper-thin fins made from a single layer of living muscle.

Muscle cells that respond to light

Each fin is made from a soft gel film. On top of that gel, the scientists placed a layer of living muscle cells — so thin that it is much narrower than a single strand of hair. These cells are special: they have been gently reprogrammed so that when blue light shines on them, they twitch.

When the researchers flash a light on the left fin, those muscles contract and the robot turns. Flash the right fin, and it turns the other way. By changing which fin gets light and how often, the robot can swim forward, turn, and even navigate through a simple underwater maze. No batteries, no wires, no motors — just light and living tissue.

Tiny but mighty

The robot is slow by human standards. At top speed it covers about four times its own body length in a minute. That may sound sluggish, but in the tiny world of micro-robots, that is a real feat — it matches the leisurely pace of a resting cow shark. More importantly, it is the first example of a flat, two-dimensional robot powered by living muscle that can move on its own.

Why build a living robot?

The dream is that robots like this could one day slip into places where regular machines cannot go — fragile coral reefs, narrow underground water pipes, or even inside the human body to deliver medicine. Because the robot is built from living tissue, it is soft, bendy, and can even heal itself. It is gentle enough not to damage delicate environments.

MIT engineer Ritu Raman explains that today's biohybrid robots are usually chunky, 3D blocks of muscle that need millions of cells. Their new flat design needs far fewer cells, is cheaper to build, and moves more efficiently through water.

Try this at home

You can't build a muscle-powered robot at home, but you can see biohybrid principles in action! Try this: place a single drop of water on a piece of wax paper. Add a drop of dish soap shaped like a tiny dot on one side. Watch the water droplet dance and move — the soap's surface tension pulls the water in one direction. That is how a tiny push can create motion. Real robot muscles work on a similar idea, just at a microscopic scale.

Fun Fact

The word "robot" was coined by playwright Karel Čapek in 1920 from the Czech word "robota," meaning forced labor. And the first living robot ever created was a tobacco mosaic virus controlled by a muscle cell — built by scientists in 2011, a decade before MIT's paper-thin swimmer!

for-grownups

The aquabot's skeleton is a gel film patterned with micro-grooves that guide muscle cell growth in a monolayer. The C2C12 skeletal muscle cells are transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2), enabling optogenetic control via 470 nm blue light at 1–5 mW/mm². Each muscle strip is ~1 mm wide and generates peak specific force of ~100 mN/mm². Thrust measurement via particle-image velocimetry (PIV) at 60 fps showed net displacement of ~2 mm over 60 seconds (~0.07 body lengths/min), comparable to the cruising speed of the spiny dogfish shark (Squalus acanthias). The gel fins, composed of a polyethylene glycol (PEG) hydrogel, measure ~12 mm × 6 mm and flex ~0.5 mm per contraction. The full study, "Optically-Controlled Paper-Thin Biohybrid Robots Powered by Skeletal Muscle Monolayers," appears in Advanced Functional Materials (DOI: 10.1002/adfm.78065, October 2026).

For grown-ups

The robot's skeleton is a gel "fin" about the size of a stick of gum. Genetically engineered C2C12 muscle cells — deposited as a monolayer thinner than a human hair — are optically stimulated via channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) to twitch on command. Each contraction produces ~10 microns of displacement, generating enough thrust (~10 μN) for net forward propulsion at ~0.07 body lengths/min. Published in Advanced Functional Materials (DOI: 10.1002/adfm.78065), Raman Lab, MIT.
