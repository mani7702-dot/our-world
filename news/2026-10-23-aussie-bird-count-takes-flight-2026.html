"How a nationwide bird count turned every Australian into a citizen scientist?"

By Our World Published 23 October 2026 at 21:42 IST IST
Reading options

"Australia's annual Aussie Bird Count asks anyone outside for 20 minutes to tally the birds they see — turning everyday walks into data that helps conservationists track how bird populations are faring across the continent."

How a nationwide bird count turned every Australian into a citizen scientist?

1. A count anyone can join

Every October, people across Australia step outside — onto porches, park benches, school playgrounds, and bush trails — and start counting birds. The Aussie Bird Count, run by BirdLife Australia, asks for just 20 minutes of your time. All you need is a smartphone and eyes on the sky.

2. Schools dive in

At Cedars Christian College in Wollongong, maths class transforms during bird-count week. Teacher Karen Jooste has blended birdwatching into her Year 2 curriculum — teaching data collection, storytelling, and conservation while students sit beside the school's tree-lined creek. The grounds are a bird hotspot: tawny frogmouths with chicks, nesting magpies, king parrots, and rainbow lorikeets fill the air with colour and sound.

"We try to make the most of it," Jooste says. "They actually love it."

3. Numbers that matter

In 2025, 64,000 Australians counted more than 5 million birds. As of October 23, 2026, more than 3.7 million birds had already been logged — on track to beat last year's record.

The app shows the 30 most commonly recorded birds within 10 km of you. Spot something unusual? Search by size, shape, and colour — no expertise needed. Whether you are in a city apartment or a remote outback property, your 20 minutes of observation helps scientists build a continent-wide picture.

The most commonly seen birds? The Australian magpie, the rainbow lorikeet, and the noisy miner — three of Australia's most beloved backyard visitors.

Fun Fact

Australia has 898 recorded bird species — and over 45% are found nowhere else on Earth. That means more than two in every five of Australia's birds are unique to the continent, like the laughing kookaburra, the emu, and the colourful sulphur-crested cockatoo.

Try this at home

Backyard bird count: Spend 20 minutes in your garden or local park. Count every bird you see or hear. Draw or list them in a notebook. Try again from a different spot — a street corner, playground, or shopping trip. Compare your lists.

Bird ID challenge: Look up pictures of three common birds near you. Can you tell them apart by size, colour, and shape?

For grown-ups

"The Aussie Bird Count is an annual week-long citizen-science survey run by BirdLife Australia. In 2026, more than 3.7 million birds had already been counted by October 23, putting the survey on track to beat last year's total of over 5 million birds recorded by 64,000 participants. The count runs until midnight on Sunday, and anyone who spends 20 minutes outdoors — whether walking the dog, waiting for a train, or watching a child's sports game — can contribute. Participants use a free mobile app that shows the 30 most commonly recorded birds within 10 km and lets users search by characteristics like size, shape, and colour. The most commonly recorded species include the Australian magpie, rainbow lorikeet, and noisy miner. Australia has 898 native and migrant bird species, with over 45% being endemic. Schools like Cedars Christian College in Wollongong have integrated the count into their curriculum, teaching children data collection, biodiversity, and conservation through real field observation. Teacher Karen Jooste reported seeing tawny frogmouths with chicks, magpies, king parrots, and rainbow lorikeets on school grounds."
