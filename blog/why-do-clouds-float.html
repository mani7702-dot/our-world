Why do clouds float in the sky?

By Our World Published 19 October 2026 4 min read
Reading options

A cloud can hold as much water as a hundred elephants, yet it drifts overhead instead of falling on your head. The secret is droplet size, rising air, and a trick of heat.

A fluffy summer cloud looks like a giant cotton ball floating in the air. It looks soft, weightless, and permanent. But one of those cotton balls can hold more water than a hundred elephants — and none of it crashes down. So how does a cloud stay up?

The answer has three parts, and none of them is magic.

Clouds are made of incredibly tiny droplets

A cloud is not a blob of water. It is a huge cloud of air holding billions and billions of microscopic droplets of liquid water (or tiny ice crystals, when it is cold enough high up).

A typical cloud droplet is about a hundredth of a millimetre across — roughly a tenth the width of a human hair. Far too small to see as an individual drop. Together, though, they scatter sunlight so strongly that the whole patch of sky turns bright white. That is the cloud you see.

Each droplet also needs somewhere to form. Water vapour cannot turn into liquid all by itself; it needs a speck to cling to. So droplets build around a condensation nucleus — a tiny particle of dust, salt from sea spray, smoke, or pollen, floating in the air.

Tiny things fall very slowly

Here is the part that surprises most people. Cloud droplets are falling. Gravity pulls on them just like everything else.

But a droplet that small pushes almost no air out of its way as it sinks. It drifts downward at only a few centimetres per hour — around 18 to 36 metres per hour for a droplet with a radius of just 5 to 10 micrometres. From a cloud thousands of metres up, that crawl is far too slow for your eyes to notice. Dust in a sunbeam behaves the same way: it is falling, but the gentlest current in the room keeps it dancing.

Rising air holds the droplets up

If droplets are sinking, what keeps them from eventually reaching the ground? Rising air.

Clouds are born in updrafts — air moving upward, often a few metres per second. The droplets fall through that rising air at the same time the air carries them up. When the two balance out, the cloud simply hovers. Meteorologists describe droplets as tracers of the air's motion: the cloud is the shape of an invisible current, made visible by the water inside it.

The air itself is doing the rest of the work. Warm, moist air near the ground is less dense than the cooler air around it, so it rises. As it climbs, the air expands and cools — and cool air cannot hold as much water vapour as warm air. The vapour has to go somewhere, so it condenses into droplets.

There is one more neat detail. When vapour condenses into liquid, it releases a little heat. That heat warms the air inside the cloud, making it lighter still, and helping the whole thing keep rising.

So how heavy is a cloud really?

Take a typical puffy cumulus cloud about one kilometre wide, one kilometre deep and one kilometre tall. Scientists estimate that the liquid water alone inside it adds up to roughly 500,000 kilograms — about as much as a hundred elephants, or several large aeroplanes.

It does not fall, because all that water is smeared across a billion cubic metres of air. Only a small fraction of the cloud is water at all; the rest is air, which is doing the heavy lifting.

Rain happens when the balance breaks. Droplets bump into each other and merge, growing bigger and heavier until they fall faster than the updraft can carry them. When gravity finally wins that race, you get wet.

So the honest answer to "why do clouds float?" is that they don't — they're sinking, just far too slowly to see, inside air that's rising fast enough to win the tie.
