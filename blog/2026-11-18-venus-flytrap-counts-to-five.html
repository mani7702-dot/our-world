How does a Venus flytrap count to five?

By Our World Published 18 November 2026 at 17:10 IST 3 min read
Reading options

A Venus flytrap only snaps shut when it feels two quick taps - and it starts digesting only after about five. Scientists have found the plant's secret: a tiny clock made of calcium.

A Venus flytrap is a plant that eats animals. But it is also picky: it will not snap shut for just anything that touches it.

Its trap is a pair of leaves with about three tiny trigger hairs on each half. A raindrop or a fallen twig might move a hair, and the trap stays open. But if a hair is touched twice within about 30 seconds, the trap snaps shut in a fraction of a second.

A message with no nerves

Plants have no brain and no nerves, yet the flytrap sends a message. Each touch sets off an electrical pulse across the trap, and the pulse briefly raises the level of calcium inside the plant's cells.

One pulse is not enough. The calcium rises, then fades. If a second pulse arrives before the calcium has faded, the two add up and cross a threshold - and the trap closes.

Scientists call this a calcium clock: a kind of short-term memory made of chemistry.

Five taps to start digesting

Closing the trap is only step one. Touches keep coming from the struggling insect, and each one adds to the count. From about the fifth touch, the plant begins making digestive juices - the mix that breaks the insect down into food.

That is why the plant counts. A single bump is probably a raindrop or a leaf. Several quick taps in a row almost always mean a live meal.

Why this is a big deal

The flytrap shows that counting and memory do not need a brain. A plant can store information about the recent past in the chemistry of its own cells - and use it to make a decision.

Try this at home

If you meet a Venus flytrap, do not poke it for fun: every snap costs the plant energy, and a trap that closes for nothing can take days to reopen.

Instead, watch closely. Can you spot the tiny trigger hairs on each half of a trap? They are the plant's fingertips.

Takeaway: A Venus flytrap counts to two to catch its dinner and to five to start digesting. No brain, no nerves - just a clock made of calcium.
