Why do tropical trees breathe at night?

By Our World Published 22 October 2026 at 00:23 IST 4 min read
Reading options

Tropical trees in dry places close their pores by day and take in carbon dioxide by night — a clever trick called CAM photosynthesis that saves water.

In the hot, dry places where tropical trees grow, every drop of water counts. So some of these trees have learned to flip the usual daily schedule upside down — they take in carbon dioxide at night, store it, and use it during the day while keeping their pores shut.

It is a trick called CAM photosynthesis, and it lets a tree live where most plants would quickly dry out.

A 200-year-old mystery

Around the year 1800, the naturalist Alexander von Humboldt was studying a tropical tree and noticed something odd. He put a leaf into water and watched it in the sunlight — but, unlike other plants, it did not bubble. Most plants release tiny oxygen bubbles when they photosynthesize in light. This one did nothing.

The reason: its pores, called stomata, were closed.

The night-shift photosynthesizers

Every leaf on every green plant has stomata — microscopic doors that let carbon dioxide in and let water out. Most plants open them during the sunny day, when they need CO2 to feed the sugars that power growth.

But open those doors when it is hot and dry, and the leaf loses water fast. The trees that live in seasonally dry tropical forests solved this by switching their schedule:

  • At night — when the air is cool and humid — they open their stomata, pull in carbon dioxide, and store it as a molecule called malic acid inside tiny storage bubbles.
  • During the day — when the sun is strong and the air is dry — they keep their stomata shut, pull the stored carbon dioxide back out, and run their photosynthesis without losing a drop of water.

The only known trees that do this belong to a group called Clusia, found mostly in the Caribbean and northern South America. They are the only trees on Earth that use full CAM, and different species have settled on slightly different versions of the trick.

Three trees, three strategies

Scientists studying three Clusia species found that the night-shift trick evolved in more than one way. One species uses strong CAM — it stores lots of carbon dioxide every single night, no matter what. Another only turns CAM on when water gets hard to find, like a backup plan. The third uses a hybrid strategy, mixing both approaches.

Why the difference? These trees used to be the same species, but long ago their entire genomes got copied — every gene duplicated. Over millions of years, those extra copies were lost, changed, or took on new jobs. The ones that ended up controlling the night-time CO2 pump were rewired differently in each lineage, giving each tree its own flavor of the water-saving trick.

How plants teach us about surviving dry

CAM is not just a clever biology story — it is a survival plan that lets a tree live where water runs out. As dry seasons get longer in many parts of the world, plants with this trick are the ones that keep growing while others go dormant.

Scientists think understanding how CAM evolved in Clusia could one day help crops save water too. After all, every plant needs CO2 to make food — but not every plant can afford to leave its doors wide open all day.

Try this at home: soak two similar leaves (like from a houseplant or lettuce) in water in clear cups. Put one in bright sunlight and one in a dark cupboard. The one in sunlight should bubble — that is oxygen from photosynthesis. The one in the dark should barely bubble. That is the gas exchange that CAM trees learn to hold in until night.

Fun fact: the word "CAM" stands for Crassulacean Acid Metabolism. It was first discovered not in a tree, but in the jade plant — a succulent in the Crassula family, which is why the acronym starts with that name.

For grown-ups

The short version for curious grown-ups: the technique is CAM photosynthesis (Crassulacean Acid Metabolism). At night the plant opens its stomata, absorbs CO2, and stores it as malic acid in vacuoles; during the day it keeps stomata shut to conserve water and releases that CO2 for the Calvin cycle. It evolved more than once in the tropical genus Clusia via ancient genome duplication (polyploidy) followed by millions of years of gene reshuffling, producing three strategies — strong CAM, stress-triggered CAM, and a hybrid form.
