Who gets to name a brand-new place on the map?

By Our World Published 18 November 2026 at "11:51" IST 4 min read
Reading options

"A stretch of water that was solid ice not long ago now has a name on the map of Antarctica. Here is who decides what new places are called, and why so many of them are named after scientists."

Maps look finished, as if every bay and mountain already had its name. But Earth is still making new places - and every time one appears, somebody has to decide what to call it.

A stretch of water that was not there before

Far away in the west of Antarctica, there is a channel of water between two islands: Rothschild Island and Alexander Island. Not long ago, that channel was not open water at all. A chunk of ice joined the two islands together. Then the ice broke apart and drifted away, and where solid ice had been, there was suddenly a way through. Alexander Island is the biggest island in Antarctica - about 49,070 square kilometres of ice, rock and mountains, with peaks almost 3 kilometres high. Almost nobody has ever stood on it.

So somebody had to name it

The new channel is now called the Amos Channel. It is named after Jonathan Amos, a science reporter who spent 38 years at BBC News. He joined the BBC in 1986 as a local radio reporter in Cambridge, helped launch the BBC News website in 1997, ran its online science desk for years, and later reported science across television, radio and the web. He left the BBC in 2024. The naming committee said he was honoured for his "exceptional and influential role in bringing Antarctic research to the forefront of public understanding".

Here is the funny part: Jonathan Amos has never actually been to Antarctica. "I've written about it so often, but always from my desk," he said. "This is quite the incentive to go and visit."

Who decides? A committee with strict rules

New Antarctic names are not handed out to whoever spots a place first. In the British Antarctic Territory they are recommended by the UK Antarctic Place-names Committee, which also looks after names for South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands. Naming follows strict rules, and every suggested name has to be checked and agreed at a meeting. The committee does not only honour journalists. When it met earlier this year it added 20 new names, including:

  • Four places near Signy Island, named after British Antarctic Survey staff who kept long-running counts of seabirds and seals - Dunn Cove, Adlard Point, Fox Point and Briggs Point.
  • Cape Abrahamsen, near the Larsen Ice Shelf, named after Dr Einar Povl Abrahamsen, who studied the Southern Ocean.
  • A new set of names around Adelaide Island for scientists whose work changed the world, including Curie Cove, Franklin Peak and Anning Island.

Every one of those names has a reason behind it. That is the rule: a name is not just a label, it is a small story about why a place matters.

Try this at home: name your own island

  1. Draw an island on a sheet of paper. Give it a coast, a mountain, a bay and a channel.
  2. Now name each feature - but you may only use a name if you can write one sentence saying why it belongs to that spot.
  3. Hand your map to someone else and read out your reasons. Can they guess which name goes where?

Real map-makers work exactly this way. Next time you look at a map of somewhere far away, remember that every single name on it was chosen by a person, for a reason.
