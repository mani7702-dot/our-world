How do volcanoes work?

By Our World Published 19 October 2026 4 min read
Reading options

Melted rock, a trapped gas trick, and mountains that build themselves. The pressure cookers of planet Earth, explained.

Deep underground it is hot enough to melt stone. That melted rock, called magma, is lighter than the solid rock around it — so it rises, slowly but surely, like a bubble of oil in water. When it finds a weak spot in Earth's crust and bursts out, you get a volcano. Every volcano is basically a pressure cooker with the lid left ajar.

Here is how it works.

Melting rock on the move

Most magma does not come from Earth's core. It forms from partially melted rock in the upper mantle, and the heat that does the melting comes in large part from the natural radioactivity of elements like uranium, thorium and potassium inside the Earth. Nothing dramatic — just steady warmth, built up over billions of years.

Once a blob of magma forms, it rises because it weighs less than the rock surrounding it. It squeezes upward through cracks, sometimes collecting in a magma chamber — an underground reservoir — before it bursts through a vent. A volcano is simply that vent: a doorway between the melting rock below and the sky above.

Magma or lava? It depends on the address

The two words describe the same stuff: molten rock, crystals and dissolved gases. Before it erupts it is called magma. Once it pours onto the surface, it gets a new name: lava. So lava is just magma that has made it to the surface — like juice is still juice once it leaves the bottle, but now it is a spill.

The soda bottle trick

Volcanoes are explosive largely because of gas. Underground, enormous pressure from the rock above keeps gases dissolved in the magma, the way the fizz in a bottle of soda stays dissolved while the cap is on. As magma nears the surface, that pressure drops and the gas bursts out into bubbles — scientists call this vesiculation.

You have done the same experiment at home. Shake a soda, twist the cap, and the escaping gas blows foam everywhere. A volcano is that soda bottle, scaled up enormously. The faster the magma rises and the more gas it carries, the more violent the eruption. Basaltic eruptions at places like Kīlauea in Hawaii can be relatively calm oozes, while gas-rich, sticky magmas can shatter into ash clouds that shoot tens of thousands of feet into the sky. The 1989 eruption of Redoubt Volcano in Alaska, for example, sent an ash column about 62,000 feet (19 km) high.

Sticky lava versus runny lava

What a lava does next depends on how thick it is — its viscosity — and that comes mostly down to silica content. Silica molecules in the melt tangle together like strands of spaghetti: more silica, more tangles, more stickiness.

  • Runny, low-silica lava spreads easily. Flow fronts at Kīlauea have travelled up to about 6 miles (10 km) per hour. This runny lava builds broad, gently sloped shield volcanoes like Mauna Loa, one of the largest active volcanoes on Earth.
  • Sticky, high-silica lava barely flows at all. It piles up over the vent into steep stratovolcanoes — the classic pointy cone shape of Mount Fuji — and traps gas until it explodes.

A world full of them

Scientists count roughly 1,350 potentially active volcanoes on land, not counting the countless ones hidden under the oceans, and about 500 of those have erupted in the past century. Many sit around the Pacific Ocean in the Ring of Fire, where sliding tectonic plates dive under one another, releasing water that melts the rock above. Others, like Hawaii's, sit over hotspots: fixed plumes of hot material that burn a new volcano through a passing tectonic plate.

Volcanoes can be terrifying, but they are also builders. Islands like Hawaii exist because of them, and their weathered ash makes some of the most fertile farmland on Earth. Lava has built whole landscapes — and, in a way, a whole planet's crust.
