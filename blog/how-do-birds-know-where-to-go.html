How do birds know where to go?

By Our World Published 19 October 2026 at 19:27 IST 4 min read
Reading options

A young bird with no map, no phone and no grown-up to ask still flies thousands of kilometres to the right place. Sun, stars, Earth's magnetic field and smell all take a turn.

Every year, billions of birds set off on journeys that can cross whole oceans and continents. Many fly at night, guided by the moon and stars, and arrive at a specific patch of forest or shoreline they may have never seen before.

Somehow they know where to go. Here is what scientists have figured out so far — and the honest answer is that they still don't know all of it.

Two different skills: compass and map

Scientists split bird navigation into two jobs.

Orientation is knowing which way is north. That is the compass part. Navigation is knowing where you actually are compared to where you want to be — a kind of mental map of the destination.

A bird can have a perfect compass and still get lost without the map. Young birds seem to be born with both pieces partly built in: an inherited sense of which direction to fly, plus a mental map they fill in with experience as they grow.

Following the sun

By day, many birds use a sun compass. But it is not as simple as "sun rises in the east." The sun moves across the sky all day, so a bird has to combine what it sees with what time it is. It carries an internal clock, which makes the whole system work like a living sundial.

That has been tested in a slightly sneaky way: when researchers messed with birds' day-night rhythms using artificial light, their navigation went haywire. Take away the clock and the sun compass stops making sense.

Reading the stars

Most songbirds migrate at night, when the air is calmer and predators are fewer. Up there, the sun is no use, so they switch to a star compass.

Before their very first migration, young birds sit outside and watch the night sky. The stars appear to wheel around one fixed point — the North Star, Polaris. By learning that pattern, they work out which way is north. They do not memorise individual constellations so much as the overall rotation of the sky.

A magnetic sense you can almost see

The strangest tool is the one we cannot see at all. Birds can sense Earth's magnetic field — the field generated by the churning molten metal in our planet's core. It works even on a completely cloudy, starless night, which makes it a reliable backup.

Exactly how is still being argued over, but the leading idea is extraordinary. A molecule called cryptochrome, found in the retinas of birds' eyes, may host a light-dependent chemical reaction that is subtly influenced by magnetic fields. In effect, birds may be able to see the magnetic field as a pattern overlaying their normal vision. Researchers also report magnetite-based receptors near the upper beak that might sense the field's strength, and birds can read polarised light in the sky to locate the sun even when it is hidden behind cloud.

Landmarks, smells, and friends

None of this replaces plain old looking around. Birds use rivers, coastlines, mountain ranges, and even the smell of places to guide them, especially on return trips they have made before.

Young birds also learn routes from experienced ones. Some species simply follow the flock. In one famous programme, hand-reared whooping cranes were taught their migration route by following ultra-light aircraft — proof that a route can be learned, not just inherited.

The real answer: all of the above

Birds do not pick one tool. They carry several and switch between them depending on conditions. If the sky is overcast and the stars are hidden, they fall back on magnetism, landmarks, or smell. It is a redundant system built for a world that keeps changing.

So a small bird weighing less than a biscuit crosses a hemisphere using starlight, an internal clock, a built-in compass, a memory of coastlines — and a great deal of stubbornness.
