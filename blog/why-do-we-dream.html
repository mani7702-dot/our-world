Why do we dream?

By Our World Published 19 October 2026 at 19:35 IST 4 min read
Reading options

Wild stories, flying elephants, and a brain that is almost as busy as when you're awake. Scientists are still unpacking the mystery.

Every night, your brain builds a movie you never asked for. You might fly over your school, chat with a dinosaur, or show up to class in your pajamas. Dreams can be funny, scary, or just plain strange — and honestly, nobody knows the full reason we have them. But scientists have learned a lot about what happens inside a dreaming brain.

Here is what they know.

The dream stage: REM sleep

Sleep is not one flat thing. During the night you cycle through different stages, and the most vivid dreams happen during REM sleep — rapid eye movement sleep. If you have ever watched a sleeping cat twitch its paws or a baby's eyes flicker under closed lids, you have probably seen REM in action.

During REM, your brain is not resting. Sleep studies show your brainwaves are almost as busy as they are when you are awake. Your mind is running its own private show while your body stays put.

Who presses "play"?

Researchers have learned something odd by studying people with brain injuries. The brainstem, down at the base of the brain, seems to switch REM sleep on and off. The forebrain, the big thinking part up front, generates the dreams themselves.

People with an injured brainstem can experience dreams even without going into REM sleep. People with an injured forebrain fall into REM sleep perfectly well — but report no dreams at all. Two different machines, two different jobs: one turns on the stage, the other writes the play.

The daytime on repeat

Take a moment to remember your strangest dream. Chances are it was built from stuff you have already seen. Researchers find that dreams are mostly stitched together from experiences, places, people, thoughts and feelings you have met in real life. Your brain is not inventing a whole new world from nothing — it is remixing the one you live in.

So what is the remix for? One big idea is memory. Your brain is constantly recording your day, but memories only stick if they get replayed and filed away. Dreaming seems to be tied to that filing process. Some scientists think dreams also work as a rehearsal — a safe way to practice facing challenges, like giving a speech or taking a test, before doing it for real.

That is why a night of good sleep after practicing something can genuinely help you do it better the next day. Your brain keeps working after you close your eyes.

Why dreams feel so weird

Part of the answer is brain chemistry. During REM, the brain turns some chemical messengers up and others down. The chemicals that normally keep you focused on the real world get quietened, which is why a passing car outside can turn into a waterfall in your dream without waking you up. Other chemicals linked to vivid, surreal thinking become more active — and that may be exactly why dreams have that made-up, silly, drift-off-a-cliff quality.

And the honest answer

Science has plenty of ideas and very few final answers. There is the memory-filing theory, the rehearsal theory, the creativity theory — and there may be a bit of truth in all of them. One thing experts agree on is that your dreams are not secret codes. In the 1950s Sigmund Freud made dream-interpreting famous, but researchers have never been able to show that dream symbols have fixed meanings. Figuring out what a dream "means" stays up to you.

What is certain is smaller but still amazing: every night, a brain of roughly 80 billion neurons builds a world out of your own memories, shuts the door to the outside, and lets you watch.
